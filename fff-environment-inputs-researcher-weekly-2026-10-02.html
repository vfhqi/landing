Report 1
G10 and European macroeconomic inputs (source #1)
Executive summary
  • The read: United States activity is strong, core inflation printed softer than feared, and the sell side cut Federal Reserve (Fed) hikes this week
  • Strongest view: Goldman Sachs US economics (30-Sep, 1-Oct) dropped its October hike after core personal consumption expenditures (PCE) inflation printed 3.01% year on year and moved the second hike to December
  • Sharpest disagreement: James Ferguson (MacroStrategy, 28-Sep) sees no inflation, only an energy terms-of-trade shock; Vice Chair Jefferson (1-Oct, via Goldman Sachs) still fears energy and AI costs leaking into core
  • Change on the week: the market's priced peak Fed rate rose from 4.00% three weeks ago to 4.80% by 25-Sep (R. Marshall, Pacific Asset Management), before Goldman Sachs reversed course by 1-Oct
  • Watch: September payrolls (Goldman Sachs +80k), Brazil's first round on 4-Oct, Iran talks before the 2-Nov midterms

Inflation

Goldman Sachs US economics (30-Sep) reads August core PCE as the turn: +0.25% on the month, 3.01% on the year, below expectations after methodological revisions. It now expects 3.0% core PCE for 2026 against the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) median of 3.4%, and calls an October hike unlikely.

Vice Chair Jefferson (1-Oct) backed September's hike, still "concerned about the risk of higher energy prices leading to a persistent rise in inflation" and about AI costs driving "unusually strong increases".

James Ferguson (MacroStrategy, 28-Sep) dissents: only energy and airfares, 8% of the basket, run above the 3.4% headline, and core CPI is 2.4%. He calls it a terms-of-trade shock needing looser policy, and the only crisis "one of the Fed's own making".

Why it matters: whether the undershoot or the spillover is right sets the discount rate on European equities for the next year.

StatusUS core PCE 3.01%, Conference Board inflation expectations 6.1%
TrendCore decelerating on Goldman Sachs' numbers, energy pass-through still live
Change in trendYes, the Fed path was marked down this week
SkewGoldman Sachs and Ferguson to the downside; Jefferson to the upside from energy and AI costs

Economic health and consumer surveys

Goldman Sachs tracks Q3 US GDP at +3.4% (1-Oct) and reads the ISM at 54.5 as "a healthy pace of economic activity but no sign of a surge". Barclays' Ajay Rajadhyaksha (30-Sep) argues higher rates now weigh less on growth because AI investment, earnings and a resilient consumer carry the economy, hence "Reluctantly Optimistic"; Mark Wilson (Goldman Sachs, 25-Sep) agrees on the level but expects "the 2nd derivative will slow" as tax-cut benefits fade and petrol and mortgage rates bite. In Europe, French and German purchasing managers' indices (PMIs) are expanding and the euro area is firm (Marshall, 25-Sep); UK unemployment should peak at 5.2% in early 2027 (Goldman Sachs).

By country: the United States Conference Board (29-Sep) fell to 81.9, the lowest since 2014. Deep Value Capital (1-Oct) counters that "consumers feel terrible, and they keep spending anyway", with the retail control group up 5.6% year on year.

Why it matters: Europe is being told growth is fine at the level and rolling over at the margin, the mix in which cyclicals disappoint before the data does.

StatusUS growth above trend, Europe expanding, US confidence at a 12-year low
TrendActivity up, labour and confidence softening
Change in trendThe US confidence fall accelerated this month
SkewBarclays up; Goldman Sachs sales and the Conference Board down

Rajadhyaksha names sovereign debt and bond markets as the main fiscal risk; Molavi points to France and a UK budget between fiscal pressure and growth.

Report 2
G10 and European macroeconomic outputs (source #2)
Executive summary
  • The read: the discount rate rose again (US 10-year 5.25%, highest since July 2007) and equities held, with every desk asking how long that lasts
  • Strongest view: Goldman Sachs' Christian Mueller-Glissmann (GOAL, 28-Sep) says "the clearest path to equity upside from here remains rates relief", tactically neutral for three months, overweight equities for twelve
  • Sharpest disagreement: Mark Wilson (Goldman Sachs, 25-Sep) says "now is the wrong time to be worrying about higher yields"; Howard Marks and Jeffrey Gundlach (via Santangel's, 25-Sep) say optimists are in charge at a CAPE of 42 and cracks are showing
  • Change on the week: seven straight months of rising 10-year yields, the longest run in 50 years (Wilson); macro funds "taking turns to stop out" (R. Marshall, Pacific Asset Management, 25-Sep)

Rates and bond yields

The Fed hiked 25 basis points in September to 3.75-4.00% (Deep Value Capital, 1-Oct). By 25-Sep the market priced a 4.80% peak, three more hikes than three weeks earlier (Marshall); by 1-Oct Goldman Sachs expected no October hike, one in December and possibly none. The Bank of England should hike in November but four priced hikes are "less likely to be realised" (Goldman Sachs, 28-Sep); the Bank of Japan has turned more hawkish (GOAL). Yields: US 10-year 5.25%, European bonds underperforming on "energy/fiscal concerns" (GOAL, 28-Sep). Bobby Molavi (Goldman Sachs, 25-Sep) notes a flatter curve, a stubborn front end forcing unwinds. Wilson's case for relief: hikes are biting and tariff pass-through is fading, so "central banks likely disappoint on hikes versus what's currently priced". Barclays' Rajadhyaksha (30-Sep) keeps sovereign debt as the risk he will not look through.

Why it matters: the European book is valued off a rate that has risen for seven months; relief is the bull case and both Goldman Sachs desks say so.

StatusUS 10-year 5.25%, peak Fed rate priced 4.80%
TrendYields up seven months running
Change in trendThe Fed path was marked down this week, yields not yet
SkewGoldman Sachs to lower yields; Barclays and Molavi to fiscal upside risk

Credit spreads and commodities

Goldman Sachs' EM team (30-Sep) says spreads widened "across the board" in sympathy with US corporate spreads, but the cycle caps the widening and they stay rangebound for twelve months. GOAL is underweight credit. Molavi says Oracle's force majeure "rekindled AI credit questions"; Deep Value Capital notes hyperscaler credit default swaps up about 70 basis points since 2025. Oil: Brent stayed above $100 after the ceasefire rejection (GOAL); WTI fell 3.6% to $92.66 on deal hopes (Bison Insights, 26-Sep). Gold: Goldman Sachs cut its 2026 target to $4,650 an ounce because real rates outweigh central bank demand (GOAL). Molavi asks whether oil at $80 is the hidden tailwind that removes the priced hikes.

Why it matters: credit is the first market where AI supply and higher rates meet; its widening is the crack to watch under European equities.

StatusSpreads wider from tight, Brent around $100
TrendSpreads widening with rate volatility
Change in trendFirst weekly spread move outside this year's ranges (Goldman Sachs EM)
SkewGoldman Sachs sees a ceiling; Molavi and Wilson see oil downside

Risk appetite and the diagnostic questions

Are equities ignoring the discount rate? Wilson finds it "surprising equities managed to hold a week of gains given the scale of the move in yields" and credits growth and cleaner positioning; Molavi calls it "sprinting to stand still". Cracks underneath: GOAL says "market breadth remains poor", with large caps, the Nasdaq and momentum outperforming while rate-sensitives (homebuilders, real estate, utilities, Russell 2000) sit in the largest drawdowns; Deep Value Capital counts 40% of S&P 500 stocks above their 200-day average, down from 66%, and cites the BofA survey naming a "disorderly rise in bond yields" the top tail risk. Defensives are not outperforming either: staples lag and managers are the most underweight staples since 2004 (Deep Value Capital). Is this 2007 into 2008? No source made that case; Gundlach says a CAPE above 35 has always meant negative ten-year real returns, Dalio says rising rates and "production of stock" prick bubbles, and Wilson's analogy is the mid-1990s. On index risk and reward, GOAL says "rates headwinds have intensified while growth tailwinds have moderated", equity volatility stays low against rates volatility, and Marks says build in "a little defense".

Why it matters: the index is calm while the risk-off spreads under it widen; a European book holding rate-sensitives is already in the drawdown.

SkewDown beneath the index, two-sided at the index

AI capital spending: scale, bill and sceptics

Goldman Sachs economics (AI Adoption Tracker, 1-Oct) sizes the theme: US firm adoption 23.8%, AI hardware investment $555bn above its 2022 level (1.7% of GDP), and $1.1tn of gross-profit upgrades for AI-exposed companies since 2022; the labour effect is "visible but narrow". Jefferies' Sherman Shang (27-Sep) sees TSMC capex at $95-100bn by 2028 and datacentre demand above 30GW, but his checks put the HBM price peak in Q2 or Q3 2027 and the Rubin Ultra despec lowers chip cost from $75k to $45-46k. Wilson relays Ben Snider's "over-earning, but not an earnings bubble": memory and some semis over-earn, core earnings stay strong through 2027. The sceptics: Bobby Molavi (Goldman Sachs, 25-Sep) says Oracle's force majeure made investors "re-underwrite contracted obligations"; Jenny Johnson (via Santangel's, 25-Sep) says capital-light businesses turning capital-intensive have not yet run that cost through the income statement; Michael Burry's "GPU Depreciation & Useful Lives" (1-Oct) and "Capital Cycle IQ ... Big 5 Hyperscalers" (26-Sep) were previews only.

Why it matters: European semis, capital goods and power names ride this spend; the week's news cuts the bill without cutting the demand.

Deep Value Capital (newsletter, 1-Oct) quantifies the gap: semiconductors beat the S&P 500 by about 62% over a year while consumer discretionary lagged by 21%, and "long semiconductors is still the most crowded trade in the world". Consumer and banks

Consumer: Goldman Sachs (29-Sep) reports the Conference Board at 81.9, its lowest since 2014, and expects spending growth to slow in Q4 as fiscal support fades. Deep Value Capital counters that the retail control group accelerated to 5.6% year on year and managers are the most underweight staples since 2004, so the fear is priced. Wilson and Yet Another Value Blog (28-Sep) add the structural threat: Muse-style agents price-shop for the consumer and break habitual brand loyalty, which is "disastrous" for low-end brands and most retailers. Banks: CaixaBank BPI (25-Sep) relays BBVA's management that Spain's customer spread "has already started to improve and should benefit progressively from the higher interest-rate environment", with deposit competition contained.

Why it matters: the European consumer book faces a cyclical squeeze and a new structural one at once, while banks are the industry the rate move helps.

TrendConsumer down, banks up
Change in trendThe Muse threat is new
SkewConsumer two-sided (fear priced against agents); banks up with rates

Semiconductors: memory, foundry and optical

Jefferies (27-Sep) is the only deep source. Foundry: TSMC 2028 capex $95-100bn, CoWoS packaging 180k wafers a month by end-2027 and 220k by end-2028, Nvidia half of all AI wafer demand; AMD's share of TSMC sales rises from 9% to 13% on 14m CPUs booked for 2027. Memory: ODM checks say HBM prices decline "slightly" from a Q2 or Q3 2027 peak because long-term agreements cap upside; the Rubin Ultra despec to 192GB cuts implied chip price 44%. Optical: future racks shift cost from GPU and memory to networking; the co-packaged versus near-packaged optics debate "isn't about costs, it's about yields", with Corning and Largan at 50-60% yields against Nvidia's 70-80% requirement by June 2027. Jefferies' 25-Sep note lists the bear points on memory: CXMT margins, the YMTC IPO, despec and long-term agreements removing pricing surprises.

Why it matters: European semi-equipment and optical suppliers sit on the constrained side; European memory exposure sits on the side the market is starting to doubt.
