## PART 1: THE DIAGNOSIS [~10 minutes]

Here's what's interesting about the knowing-doing gap. It's not a mystery. It's one of the most thoroughly diagnosed conditions in performance psychology. And the diagnosis is the same whether it comes from a psychologist, a poker player, a statistician, or a momentum trader.

Let me show you.

Mark Douglas spent thirty years studying why intelligent people with well-designed systems fail to execute them. His conclusion: you intellectually accept probabilities but emotionally reject them under pressure. You can write "no certain outcomes for any one stock, just probabilities — this is freedom" in your affirmations. And you mean it. But when a fifteen percent position is down twenty percent and the fear centres of the brain are firing, the probabilistic framework collapses. The mind doesn't want probabilities anymore. The mind wants certainty. And it will generate rationalisations — "the thesis hasn't changed," "the market doesn't understand," "sell-side is wrong" — to create the illusion of certainty where none exists.

Douglas calls this the gap between intellectual agreement and emotional embodiment. Stage one is understanding the concept. Stage three is living it automatically. Most investors, he observed, are permanently stuck at stage one — fluent in the language of probability, but operating from the belief system of certainty whenever it matters most.

[PAUSE]

Now here's Annie Duke, arriving at exactly the same place from a completely different direction. Duke is a poker player and decision scientist. She doesn't talk about probabilities and beliefs. She talks about kill criteria, sunk costs, and identity.

Her diagnosis: you set exit conditions — your thirty-day shot clock, your ACH threshold, your deterioration triggers — but you override them when your identity is threatened. Because your self-image includes "I trust my judgement one hundred and fifty percent" and "I have outlier critical thinking." Those are powerful, positive identity statements. And they serve you brilliantly most of the time. But when a thesis deteriorates, those same statements become a trap. "I trust my judgement" becomes "I shouldn't sell — my judgement says this company is good." "Outlier critical thinking" becomes "I see something the market doesn't." "Bold action" becomes "selling would be timid."

Duke calls this identity-based persistence. The position isn't just a position anymore. It's a referendum on who you are. And quitting it feels like quitting yourself.

Her resolution is elegant. Identity should be built around process execution, not around position outcomes. "I am someone who sets kill criteria and honours them" is a stronger identity than "I am someone who holds through conviction." Because the first identity includes both holding — when no kill criteria are breached — and quitting — when they are. It's a more complete self-image. Not weaker. More complete.

And she'd point out something else. The fear of being wrong twice — selling and watching it bounce — is the most powerful psychological force keeping investors in deteriorating positions. She calls this the asymmetry of regret. The regret of quitting something that might have worked is vivid, specific, and humiliating. You can picture the stock recovering the day after you sell. But the regret of staying in something that's definitely not working accumulates slowly — a little more each week. A slow burn. And slow burns are easier to tolerate than sharp pains, even when the cumulative damage is far greater.

Pre-commitment strips FOFR of its power. If the kill criterion says exit, and the criterion is met, there's no decision to feel regret about. The criterion was met. The process executed. Just as you wouldn't feel regret about stopping at a red light.

[PAUSE]

Then there's Van Tharp. The mathematician. The system designer. He doesn't talk about emotions or identity. He talks about R-multiples and expectancy.

His diagnosis: your edge leaks through stop slippage, not through bad stock picks. Your analytical engine — the four pillars, the eight triaging steps, the HQI framework — is world-class. He'd call your setup and entry components excellent. But when a planned one-R loss becomes a four-R loss because the stop wasn't honoured, the mathematics are devastating. He ran the numbers on your system. If twenty percent of your losses slip from minus one R to minus four R, your expectancy drops from plus one-point-two-six-five R per trade to plus one-point-zero-zero-four R. That's a twenty percent erosion of your edge — not from picking wrong stocks, but from exiting right stocks at the wrong time.

Put it in pounds: eliminating stop slippage alone, without changing a single stock pick, could raise your CAGR from eight-point-three percent to approximately twelve percent. That's a forty-five percent improvement in returns from position management. Zero improvement in analysis required.

[PAUSE]

And finally, Minervini. The practitioner. Ten thousand stop-outs in thirty years and still a fortune with minimal drawdown. He maps the emotional cycle with forensic precision: Confidence, then Denial, then Frustration, then Hope, then Capitulation. Five stages. And the knowing-doing gap lives in the transition from stage one to stage two. The moment the stock goes against you and conviction says hold but the system says cut.

Minervini's contribution is to name the specific mechanism. It's not abstract. It's not about beliefs or identity in the theoretical sense. It's about being down five percent and telling yourself it's noise. Then being down ten percent and telling yourself the market is wrong. Then being down twenty percent and hoping. Then being down thirty and capitulating.

His system eliminates stages two through four entirely. The stop fires at seven or eight percent. You go from Confidence directly to a clean exit. No Denial. No Frustration. No Hope. Because there's no discretion. The rule executes.

[PAUSE]

Four authors. Four disciplines. One diagnosis.

Douglas says: you accept probabilities intellectually but reject them emotionally.

Duke says: you set kill criteria but override them when identity is threatened.

Van Tharp says: your expectancy leaks through stop slippage, not through bad stock picks.

Minervini says: the emotional cycle from Confidence to Capitulation is the specific mechanism of the leak.

Same gap. Four angles. And every one of them points at the space between knowing and doing.

[PAUSE]

Now here's the thing that makes this diagnosis so powerful — and so uncomfortable. It's not a diagnosis of ignorance. You're not being told you don't know something. Every one of these authors would say: you already know. That's the point. The diagnosis is not "learn more." The diagnosis is "the knowing itself is insufficient." And for someone who has built their identity around analytical excellence — around knowing things deeply, understanding things precisely, seeing things that others miss — that's a confronting message.

Because it means the solution isn't more analysis. It isn't more research. It isn't another memo or another framework. It's something much harder than any of those things. It's changing the relationship between what you know and what you do. Between the conscious mind that understands probabilities and the emotional system that rejects them under fire.

Your own principle captures it: "Focus purely on making money — being analytically right ex ante is irrelevant." That's Mark Douglas, channelled through your own voice. The market does not reward analytical brilliance. It rewards capital allocation that is in the right place at the right time, managed by a person who acts on their system without flinching. Sometimes your analysis gets you there. Sometimes it doesn't. The analysis is a tool. Not a verdict. Not an identity. A tool.

## PART 4: THE FOUR LAYERS [~10 minutes]

So how do you close the gap? Not with willpower. Willpower is a depleting resource and the gap opens precisely when willpower is least available.

You close it with architecture. Four layers. Each one reinforcing the others. Each one drawn from a different author but aimed at the same target.

[PAUSE]

**Layer One: Philosophy. This is Douglas.**

Douglas gives you the foundational insight: you do not need to know what is going to happen next in order to make money. Every edge has a random distribution of wins and losses. Consistency comes from executing the edge identically every time, regardless of the outcome of the previous trade.

Why does this matter for the gap? Because the gap opens when you believe a specific outcome is necessary. "This stock needs to recover." "I need to be right about this thesis." "I can't afford another loss." Each of those beliefs creates fear. And fear narrows perception, degrades decision quality, and produces exactly the outcome you're trying to avoid.

If you genuinely accept — emotionally, not just intellectually — that any individual position might fail, and that failure is the expected cost of operating a probabilistic system, then there is nothing to fear. No outcome threatens you. No loss invalidates you. You follow the process. The outcome is what it is.

This is what "Mark Douglas concept — no certain outcomes for any one stock, just probabilities. This is freedom" means when it's fully installed. Not as an affirmation you read in the morning. As a belief that operates automatically when a position is down twenty percent and the fear centres are screaming.

Douglas mapped the developmental path explicitly. First, the mechanical stage — follow rigid rules without deviation for a sustained period. The purpose isn't to make money. It's to build the neural pathways of rule-following under pressure. Second, the subjective stage — once the mechanical habit is established, reintroduce discretionary elements. But the mechanical foundation stays non-negotiable. Third, the intuitive stage — where deep pattern recognition and trained intuition guide decisions within the framework of the mechanical base.

Your natural inclination is toward stage three. Intuition. Pattern recognition. "Fingertip feel." Creative insight. This is a strength. But Douglas would say you've skipped stage one. You're trying to operate intuitively without the mechanical foundation. The result: brilliant analysis that sometimes produces brilliant outcomes — telecoms, Greggs — and sometimes produces avoidable losses — BFF, XVIVO — because the intuition lacks the mechanical guardrails that prevent it from being hijacked by emotion.

The mechanical stage isn't beneath you. It's the foundation you haven't built yet. And building it is the single most important developmental step between where you are and where you want to be.

[PAUSE]

**Layer Two: Identity. This is Bassham.**

Bassham's core principle: performance equals self-image. You cannot consistently outperform the image you hold of yourself. If your self-image includes "I sometimes hold losers too long," you will continue to hold losers too long — because that's who you believe you are.

The target identity is specific: "I am a disciplined position manager who protects capital. When my system signals an exit, I execute it. That's like me."

Not "I'm always right." Not "I never lose money." The identity includes losses. The identity includes being wrong. But it also includes — crucially — the response to being wrong. "And when my judgment is wrong, I recognise it instantly and move on. That's part of what makes me great."

Every small loss you cut quickly is evidence that supports this identity. Every stop honoured, every shot clock that defaults to exit, every kill criterion met — that's a repetition that builds the neural pathway. Bassham's mental rehearsal technique applies directly: before the market opens, thirty seconds visualising a position hitting your stop. You see it. You sell it. You feel clean. You move on. "That's like me."

And every override — "just this once, the thesis is special" — reinforces the old identity. The one that holds. The one that hopes. The one that watches the gap widen.

[PAUSE]

**Layer Three: Cognition. This is Duke.**

Duke gives you the pre-commitment architecture. Kill criteria defined at the point of investment — not at the point of pain. States and dates. Observable, measurable, non-negotiable.

Why pre-commitment? Because at the point of investment, you're rational, clearheaded, calm. You can define exactly what would invalidate the thesis. Six months later, when the stock is down twenty-five percent and your ego is attached, you cannot think clearly about exit conditions. The sunk cost is singing. The endowment effect is singing. FOFR is singing. You are Ulysses hearing the Sirens. The kill criteria are the ropes that keep you tied to the mast.

Duke's research shows that pre-committed exit conditions produce exits forty to sixty percent earlier than real-time decisions. Not because people quit too early — because real-time decisions are systematically delayed by the bias triad.

And then there's monkeys and pedestals. Attack the fulcrum driver first. Before the fourteen-page case file, before the detailed competitive analysis — can you validate or invalidate the hardest thing in thirty minutes? If the monkey is untrainable, stop building pedestals. Every hour of pedestal-building adds sunk cost that makes quitting harder.

Duke also gives you the decision hygiene to judge your own performance accurately. Stop resulting. A thirty-four percent gain on telecoms doesn't mean the decision was good. A thirty percent loss on BFF doesn't mean the decision was bad. The decision quality depends on the process at the time of the decision, not on the outcome. Evaluate every decision on process alone.

And here's the practical tool: explicit probabilities. Not "high conviction" or "medium conviction." A number. Seventy-two percent. Sixty-five percent. Eighty-eight percent. The power of the number is threefold. First, it forces honesty — saying seventy-two percent is a very different cognitive act from saying "I'm pretty confident." The number makes you confront what you don't know. Second, it enables calibration — track your probability assignments against actual outcomes over twenty positions and you learn whether you're systematically overconfident or underconfident. Third, it makes updating natural. When new information arrives, you don't face the binary question "has my thesis changed?" — which triggers endowment bias. Instead you face: "Does this shift my probability from seventy-two to sixty-five? To fifty? To forty?" Probability updating is continuous and incremental. It's psychologically easier than the binary hold-or-sell decision.

Define a probability floor — say, fifty-five percent — below which the shot clock starts automatically. That's a kill criterion expressed as a probability. And it integrates seamlessly with your IAJA framework: Information, new data arrives. Analysis, probability update. Judgement, above or below floor? Action, hold or start shot clock.

[PAUSE]

**Layer Four: Mechanics. This is Minervini — with Van Tharp providing the mathematical foundation.**

Minervini gives you the engineering. The Trend Template — eight mechanical criteria, no judgment required. Progressive exposure — three percent test, add on confirmation, full size only on validation. The seven-to-eight percent maximum loss. The sell half at double the risk.

These aren't just rules. They're the mechanical enforcement layer that protects you when layers one through three fail under pressure. And based on your documented experience — they sometimes do. That's not a judgment. That's a fact. The mechanical layer exists specifically for the moments when philosophy, identity, and cognition aren't enough.

Van Tharp adds the mathematical language that makes the mechanics viscerally real. R-multiples strip the emotional narrative from every outcome. A minus one R loss on BFF is structurally identical to a minus one R loss on any other stock. The stock's identity, the hours of analysis, the emotional investment — none of it changes the mathematical reality of minus one R. When you start expressing every outcome in R-multiples, the gap narrows because the language itself enforces probabilistic thinking.

And expectancy is the number that tells you whether the system works. Your system's expectancy — if you execute it — is positive. Strongly positive. The gap between your actual CAGR and your potential CAGR is almost entirely explained by the distance between planned R-multiples and actual R-multiples on your losing positions. Fix the exits. The maths does the rest.

Van Tharp would also challenge one of your beliefs directly. "My sizing framework is right. Trust my judgement." He'd say: dangerous as stated. Because "trust my judgement" on sizing is precisely what leads to oversizing early in a thesis, holding too large through deterioration, and inconsistency between positions. The reformulation: "My sizing framework is right when it's calculated, not when it's felt. Trust the system's mathematics. Execute the CPR formula. Then trust the result."

And the CPR formula itself is disarmingly simple. C — capital at risk, the amount you're willing to lose. R — risk per unit, the distance from entry to stop. P — position size, which equals C divided by R. Every position sizing decision is fundamentally an answer to: given how much I'm willing to lose and how far my exit is from my entry, how many shares can I buy? If you start from this formula, emotional sizing becomes impossible. The maths decides. Not the excitement of the thesis. Not the conviction in your gut. The maths.

[PAUSE]

Four layers. Philosophy, identity, cognition, mechanics. Each one necessary. None sufficient alone.

Without Douglas, you take losses personally and they erode your process. Without Bassham, you don't build the identity that sustains the discipline long-term. Without Duke, you have the rules but not the psychological tools to honour them when biases activate. Without Minervini and Van Tharp, you have the understanding but not the specific mechanical triggers and mathematical framework.

Together, they form a complete architecture for closing the gap.

## PART 5: THE ENERGY DIMENSION [~5 minutes]

There's a fifth dimension that none of the four authors fully addresses but that Jim Loehr and Steve Ward both emphasise. And it's critical for you specifically.

The gap widens when you're depleted.

Your journal captures this clearly. "No morning routine done — for a long time now." "Ditto meditation." The ostriching pattern — "Avoided looking at prices. Still ostriching. Should be checking at ten hundred hours." Morning routine slippage correlates directly with the periods where the gap is widest.

Loehr's framework — The Power of Full Engagement — argues that energy management, not time management, determines performance quality. Decision quality degrades when energy is low. The prefrontal cortex, where rule-following and impulse control live, is the first system to degrade under fatigue. When you're depleted, the older brain systems — fear, avoidance, status quo bias — take over. That's when stops get overridden. That's when the shot clock gets extended. That's when ostriching happens.

Ward maps the same territory through the four Cs. When energy drops, Commitment weakens — "I'll check the portfolio tomorrow." Concentration fractures — the losing position consumes disproportionate bandwidth. Confidence erodes — "the fund is really underperforming." Control slips — reactive, not proactive.

[PAUSE]

Here's the connection to the mechanical system. The mechanical system is not just a risk management tool. It's an energy management tool.

Think about what a deteriorating position does to your energy. It creates internal tension — should I sell? It obscures priorities — the losing position demands disproportionate mental bandwidth. It invites over-analysis — researching deeper to justify holding. It generates avoidance — ostriching because looking is painful.

Cutting the loss at seven percent eliminates all of that. Frees the bandwidth. Clears the tension. Removes the drag. One decisive action creates space for twenty better decisions.

And the morning RS check — ten minutes every morning — is both a monitoring ritual and an energy ritual. It forces presence. It forces eyes-on-data. It breaks the ostriching pattern before it starts. When you can't face looking, that itself is diagnostic information. The resistance to checking is the signal that the gap is opening.

Ward maps this through his three behaviour change drivers: social support, reframing, and repetition. The morning RS check is all three. It's social support — Watson monitors alongside you, flags deterioration, asks the uncomfortable questions. It's reframing — you're not "checking on your losses," you're "executing your monitoring process." And it's repetition — every morning, without variation, building the habit that makes the behaviour automatic.

Your own recovery strategies point at the same thing. Sleep. Exercise. Meditation. Journaling. Change of environment. Napoleon Hill audio. These aren't luxuries. They're inputs to the system that determines whether the knowing-doing gap is narrow or wide on any given day. The gap has a physiological dimension. Treat it accordingly.

The energy dimension explains why the gap is intermittent. On good energy days — clear, focused, well-rested, morning routine done — you execute like telecoms and Greggs. Decisive, trusting, in the zone. On depleted days — routine slipped, sleep poor, administrative overload — the gap opens and BFF happens. The system doesn't change. You change. And the mechanical layer exists to protect the system from the depleted version of you.

## PART 6: THE IMPLEMENTATION [~5 minutes]

I'm not going to give you another list of eleven rules. The first podcast did that. And you've read all four memos. You have more frameworks, more rules, more protocols than any single investor needs.

Instead, I'm going to tell you the single most important thing to do differently. Starting Monday.

[PAUSE]

Progressive exposure on the next new position.

Not because it's the most important rule in the system — though it might be. But because it's the one rule that, if fully executed, changes the experience of every subsequent rule.

Here's what I mean. The reason stops get overridden is that losses are painful. A ten percent position declining fifteen percent is a one-point-five percent portfolio hit. That hurts. And pain triggers all the biases — sunk cost, endowment, FOFR, identity. The gap opens because the loss is big enough to engage the emotional machinery.

Progressive exposure makes the first loss tiny. A three percent test position declining seven percent is a zero-point-two percent portfolio hit. That's a rounding error. That's genuinely the cost of doing business. You barely notice it. You cut it. You move on. And — this is the crucial part — cutting that small loss builds the neural pathway. It provides evidence for the new self-image. It's a repetition of the behaviour you want to make automatic.

Each small loss cut cleanly makes the next cut easier. And eventually, when a confirmed position at ten or twelve percent hits its stop, the habit is there. The pathway exists. The identity says "that's like me." The cut happens not because you white-knuckled through the fear but because cutting is what you do.

Bassham calls this building the evidence base. Douglas calls this the mechanical stage. Duke calls this progressive exposure applied to the quitting skill itself. Minervini calls this the cost of doing business. Van Tharp calls this a minus one R outcome that preserves expectancy. Five frameworks, one behaviour.

[PAUSE]

So here's the protocol. The very next new position that enters your portfolio — whatever it is, whatever the thesis — enter at three percent. Not six. Not eight. Three. Set the exit condition before you buy. Write it in the case file. Share it with Watson for monitoring. Then wait. If the stock confirms — holds above the buy point for four weeks, makes a higher high, fundamentals on track — add to six or seven percent. If it confirms again — full validation, Stage 2 intact, thesis progressing — go to ten or twelve.

And if it doesn't confirm? If it goes against you immediately? Cut the three percent position at the pre-defined exit. Take the zero-point-two percent portfolio hit. Log it. Celebrate it. "That's like me — I cut losses quickly and cleanly." Then move to the next opportunity.

That one cycle — enter small, cut if wrong, add if right — teaches more about closing the gap than reading all four memos combined. Because it's not knowledge. It's experience. And experience is the only thing that moves you from Douglas's stage one to stage three.

And then — add one question to the Friday weekly review. "Did I honour every exit condition this week? If not, what rationalisation did I use?" That second question is the coaching mirror. Because the rationalisations repeat. They have patterns. And once you see the pattern — ah, there it is, the "thesis hasn't changed" rationalisation, right on schedule — you can recognise it in real time. Not overcome it through willpower. Recognise it through awareness. And recognition is what makes the gap visible in the moment it matters.

Rotella would frame this as the training-trusting distinction. Setting kill criteria, sizing with the CPR formula, defining the exit condition — that's training mindset. Analytical, deliberate, conscious. Honouring the exit in the moment the criterion is breached — that's trusting mindset. Subconscious, committed, fluid. You cannot be in both modes simultaneously. When the kill criterion fires, you're not in analysis mode. You're in execution mode. Trust the criterion. Trust the pre-commitment. The analysis was done at entry. Now the job is to act.

[PAUSE]

## PART 7: CLOSE [~5 minutes]

Let me bring this home.

In October 2024, you wrote a self-portrait. And it's extraordinary.

"I'm a really great investor. Dynamic swagger."

"The Finn Russell of stock pickers. Cheeky. Impish. Confident. Brazen."

But there's something missing from the portrait. And it's the piece that completes it.

The portrait describes what you do when you're right. Probing. Finding weakness. Smashing through. But it doesn't describe what you do when you're wrong.

[PAUSE]

And what you do when you're wrong is the thing that determines everything.

Douglas would say: the great investor isn't the one who's right more often. It's the one who responds to being wrong with identical discipline every time.

Duke would say: the great investor isn't the one who never quits. It's the one who quits the right things at the right time and redeploys capital into the next opportunity without emotional residue.

Van Tharp would say: the great investor isn't the one with the highest win rate. It's the one whose average loser is small enough that the winners can compound.

Minervini would say: the great investor has been stopped out ten thousand times and still made a fortune. Because each stop-out was a choice to stop being wrong.

[PAUSE]

So here's the completed self-portrait.

"I'm a really great investor. Dynamic swagger. I probe, I experiment, I push hard when I see something working."

And — "When I'm wrong, I recognise it instantly and I move on. I don't hope. I don't rationalise. I don't wait for the thesis to prove itself. I cut, I learn, and I redeploy. That's not a weakness. That's the hardest part of what makes me great."

[PAUSE]

That version of the self-portrait doesn't subtract anything from the original. It adds the one dimension that was missing. The dimension where the P&L leaks. The dimension where the gap lives.

The knowing-doing gap is not about learning more. You have more knowledge than you need. It's not about trying harder. Willpower fails at the exact moment it's needed most. It's about building an architecture — philosophy, identity, cognition, mechanics, energy — that makes the right behaviour automatic. So that the decisive, dynamic, "smashing a carrier fleet through the gap" version of you is the same person who cuts a three percent test position at minus seven percent without a second thought.

Because they're the same person. They always were. The gap just made them look different.

Your investing philosophy document has a line that lands differently now. "Discretion is the better part of valour — act ex ante on probabilistic deterioration." That's the commander speaking. The probing, decisive, carrier-fleet-through-the-gap commander. And discretion — the judgement to act early, to cut before the damage compounds, to redeploy before the opportunity cost accumulates — that's not the opposite of boldness. It's the highest expression of it.

Ed Seykota, who Van Tharp considered the greatest trader he ever studied, put it simply: "There are old traders and there are bold traders. But there are very few old, bold traders." Your concentrated, high-conviction approach is bold. The knowing-doing gap is what makes it dangerous. And closing the gap — making the mechanical discipline as automatic as the analytical brilliance — is what makes it possible to be both bold and old. Both decisive and disciplined. Both the Finn Russell of stock pickers and the investor who cuts a three percent test position at minus seven percent without blinking.

[PAUSE]

You wrote: "The gap between this and current state is primarily about consistency of execution, not knowledge."

You're right. And closing that gap — making execution consistent, making the knowing and the doing the same thing — that's not a project for next quarter. It starts with the next position. Three percent test. Pre-defined exit. Cut if wrong. Add if right. One cycle. One piece of evidence. One step from stage one to stage three.

That's the knowing-doing gap. And that's how you close it.
