## PART 2: TRADING AS A PERFORMANCE DISCIPLINE — THE FOUNDATIONAL REFRAME

### 2.1 The Performance Analogy

- Steenbarger's foundational argument, developed most fully in *Enhancing Trader Performance*, is that ==trading is a performance discipline comparable to elite athletics, surgery, chess, or military operations.== It is not a knowledge discipline (where success comes from knowing more) or a prediction discipline (where success comes from forecasting better). It is a performance discipline where success comes from ==executing a well-defined process under conditions of uncertainty, pressure, and emotional activation.==

- This reframe matters enormously for you, because your natural orientation is analytical. Your system — the four pillars, the HQI framework, the eight-step triaging checklist — is a masterpiece of analytical architecture. But ==the analytical architecture is the preparation, not the performance.== The performance is what happens when you sit alone at your desk, the market opens, a position deteriorates, and you must decide: hold, sell, or add? In that moment, the quality of your analysis is irrelevant. What matters is the quality of your execution. And execution is a performance skill that must be developed through structured practice, not through more analysis.

- Steenbarger draws directly on the expertise development research of K. Anders Ericsson (the "10,000 hours" researcher, though Steenbarger is careful to note that hours alone are insufficient — it must be ==deliberate practice==). The key finding: expertise in any performance domain develops through a specific cycle: (1) structured practice with clear goals, (2) immediate feedback on performance, (3) targeted adjustment based on that feedback, and (4) repetition under increasingly challenging conditions. ==This cycle does not happen naturally. It must be engineered.==

- [++] **Your personal performance gap:** Your self-diagnosis — "I genuinely know what I need to do but am not executing it" — is the performance discipline gap in its purest form. In Steenbarger's language, you have invested heavily in ==knowledge development== (the system, the frameworks, the process documents) but under-invested in ==performance development== (the structured practice, the feedback loops, the deliberate repetition that converts knowledge into automatic execution). The knowing-doing gap is not a character flaw. It is a training deficit. And training deficits have solutions.

### 2.3 The Performance Loop

- Steenbarger's central operational concept is the ==Performance Loop==: **Plan → Act → Review → Refine.** This is not a one-time exercise. It is a daily, weekly, and monthly cycle that transforms ordinary investment activity into a structured laboratory for improvement.

- **Plan:** Before the market opens, define what you will do today. Not "I'll check my positions" — that is too vague. Specific: "I will check the RS of all positions against the Trend Template. If MTU has broken below the 50-day moving average, I will initiate the 30-day shot clock. I will spend 90 minutes on DKSH triaging." ==The plan converts intention into commitment.==

- **Act:** Execute the plan. This is where the performance happens. Notice your emotional state during execution. Are you anxious checking prices? Are you avoiding a position? Are you excited about a new idea and tempted to skip the triage checklist? ==The emotional data during the Act phase is the most valuable data you will collect.==

- **Review:** After market close (or at the end of your working day), review what happened against the plan. Not just the P&L — the process. Did you follow the plan? Where did you deviate? What triggered the deviation? What did you feel at the moment of deviation? ==The review converts experience into insight.==

- **Refine:** Based on the review, make one targeted adjustment to tomorrow's plan. Not five adjustments — one. The adjustment should address the highest-leverage deviation from today. ==The refinement converts insight into improved practice.==

- [+++] **The critical insight:** The Performance Loop is not journaling. It is not reflection. It is ==deliberate practice applied to investing.== Each cycle is a repetition. Each repetition builds neural pathways. Over weeks and months, the repetitions compound into automatic execution patterns — Steenbarger's equivalent of Douglas's Stage 3 (automatic implementation). ==But the repetitions must be structured, intentional, and daily. Sporadic reflection produces sporadic improvement. Daily deliberate practice produces systematic transformation.==

## PART 3: THE PERFORMANCE JOURNAL — YOUR PRIMARY COACHING TOOL

### 3.1 Why Most Trading Journals Fail

- Steenbarger is honest about a problem he observed repeatedly in his coaching work: ==most trading journals fail.== They start with enthusiasm, become rote exercises within weeks, and are abandoned within months. The reason: they focus on the wrong things. A journal that merely records entries, exits, and P&L is a trade log, not a performance journal. It documents what happened without exploring why it happened or what to do differently.

- Steenbarger identified the failure mode precisely: "Journals often did not accomplish their purpose, becoming rote exercises that did not get to the heart of either trading problems or solutions." ==The journal must answer 'What did I think, feel, and learn?' just as thoroughly as it answers 'What did I trade and what was the result?'==

- [++] **Your specific journal risk:** You have a documented pattern of generating insights but not completing them. "Multiple journal entries contain 'FINISH THIS' or 'FINISH WRITING UP THIS INSIGHT.'" This is the journal failure mode in action. The insight is generated, partially captured, never embedded, and the same pattern repeats. ==Steenbarger would say: the problem is not discipline. The problem is design. Your journal's design does not force completion. It allows open-ended reflection that trails off. The fix is structural, not motivational.==

### 3.2 The Steenbarger Performance Journal — Design Principles

- Steenbarger's journal has a specific architecture designed to prevent the failure modes above. The key design principles:

**Principle 1: Track process, not just outcomes.**

- Every journal entry should record: (a) what the plan was, (b) what you actually did, (c) where the deviation occurred (if any), and (d) what you felt at the moment of deviation. ==The deviation point is the gold.== That is where the coaching data lives.

- For you: if the plan was "check RS at 10:00" and you didn't do it until 14:00, the journal entry is not "missed morning check." The journal entry is: "Plan: RS check at 10:00. Actual: 14:00. Deviation point: 09:45, opened DKSH research instead. Feeling: excited about DKSH thesis, avoidant about checking MTU position which I suspect has deteriorated." ==That level of specificity turns a journal entry into a coaching session.==

**Principle 2: Record the emotional data.**

- Steenbarger, drawing on his clinical background, insists that ==emotions are not noise to be filtered out — they are diagnostic signals.== Your anxiety about checking a position is information. Your excitement about a new idea is information. Your reluctance to exit is information. The journal must capture these signals explicitly.

- The format Steenbarger recommends: before every major decision, rate your emotional state on a simple scale (e.g., 1-10 for confidence, anxiety, clarity). After the decision, rate again. Over time, ==the correlation between emotional state and decision quality becomes visible.== You will discover, for example, that your best decisions happen when confidence is 7/10 and anxiety is 3/10 — engaged but not activated. And that your worst decisions happen when confidence is 9/10 (overconfidence) or anxiety is 8/10 (avoidance mode).

**Principle 3: Always end with one actionable refinement.**

- Every journal entry must end with a single specific adjustment for tomorrow. Not three adjustments. Not a vague intention. ==One concrete action.== "Tomorrow I will check MTU's RS before opening any research file." "Tomorrow I will write the exit criteria for FEVR before looking at any new IG ideas." ==The single adjustment prevents the journal from becoming an exercise in self-criticism and transforms it into a structured improvement protocol.==

**Principle 4: Track best practices, not just mistakes.**

- This is the solution-focused element (covered in depth in Part 4). At least half of your journal entries should document ==what went well.== "Checked RS at 10:00 as planned. Felt clear and decisive. Noticed MTU weakness and initiated shot clock without hesitation. That felt like the right version of me." ==This is Bassham's "That's like me" integrated into Steenbarger's journal protocol.== The positive data is at least as valuable as the negative data — because it tells you what conditions to replicate.

### 3.4 The ABCD Journal for Disputing Negative Thoughts

- Steenbarger also introduces the ==ABCD framework== from cognitive therapy, which extends the three-column journal with an additional element:

- **A — Activating Event:** What triggered the emotional response? (e.g., "XVIVO reported weak kidney numbers")
- **B — Beliefs / Self-Talk:** What did you tell yourself? (e.g., "I need to understand this completely before I can decide. Let me run the full analysis again.")
- **C — Consequences:** What was the behavioural consequence of that self-talk? (e.g., "Spent 3 days in analytical deep-dive. Did not initiate shot clock. Position continued to deteriorate.")
- **D — Disputation:** What would you tell a friend in this situation? What does your system say? (e.g., "The system says: deterioration in a key driver = start the 30-day shot clock, default to zero. I don't need to understand it completely. I need to follow the process.")

- [++] The ABCD framework is particularly powerful for your ==complexity creep pattern.== The sequence A→B→C makes the link between triggering event, analytical rationalisation, and delayed action visible and explicit. The D (Disputation) breaks the chain by introducing the system's voice at the point where emotion has taken over. ==Over time, the D response becomes automatic — the system's voice starts to override the emotional voice without conscious effort.== This is Steenbarger's route to Douglas's Stage 3.

## PART 4: THE SOLUTION-FOCUSED APPROACH — AMPLIFYING WHAT WORKS

### 4.1 The Revolution in Steenbarger's Thinking

- Steenbarger's most distinctive contribution — and the one that separates him from every other author in your coaching library — is his application of ==solution-focused brief therapy== to trading. This is not a minor technique. It is a fundamental reorientation of the coaching enterprise.

- Traditional coaching (and most of the Douglas/Minervini framework) is ==problem-focused==: identify what goes wrong, diagnose the cause, and fix it. Steenbarger argues that this approach, while necessary, is ==insufficient and sometimes counterproductive.== It is insufficient because fixing problems only gets you to baseline — it doesn't reveal what excellence looks like. It is counterproductive because constant focus on failures erodes confidence, reinforces negative self-image, and creates a coaching dynamic that feels like perpetual criticism.

- Solution-focused brief therapy, developed by Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg at the Milwaukee Brief Family Therapy Center, starts from a radically different premise: ==the solution is not necessarily related to the problem.== Instead of asking "Why do you keep holding losers?", the solution-focused coach asks: "Tell me about a time when you cut a loss quickly and cleanly. What was different about that situation? How did you do that? How do you do more of that?"

- [+++] **Why this matters enormously for you:** Your coaching files are heavy with problem analysis. BFF: held too long. XVIVO: complexity trap. Ostriching. Delayed exits. Morning routine slippage. These are real patterns and they need addressing. But ==the coaching files also contain powerful evidence of excellence== that receives far less attention: Telecoms (+34%), Greggs (+25%), the cables theme identification, the Iran sector analysis, the speed and creativity of your IG process. ==Steenbarger would argue that these successes contain more coaching value than the failures — because they reveal the conditions, behaviours, and mental states that produce your best work.== The task is not to eliminate the worst. It is to ==make the best more frequent.==

### 4.2 The Exception-Finding Protocol

- Steenbarger's solution-focused method follows a specific protocol:

**Step 1: Identify the exception.**

- When was the problem absent? When did you perform as you wanted to? For your delayed exit pattern, the exception might be: "I exited [position X] on the first signal without hesitation." Or for the ostriching pattern: "During the Telecoms trade, I monitored daily and acted on the data without avoidance."

**Step 2: Analyse the exception in granular detail.**

- What was different about that situation? What was your emotional state? What had you done that morning? What was the clarity of your thesis? How strong was your conviction? Were you in a good energy state? Had you done your morning routine? ==The answers to these questions reveal the recipe for your best execution.==

**Step 3: Reverse-engineer the conditions.**

- Once you know what conditions produced the excellent performance, ==engineer those conditions deliberately.== If your best exits happen when the thesis is simple and the exit criteria are pre-defined in writing, then ==every position must have a simple thesis and written exit criteria.== If your best monitoring happens when you're in a high-energy state after your morning routine, then ==the morning routine is not optional — it is a pre-condition for performance.==

**Step 4: Do more of what works.**

- This is the Steenbarger mantra, the single sentence that encapsulates the entire solution-focused approach: =="Do more of what works."== Not "fix what's broken." Not "stop doing what hurts." Do more of what works. ==If your best decisions happen after a focused 10-minute RS check at 10:00, do more of that.== If your best entries happen after completing the full triage checklist, do more of that. If your best exits happen when the shot clock is running and the criteria are written down, do more of that.

- The follow-up (this is the key): "What would it take to move from a [your number] to one point higher?"

- ==Not ten points higher. Not the perfect score. One point.== What is the smallest, most achievable improvement that would move you up one notch? This technique bypasses the perfectionism trap — the tendency to design comprehensive overhauls that never get implemented — and focuses attention on the ==minimum effective dose.==

- **Applied to you today:** If you rate yourself 5/10 on exit discipline, what would 6/10 look like? Perhaps: "I have written exit criteria for every current position, and I've initiated the shot clock on one deteriorating position this month." That's it. Not "I never hold a loser again." Just: written criteria, one shot clock. ==6/10 is achievable this week. 10/10 is aspirational and paralysing.==

## PART 5: THE BRIEF THERAPY TOOLKIT — CLINICAL TECHNIQUES FOR TRADING EXECUTION

### 5.1 Why Brief Therapy?

- Steenbarger's clinical speciality is ==brief therapy== — short-term, structured interventions designed to produce specific behavioural changes in a compressed timeframe. This is not long-term psychoanalysis exploring childhood patterns. It is targeted, practical, and results-oriented: identify the problem behaviour, understand the maintaining mechanism, intervene with a specific technique, and measure the change.

- The brief therapy model maps perfectly onto trading execution problems because ==trading execution problems are pattern-based, situationally triggered, and behaviourally specific.== You don't hold losers all the time. You hold losers in specific situations (deteriorating thesis + high analytical investment + FOFR). You don't ostrich all the time. You ostrich when stressed, during drawdowns, when interpersonal friction is high. ==These are patterns with identifiable triggers, not character traits. And patterns can be changed with targeted intervention.==

### 5.2 Cognitive Restructuring for Trading

- Cognitive restructuring is the core CBT technique that Steenbarger adapts for trading. The method: identify the ==automatic thought== that occurs at the trigger point, evaluate its accuracy, and replace it with a more realistic thought that serves your process.

- Steenbarger's key insight: ==the automatic thought is always faster than the rational response.== When BFF drops 15%, the automatic thought — "I can't sell now, it's too low" — arrives in milliseconds. The rational response — "My framework says exit on ACH breach regardless of price" — requires conscious effort. ==The goal of cognitive restructuring is not to eliminate the automatic thought (that is impossible) but to reduce the time gap between the automatic thought and the rational response.== With practice, the gap shrinks from hours (BFF — days of rationalisation before action) to minutes (quick recognition: "That's the FOFR talking. My system says exit.").

- The three-column journal (Part 3.3) is the primary tool for cognitive restructuring. Each entry trains the neural pathway: trigger → automatic thought → rational response. ==The repetitions matter.== Steenbarger estimates that it takes 20-30 documented instances of a pattern for the rational response to begin arriving automatically. This is not abstract. This is neuroplasticity — the brain literally rewiring in response to structured practice.

### 5.3 Behavioural Experiments

- From CBT, Steenbarger borrows the ==behavioural experiment== — a structured test of a feared belief. The logic: if you believe that cutting a loss early will result in missing a rebound, ==test it.== Take a small position (progressive exposure, 3% size). When it hits the exit criteria, sell it. Then track what happens. Over 10 such experiments, you will have data — not beliefs, not fears, ==data== — on what actually happens after an early exit.

- [++] **The power for your specific patterns:** Your delayed exit pattern is maintained by the belief (largely unconscious) that "if I sell now, it will rebound and I'll feel terrible." Steenbarger's response: test it. The next time a position hits your exit criteria, sell it. Write down the date, price, and your prediction of what will happen. Then check back in 30, 60, and 90 days. ==Over a sample of 10 exits, you will have empirical evidence about the actual frequency of post-exit rebounds.== And you will likely find what Minervini's data shows: that deteriorating positions continue to deteriorate far more often than they rebound. But you need your own data, from your own portfolio, to make this real. Someone else's data is intellectual. Your own data is emotional.

### 5.4 Exposure Techniques for Avoidance

- For the ostriching pattern specifically, Steenbarger would prescribe ==gradual exposure== — the same technique used to treat anxiety disorders. The principle: avoidance maintains anxiety. The less you check your positions, the more anxious checking becomes, which increases avoidance, which increases anxiety. ==The cycle breaks by doing the feared thing in a structured, controlled way.==

- **The protocol for ostriching:**
  1. **Day 1-5:** Check portfolio values once per day at a fixed time (10:00). Just look. No analysis. No action. Just observe the numbers for 60 seconds. Rate your anxiety before and after (1-10).
  2. **Day 6-10:** Check portfolio values and RS rankings. Note any positions below the 50-day MA. Still no action required — just observation and notation. Rate anxiety.
  3. **Day 11-15:** Check portfolio values, RS rankings, and run the Trend Template assessment for any position showing weakness. If deterioration is confirmed, ==write the observation in the journal.== Still no forced action — but the data is now visible and documented.
  4. **Day 16+:** Full monitoring protocol with shot clock initiation for any confirmed deterioration.

- The principle: ==each exposure reduces the anxiety associated with monitoring.== By Day 16, looking at deteriorating positions feels uncomfortable but manageable — not threatening. And the journal entries from Days 1-15 provide a base of data that makes action feel like the natural next step, not a painful forced decision.

- [+] **Why this works for your personality:** You respond well to structured protocols (the system design capability is a documented strength). An unstructured instruction to "just check your positions more" will fail because it fights your avoidance instinct with willpower alone. ==A graduated 16-day protocol channels your strength for system design into the service of behavioural change.== The protocol is the system. Following the protocol is following the system. And you are good at building and following systems — when they are well-designed.

### 5.5 The "What Would Flow State Richard Do?" Technique

- Steenbarger frequently uses the ==observing ego== technique — asking traders to imagine their best self observing their current behaviour. Your coaching files contain the perfect version of this: "What would Flow State Richard do?"

- This technique comes directly from Steenbarger's brief therapy toolkit. The method:
  1. When facing a difficult decision, pause.
  2. Visualise yourself at your best — the Telecoms version, the Greggs version, the "champion investment athlete."
  3. Ask: "What would that version of me do right now?"
  4. Notice the gap between what you're about to do and what Flow State Richard would do.
  5. ==Act on what Flow State Richard would do.==

- The technique works because it bypasses the emotional circuitry that is driving the current behaviour. When you're in BFF-mode (holding through deterioration, rationalising, avoiding), the emotional brain is in control. Asking "What would Flow State Richard do?" activates the prefrontal cortex — the part of the brain that can evaluate the situation rationally. ==It creates a moment of self-observation that interrupts the automatic pattern.==

- Steenbarger notes that this technique becomes more powerful with repetition. Each time you successfully invoke Flow State Richard and act accordingly, the neural pathway strengthens. Over time, the question arises spontaneously at trigger points — not because you remember to ask it, but because the pattern has been trained through repeated practice.

7. **Run the weekly protocol** (Part 10.2) every Friday. Thirty minutes. Non-negotiable. This is the keystone habit — the one habit that, if established, makes all other habits easier to maintain.
