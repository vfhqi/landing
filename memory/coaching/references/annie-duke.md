## PART 1: THINKING IN BETS — THE CORE FRAMEWORK

### 1.1 Who Is Annie Duke?

- Annie Duke is a former professional poker player who won over $4 million in tournament play, including the World Series of Poker Tournament of Champions in 2004 and the NBC National Heads-Up Poker Championship in 2010. She holds a degree in cognitive science from the University of Pennsylvania and studied under the decision scientist Barbara Mellers. She left professional poker to become a decision strategy consultant and author.

- Duke's perspective matters for you specifically because **poker is the closest analogue to investing.** Both involve: (a) decisions made under uncertainty with incomplete information, (b) outcomes that are a mixture of skill and luck, (c) repeated iterations where process quality matters more than any individual result, (d) the constant emotional challenge of losing well-played hands (or well-researched positions) and winning poorly-played hands (or poorly-researched positions). Your existing Mark Douglas framework acknowledges this — "no certain outcomes for any one stock, just probabilities. This is freedom." Duke takes this further with operational tools.

- [+] **Key commonality with your approach:** Both Duke and you operate in domains where the feedback loop is noisy and delayed. In poker, you don't know whether your fold was correct until the hand is revealed (if ever). In investing, you don't know whether your exit was correct for months or years. This noisy feedback loop is what makes "resulting" so dangerous — and why deliberate frameworks for separating decision quality from outcome quality are essential.

### 1.2 "Resulting" — The Master Bias

- "Resulting" is Duke's term for the cognitive error of judging a decision by its outcome rather than by the quality of the decision process at the time the decision was made. It is, in Duke's view, the single most corrosive bias in decision-making — and the one most endemic to investing.

- **The poker illustration:** You hold pocket aces (AA) pre-flop in Texas Hold'em — the best possible starting hand. You go all-in. Your opponent calls with 7-2 offsuit — the worst possible hand. The flop comes 7-7-2. You lose. Was your decision to go all-in bad? Obviously not. The decision was mathematically optimal. The outcome was unlucky. But the *feeling* — the visceral experience of losing — makes your brain want to code the decision as wrong. That's resulting.

- **The investing parallel is exact.** You research BFF thoroughly, build a conviction case, size appropriately, and enter. The stock declines 30% because of governance issues you could not have foreseen (Consob file on Max for fraud). Was the original investment decision bad? That depends entirely on what was knowable at the time of the decision, not on the subsequent outcome. If the process was sound — if the four pillars checked out, if the thesis was disproved by genuinely new information rather than information you should have caught — then the decision was good despite the bad outcome. ==The loss tells you nothing about the quality of the decision. It tells you only about the quality of the outcome.==

- [+++] **Why this matters for you specifically:** Your journals reveal a strong tendency to judge decisions by their P&L. The telecoms case: "+34% vs +4% and could've been another 10-20% easily." This is coded as a success — and it was, at the portfolio level. But Duke would ask: was the process that produced +34% genuinely different from the process that produced BFF's loss? If both decisions followed the same quality of analysis, the same rigour of process, the same level of conviction — then the difference is luck, not skill. Coding the telecoms as "the playbook worked" and BFF as a "mistake" may be resulting.

- **The danger of resulting:** If you code good outcomes as "my system works" and bad outcomes as "I made an error," you end up trying to fix things that aren't broken (your analytical process) while ignoring things that are (your exit discipline). Resulting corrupts the feedback loop. It makes you learn the wrong lessons from every experience.

- Duke's prescription: **evaluate every decision on process quality alone.** Ask: "Given what I knew at the time, and given my process, was this a reasonable decision?" If yes, the outcome is irrelevant to the quality assessment. If no, the outcome is also irrelevant — a good outcome from a bad process is just as dangerous as a bad outcome from a good process, because the bad process will eventually produce bad outcomes at scale.

### 1.3 Decision Quality vs Outcome Quality — The 2×2 Matrix

- Duke presents a deceptively simple framework:

  |  | Good Outcome | Bad Outcome |
  |--|-------------|------------|
  | **Good Decision** | Deserved success | Bad luck — learn nothing about the decision |
  | **Bad Decision** | Dumb luck — dangerous because it reinforces bad process | Deserved failure |

- **The dangerous quadrant is "Bad Decision + Good Outcome."** This is where investors learn the worst lessons. You make a poorly researched, emotionally driven bet — and it works. You code it as skill. You do it again. Eventually, the bad process catches up with you, but by then you've reinforced the wrong behaviour over dozens of iterations.

- **The misunderstood quadrant is "Good Decision + Bad Outcome."** This is where loss aversion and resulting conspire to destroy your process. BFF may live in this quadrant — a well-researched decision that produced a bad outcome due to unforeseeable governance risk. If so, the correct response is: honour the process, cut the loss (this is where Minervini's stop discipline activates), and deploy capital into the next well-researched idea. The *wrong* response is to question your entire analytical framework because one position went against you.

- [++] **Application to your weekly review:** Your 7-keystone scoring system evaluates process execution, not P&L. This is exactly right. Duke would endorse this wholeheartedly. But she would add: when you review individual stock decisions, apply the 2×2 explicitly. For each exit, each hold, each new entry — which quadrant does it belong in? This prevents your brain from conflating the P&L column with the decision quality column.

### 1.4 Belief Calibration — From Certainty to Probability

- Duke's most practical contribution from *Thinking in Bets* is the shift from binary to probabilistic thinking. Instead of "I believe this stock will go up" (binary), Duke demands: "I assign a 65% probability that this stock will appreciate 20%+ over the next 18 months" (probabilistic).

- **Why this matters for a concentrated portfolio manager:** Your conviction assessment framework already includes eight elements — gut feel, framework inputs, wide information gathering, deep expertise, eyes-on work, bear case exploration, pre-mortems, and monitoring quality. Duke would add: after running through all eight, express your conviction as a **specific probability.** Not "high conviction" or "medium conviction" — a number. 55%. 72%. 88%.

- The power of explicit probabilities is threefold:

  1. **It forces intellectual honesty.** Saying "72%" is a very different cognitive act from saying "I'm pretty confident." The number makes you confront what you don't know. "If I'm 72% confident, what does the 28% look like? What would cause the thesis to fail?"

  2. **It enables calibration over time.** If you track your probability assessments against actual outcomes — "I said 70% on 10 positions, and 7 of them worked" — you can calibrate your judgement. Are you systematically overconfident? Underconfident? This is the most powerful feedback loop available to a discretionary investor.

  3. **It makes updating natural.** When new information arrives (quarterly earnings, IR call, macro shift), you don't face the binary question "has my thesis changed?" — which is resistant to updating because of endowment effect. Instead you face: "Does this information shift my probability from 72% to 65%? To 50%? To 40%?" ==Probability updating is continuous and incremental, which makes it psychologically easier than the binary "hold or sell" decision.==

- [+] **Direct application to your process:** Your investment strategy document specifies a "disprove-or-exit" framework with a 30-day shot clock. Duke would enhance this: at the point of investment, assign an explicit probability to the thesis. At each monitoring check, update the probability. Define a **probability floor** — say, 55% — below which the shot clock starts automatically. This is a kill criterion expressed as a probability, and it integrates seamlessly with your existing IAJA framework: Information (new data) → Analysis (probability update) → Judgement (above or below floor?) → Action (hold or start shot clock).

## PART 2: THE ART AND SCIENCE OF QUITTING — DUKE'S *QUIT* FRAMEWORK

### 2.1 Why *Quit* Is the More Important Book for You

- *Thinking in Bets* is about making better decisions. *Quit* is about making the hardest decision of all: walking away. For your specific coaching needs — "Too often, I own for too long the stocks with negative price and fundamental momentum" — *Quit* is the more directly actionable book.

- Duke's central argument: **we are systematically biased toward persistence and against quitting.** Society celebrates grit, perseverance, and "never give up." But the same psychological forces that make us persist through genuine difficulty also make us persist through deteriorating situations long past the point where quitting is the rational choice. The skill is not grit or quit — it's knowing which is which.

- [+++] ==The core insight: "The opposite of a great quitter isn't a gritty person. The opposite of a great quitter is someone who won't make hard decisions when the data demands it."== Your self-image as "decisive, dynamic, action-oriented" (who-i-am.md) should include quitting as a form of decisiveness, not as a failure of conviction. Quitting a deteriorating position IS action. Holding a deteriorating position is passivity disguised as conviction.

### 2.2 Kill Criteria — The Central Framework

- Kill criteria are specific, observable, measurable conditions defined **at the point of commitment** (not at the point of pain) that specify when you will exit an investment. They are Duke's answer to the question: "How do I make exit decisions when I know my judgement will be impaired by loss aversion, endowment effect, and sunk cost fallacy?"

- **The formula: States and Dates.** Every kill criterion combines:
  - A **state** — an objective, measurable condition. "18M forward EPS consensus falls by 5%+." "Stock breaks below 200-day MA with MA declining." "Operator quality assessed below B-tier." "Cockroach sighting in a Stage 3 stock."
  - A **date** — a deadline. "By end of Q3 2026." "Within 30 days of the trigger." "Before the next earnings report."

- **Why they must be set at the point of commitment:** ==This is Duke's most critical insight.== At the point of investment, you are rational, optimistic, and thinking clearly. You have done the analysis. You understand the risks. You can define, calmly and precisely, what conditions would invalidate the thesis. Six months later, when the stock is down 25% and your ego is attached, you cannot think clearly about exit conditions — you will rationalise, delay, and hope. The kill criteria bypass this impairment because they were set when your cognition was unimpaired.

- [+++] **Direct mapping to your 30-day shot clock:** Your existing disprove-or-exit framework is a form of kill criteria, but Duke would identify two weaknesses:

  1. **It activates too late.** The shot clock starts when deterioration is flagged — which means it requires a *judgement call* about whether deterioration has occurred. That judgement call is precisely where endowment effect and sunk cost bias corrupt the process. Duke's kill criteria are defined at entry, not at the first sign of trouble. The question isn't "has this stock deteriorated enough to start the clock?" The question is "has this stock breached the kill criterion I defined before I owned it?"

  2. **It allows too much discretion.** "Disprove the deterioration with specific, quantifiable evidence" is a standard that a motivated reasoner can always meet. When you own a position and it's down 20%, your brain is extremely creative at finding "specific, quantifiable evidence" that the thesis is intact. Duke's kill criteria remove this discretion: the criterion is met or it isn't. There is no "disprove" option — there is only "exit" or "criterion not yet met."

- **Example kill criteria for your process** (combining Duke with Minervini):

  | Kill Criterion | State | Date/Trigger | Source |
  |---------------|-------|-------------|--------|
  | Technical breakdown | Stock enters Stage 3 (50-day below 150-day, distribution volume) | 30-day shot clock begins; Stage 4 = immediate exit | Minervini Trend Template |
  | EPS deterioration | 18M forward consensus EPS falls ≥5% from peak | Shot clock begins | Duke + your investment strategy |
  | Operator failure | CFO or CEO assessed bottom quartile | Immediate exit | XVIVO rule |
  | Cockroach | Any fundamental red flag in a position already on the shot clock | Immediate exit | BFF rule |
  | Probability floor | Conviction probability falls below 55% | Shot clock begins | Duke belief calibration |
  | Love test failure | Position fails the weekly "loved here and now, happy at 25% of AUM" test | Shot clock begins | Your existing test |
  | Thesis timeout | Core thesis has not progressed within 6 months of entry | Exit or re-argue from zero | Duke + your grace period |

### 2.3 Monkeys and Pedestals — Solve the Hard Thing First

- Duke borrows this framework from Astro Teller, the head of X (formerly Google X, Alphabet's moonshot factory). The metaphor: imagine you want to train a monkey to juggle flaming torches while standing on a pedestal in a town square.

- **The pedestal** is the easy part — you can build a pedestal any time. It's concrete, measurable, and feels like progress. **The monkey** is the hard part — can you actually train a monkey to juggle flaming torches? That's the bottleneck. That's the thing that determines whether the whole project is viable.

- Teller's insight, which Duke amplifies: **people instinctively build the pedestal first** because it creates the illusion of progress. They work on the easy, comfortable tasks while avoiding the hard, uncertain core question. If the monkey turns out to be untrainable, all the pedestal-building was wasted effort — and the sunk cost of all that pedestal work makes it psychologically harder to quit.

- [++] **Application to your investment process:** In every investment thesis, there is a monkey and there are pedestals.

  - **The monkey** is the fulcrum driver — the one thing that must be true for the thesis to work. For FEVR, it's US market traction. For MTU, it's FCAS risk resolution. For a turnaround story, it's whether the new CEO can actually execute. For an oversold compounder, it's whether the transient headwind is genuinely transient.

  - **The pedestals** are everything else — the financial model, the detailed competitive analysis, the IR calls, the sell-side comparisons, the management quality assessment. These feel productive. They demonstrate rigour. They fill Notion pages. But if the fulcrum driver doesn't work, none of it matters.

  - ==Duke's prescription: attack the monkey first.== Before building the model, before the deep competitive analysis, before the 14-page case file — test the fulcrum driver. Can you validate or invalidate it quickly? If you can invalidate it, you've saved weeks of pedestal-building and the sunk cost that comes with it. If you can validate it, the pedestal work is now well-directed.

  - **Your XVIVO case through this lens:** Your own lesson — "Too much detail, no simple ACH invalidation guide" and "Learning all that stuff was trees not wood. Narrow frame distraction." You were building pedestals. The monkey — could XVIVO's management actually execute the multi-organ expansion? — was left untested while you accumulated detail. When the monkey proved untrainable (CFO bottom quartile, execution failures), you had already invested so much in the pedestal that quitting felt like wasting all that work.

  - **The monkeys-first principle also explains your IG process.** Your Ideas Generation SOP runs Business Description + Change Forces prompts through both Claude and AlphaSense, generating four outputs per stock. This is efficient pedestal-screening. But Duke would ask: ==before you generate those four outputs, can you spend 30 minutes identifying the monkey? What is the single hardest thing that must be true? Can you test it with one IR call, one data check, one industry source?== If the monkey fails in 30 minutes, you've saved the hours of IG work.

### 2.4 Escalation of Commitment — The Ratchet That Only Goes One Way

- Duke identifies escalation of commitment as the mechanism by which small, rational-seeming decisions compound into large irrational outcomes. Each incremental decision to "hold a little longer" or "give it one more quarter" feels reasonable in isolation. But the cumulative effect is a position held far past any rational exit point.

- **The mechanism:** At each decision point, you face a choice between (a) exiting now and locking in a known loss, or (b) holding and hoping for a recovery. Prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky) tells us that people are risk-seeking in the domain of losses — meaning they prefer an uncertain chance of recovery over a certain loss, even when the expected value of holding is negative. This preference for uncertainty over certain loss means you systematically choose to hold, quarter after quarter, accumulating an ever-larger loss that becomes ever-harder to exit.

- **Duke's additional insight:** ==Clearly defined goals, paradoxically, make escalation worse.== If your goal is "25% IRR" and a losing position threatens that goal, you're more likely to hold — because exiting would lock in the failure to meet the goal, while holding preserves the (increasingly remote) possibility of meeting it. The goal itself becomes a trap.

- [++] **Your BFF case through this lens:** Reconstruct the decision sequence:
  - At -5%: "Noise. The thesis is intact. I'll hold." (Reasonable in isolation.)
  - At -10%: "Sell-side downgrade, but they're wrong. I know this company. I'll hold." (The endowment effect is now active — you know the company *because you own it*, which makes you think you know it better than the sell-side.)
  - At -15%: "The IR call was weird. But I've been following this for months. If I sell now and it bounces, I'll have been wrong twice." (FOFR has arrived. The sunk cost of all your analysis is now a weight keeping you in.)
  - At -25%: "It can't go much lower. The valuation is compelling." (Hope masquerading as analysis — Minervini's Stage 4.)
  - At -35%: "Consob file. Governance risk. Okay, I'm out." (Capitulation.)

  Each step was locally reasonable. The cumulative outcome was catastrophic. ==Duke's kill criteria prevent the first escalation step.== If the kill criterion was "stock enters Stage 3 or 18M EPS falls 5% → 30-day shot clock → default EXIT," the sequence stops at the first trigger. There is no step 2, no step 3, no FOFR spiral. The kill criterion fires, the process executes, and you move on.

### 2.5 Identity-Based Persistence — When Who You Are Keeps You In

- One of Duke's sharpest insights in *Quit*: **when your identity is tied to what you do, quitting what you do feels like quitting who you are.** A founder who identifies as "a founder" can't shut down a failing company. A marathon runner who identifies as "a marathoner" can't stop at mile 20 even when their body is breaking down. An investor who identifies as "a deep researcher who holds through conviction" can't exit a deeply-researched, high-conviction position — even when the data says exit.

- [+++] ==This is directly relevant to your coaching challenge.== Your self-image includes: "I am a champion investment athlete." "I trust my judgement 150%." "I have outlier critical thinking, judgement, decision-making at big/tough times, and ability for bold/big action." These are powerful, positive identity statements — and they serve you well most of the time. But when a thesis deteriorates, these same identity statements can trap you:

  - "I trust my judgement" becomes "I shouldn't sell — my judgement says this company is good."
  - "Outlier critical thinking" becomes "I see something the market doesn't — the sell-side is wrong."
  - "Bold/big action" becomes "Selling would be timid. I'm a bold investor who holds through noise."
  - "Champion investment athlete" becomes "Champions don't quit."

- **Duke's resolution:** Identity should be built around *process execution*, not around *position outcomes.* This aligns exactly with your Steve Ward / Bassham coaching work — "on the path to becoming a great investor" is a process identity, not an outcome identity. Duke would extend this: ==a great investor is someone who sets kill criteria and honours them.== A great investor is someone who quits deteriorating positions quickly and decisively. Quitting is not the opposite of conviction — it is the *exercise* of conviction in the exit discipline.

- **The reframe:** "I am someone who holds through conviction" → "I am someone who acts on evidence, including evidence that my thesis is wrong." This is not a weaker identity — it's a *more complete* identity. It includes both holding through genuine noise (telecoms +34%) AND exiting on genuine deterioration (the BFF rule).

## PART 3: THE PSYCHOLOGY OF BAD EXITS — THE BIAS TRIAD AND FOFR

### 3.1 The Sunk Cost / Endowment / Status Quo Bias Triad

- Duke identifies three biases that compound to make exiting positions extraordinarily difficult. Critically, they don't operate independently — they reinforce each other in a vicious cycle:

- **Sunk Cost Fallacy:** The tendency to continue investing because of what has already been invested (time, money, emotional energy, analytical effort) rather than evaluating future prospects on their own merits. Duke: "The sunk cost fallacy makes us factor past investments into decisions about future spending, when rationally only future costs and benefits should matter."

  - Your specific exposure: You invest enormous analytical effort in each position — weeks of research, multiple Claude and AlphaSense reports, IR calls, management meetings, detailed case files. This analytical investment creates a massive sunk cost that makes exiting feel like "wasting" all that work. ==The XVIVO lesson is a case study: "Learning all that stuff was trees not wood." The learning itself became a sunk cost that kept you invested.==

- **Endowment Effect:** We value things more simply because we own them. Duke extends this beyond physical objects to beliefs, ideas, and decisions. Once you own a position, you psychologically overvalue it relative to alternatives — not because the position is objectively better, but because it is *yours.*

  - Your specific exposure: Your "Love" portfolio test — "every stock is loved here and now today, such that I'd be happy if it was 25% of AUM" — is designed to combat endowment effect. But Duke would note the test itself is contaminated: you're asking whether you love something you already own, which triggers the endowment bias. The more rigorous test: "If I didn't own this stock today, would I buy it at current price with current information?" ==This inversion strips out the endowment premium.==

- **Status Quo Bias:** The preference for the current state of affairs. Exiting a position (changing the status quo) requires active effort and feels like a new risk. Holding (maintaining the status quo) feels safe and requires no action. Duke: "The status quo represents a mental account with sunk costs associated with it, and switching to a new option feels like you're wasting those resources."

  - Your specific exposure: Your concentrated portfolio (16 stocks, each deeply researched) creates a strong status quo. Each position is a world of knowledge — case files, monitoring data, relationships with IR, mental models of the business. Exiting means abandoning all of that context. The temptation is always to "give it one more quarter" rather than face the cognitive cost of closing the file and starting fresh.

- [+++] **How the triad compounds:** In practice, these three biases don't just add — they multiply.

  You've spent six months analysing XVIVO (sunk cost). You own it (endowment). It's in the portfolio, and you have a case file, monitoring schedule, and IR relationship in place (status quo). Now the CFO turns out to be bottom quartile and execution is faltering. Rationally, you should exit. But:

  - Sunk cost says: "Don't waste six months of work."
  - Endowment says: "This stock is special — I understand it better than anyone."
  - Status quo says: "Changing the portfolio is risky. Better to hold and monitor."

  ==The combined force of the triad is why your 30-day shot clock defaults to EXIT, not to "reassess."== The default must be action (exit) rather than inaction (hold) because the entire weight of the triad pushes toward inaction. Only by making the default "exit" can you counterbalance the three biases pulling you toward "hold."

### 3.2 Fear of Future Regret (FOFR) — The Hidden Force

- Duke identifies FOFR as the single most powerful psychological force preventing exits. This is closely related to Minervini's observation: "The real fear that keeps people from selling is that they're going to sell it and it's going to turn around and go back up. Being wrong twice is a powerful psychological force."

- **The asymmetry of regret:** Regret from quitting something that *might have worked* (counterfactual regret) is psychologically more intense than regret from staying in something that *is definitely not working* (experienced regret). This is because:

  1. **Counterfactual regret is vivid and specific.** You can vividly imagine the stock recovering the day after you sell. The mental image is clear, painful, and humiliating.

  2. **Experienced regret is diffuse and gradual.** The regret of holding a declining position accumulates slowly — a little more each week. It's a slow burn, not a sharp pain. Slow burns are easier to tolerate than sharp pains, even when the cumulative damage is far greater.

  3. **Acts of commission generate more regret than acts of omission.** Selling is an act — you did something. Holding is passive — you did nothing. If the stock recovers after you sell, you will blame yourself for the *act* of selling. If the stock continues to decline while you hold, you won't blame yourself as intensely because you didn't *do* anything — the market did it to you.

- [++] **Your BFF case is a textbook FOFR sequence.** Your own notes: "Don't let fallen SP stop right action — fear of future regret paralysed him when SP fell 10-15% on SS downgrade." The paralysis wasn't analytical — you knew the right action. It was FOFR: "What if I sell and it bounces? I'll have compounded the error."

- **Duke's antidote to FOFR:** ==Pre-commitment strips FOFR of its power because the decision was already made.== If your kill criterion says "exit when 18M consensus EPS falls 5%+" and it falls 6%, you don't have to *decide* to sell — the decision was already made, months ago, when you were calm and rational. There is no FOFR because there is no decision to feel regret about. The criterion was met. The process executed. Just as you wouldn't feel regret about stopping at a red light, you don't feel regret about honouring a pre-committed kill criterion.

### 3.3 How the Biases Interact — A Unified Model

- Duke presents the full psychological architecture of a bad exit:

  1. **Entry:** Conviction is high. Kill criteria are either undefined or vaguely defined. The focus is on upside.

  2. **First signs of deterioration:** Sunk cost ("I've done too much work to quit now"), endowment ("I understand this company better than the market"), and status quo ("let's give it another quarter") all activate. FOFR hasn't yet appeared because the loss is small.

  3. **Continued deterioration:** Escalation of commitment kicks in. Each small decision to "hold a little longer" feels reasonable. Identity-based persistence adds weight: "I'm a conviction investor. I don't panic sell."

  4. **Significant loss:** FOFR arrives in full force. "If I sell now and it bounces, I'll be wrong twice." The sunk cost is now enormous — not just analytical time but emotional energy, reputation (you may have discussed the position with others), and the psychological investment in being right.

  5. **Capitulation:** The pain finally exceeds the FOFR. You sell at maximum loss, maximum emotional damage, and minimum analytical clarity.

- **Where to intervene:** Duke's answer is unambiguous — **step 1.** The only way to prevent steps 2-5 is to define the exit conditions at step 1, when you're clearheaded and unemotional. By step 2, the biases are already active. By step 3, they're compounding. By step 4, they're overwhelming. The intervention point is before the first decision to hold — which means the kill criteria must exist before the position exists.

## PART 5: PRE-COMMITMENT CONTRACTS — SETTING THE EXIT AT THE POINT OF INVESTMENT

### 5.1 Why Pre-Commitment Works

- Duke draws on the behavioural economics concept of "Ulysses contracts" — binding yourself to a future course of action to prevent your future self from succumbing to temptation. Ulysses had his sailors tie him to the mast so he couldn't steer the ship toward the Sirens. The ropes weren't there because Ulysses was weak — they were there because he was wise enough to know that his future self, hearing the Sirens' song, would be irrational.

- **The investing parallel is exact.** At the point of investment, you are Ulysses before the Sirens — rational, clearheaded, confident. At the point of loss, you are Ulysses hearing the Sirens — the sunk cost, endowment, status quo, and FOFR biases are all singing. ==The kill criteria are the ropes. They don't prevent you from hearing the biases. They prevent you from acting on them.==

- Duke's research shows that pre-commitment dramatically improves quit timing. When people define exit conditions in advance, they quit an average of **40-60% earlier** than when they make exit decisions in real time. This is not because they quit too early — it's because real-time decisions are systematically delayed by the bias triad.

### 5.2 A Pre-Commitment Protocol for Your Portfolio

- Based on Duke's framework, integrated with your existing process:

  **At the point of investment (Day 0):**
  1. Define the thesis in one paragraph. What is the monkey? What must be true?
  2. Assign an explicit probability to the thesis (e.g., "72% probability of 20%+ TSR over 18 months").
  3. Define 3-5 kill criteria (states + dates). Write them in the case file.
  4. Define the position size trajectory (Minervini progressive exposure: 3% → 6-8% → 10-12%).
  5. Set a thesis review date (maximum 6 months from entry — your grace period).

  **At each monitoring check:**
  1. Update the probability estimate. Has it risen or fallen? By how much?
  2. Check each kill criterion. Is any state approaching or breached?
  3. Run the "Love" test. Would you buy this stock today at this price? (Inversion strips endowment.)
  4. Run the Minervini check. What stage is the stock in? Is the Trend Template intact?
  5. ==If any kill criterion is breached, the shot clock starts. Default is EXIT in 30 days or less. No discretion.==

  **At the shot clock deadline:**
  1. The default is EXIT. Not "reassess." Not "reduce to monitoring size." EXIT.
  2. To override the default, Richard must present a written case to Watson (APM role) for why the kill criterion breach is a false signal. Watson challenges the case using Duke's bias checklist: Is sunk cost influencing this? Endowment? Status quo? FOFR? Identity?
  3. If the override is granted, new kill criteria must be defined for the extended holding period.

## PART 7: TENSIONS AND RESOLUTIONS

### 7.1 Probabilistic Framework vs Conviction-Based Approach

- **The tension:** Duke's framework is fundamentally probabilistic — every belief is a probability estimate, every position is a bet, every outcome has a distribution. Your approach is fundamentally conviction-based — "Hell yeah or no," deep research, concentrated positions, 18-month holding periods. These can seem contradictory: probabilities imply uncertainty, conviction implies certainty.

- **The resolution:** ==Conviction and probability are not opposites. Conviction is what a 75-80% probability *feels like*.== "Hell yeah" is the emotional experience of assigning a high probability to a high-payoff outcome. Duke would not ask you to abandon conviction — she would ask you to *express it numerically.* "Hell yeah" is 80%. "Meh" is 55%. "No" is below 50%. Naming the number doesn't reduce the conviction — it makes it precise.

- The advantage of precision: when new information arrives, you can update a number more easily than you can update a feeling. "My conviction has gone from 80% to 65%" is a tractable cognitive operation. "I'm less sure than before but still kind of believe in this" is not. ==The number gives you a language for partial updating that conviction-as-feeling does not provide.==

### 7.2 Pre-Commitment vs Cognitive Flexibility

- **The tension:** Duke's kill criteria are pre-committed — set in advance, honoured mechanically. Your approach values cognitive flexibility, Bayesian updating, and "strong views, weakly held." Pre-commitment can feel like rigidity.

- **The resolution:** Kill criteria are *necessary* pre-commitments, not *sufficient* decision rules. They define the floor — the conditions under which you must exit regardless of other considerations. They do not prevent you from exiting *above* the floor for other reasons (new thesis, better opportunity, portfolio rebalancing). ==The pre-commitment is a safety net, not a straightjacket.== It catches you when your biases would otherwise prevent you from acting. It doesn't prevent you from acting for good reasons at any time.

- Duke herself acknowledges this: "Kill criteria are designed to protect you from your worst impulses, not to replace your best judgement." You retain full discretion to exit early. The pre-commitment simply ensures you don't hold past the point of no return.

### 7.3 Analytical Depth vs Monkeys-First

- **The tension:** Your process values deep analytical work — multi-week case files, detailed competitive analysis, financial modelling. Duke's monkeys-and-pedestals framework says: attack the hard thing first and quit early if it fails. This could be read as "do less analysis."

- **The resolution:** Duke is not arguing for less analysis — she's arguing for *sequenced* analysis. Do the monkey work first (test the fulcrum driver), and if it survives, then do the pedestal work (full case file). This actually produces *better* analysis because the deep work is directed at validated monkeys rather than untested hypotheses. ==Your total analytical effort may not change — but the return on that effort increases dramatically because you waste less time on positions that fail at the monkey stage.==
