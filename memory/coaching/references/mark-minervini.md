### 1.4 Stage Analysis — The Four Phases of Every Stock's Life

- Minervini builds on Stan Weinstein's foundational stage analysis framework, which classifies every stock into one of four stages:

  - **Stage 1 (Basing/Accumulation):** The stock moves sideways around its 200-day (or 30-week) moving average. No clear trend. Smart money is quietly accumulating. This is where you do your research — but you do NOT deploy capital. "Don't try to catch the bottom."

  - **Stage 2 (Advancing/Markup):** The stock breaks above the moving averages, begins trending higher. Higher highs, higher lows. Volume expands on advances, contracts on pullbacks. This is the ONLY stage to own stocks. All superperformance moves occur in Stage 2. Minervini's entire system is designed to buy early in Stage 2 and sell before Stage 3 completes.

  - **Stage 3 (Distribution/Topping):** The stock's advance stalls. Volatility increases. Moving averages flatten. Volume surges on declines, fades on rallies. The 50-day breaks below the 150-day. Smart money is distributing to retail. **This is where you must sell — or at minimum reduce aggressively.** Most fundamental investors (including you, based on your documented patterns) rationalise holding through Stage 3 because the "thesis hasn't changed." But the price is telling you something the thesis hasn't caught up with yet.

  - **Stage 4 (Declining/Markdown):** The stock is in a downtrend. Below all major moving averages. Each rally fails at lower levels. **Never buy in Stage 4. Never hold through Stage 4.** This is the stage where BFF lived while you held it. This is the stage where your P&L leaks most severely.

- **Weinstein's iron rule:** "The 30-week moving average is your guiding light. If the stock is below it and the MA is declining, the stock is in Stage 4. Get out. Period." Weinstein's language is deliberately blunt because he knows how seductive the "it's cheap now" argument is.

- **Your direct parallel — the BFF lesson [---]:** The BFF case file documents exactly this pattern. Endowment bias, commitment bias, fear of future regret — all conspired to keep you holding a Stage 4 stock despite mounting evidence of deterioration. Your own words: "ACH threshold breached = EXIT; don't wait until getting closer — walking towards fire." The stage framework makes this exit trigger *mechanical* rather than *judgmental*. You don't need to debate whether the thesis has changed; you simply observe which stage the stock is in.

- **Your direct parallel — the XVIVO lesson [--]:** "Too much detail, no simple ACH invalidation guide." XVIVO became a complexity trap because the fundamental analysis grew deeper while the stock deteriorated. Stage analysis would have provided the simple invalidation signal: is this stock still in Stage 2? If not, stop analysing and start exiting.

- **Your direct parallel — the "tennis balls vs eggs" framework:** You already use this language. Tennis balls (Stage 2 stocks with strong operating momentum) bounce back. ## PART 2: POSITION MANAGEMENT — THE CORE APPLICATION FOR YOU

### 2.1 The 7-8% Maximum Loss Rule

- Minervini typically cuts even tighter — at 4-5% when the setup doesn't immediately confirm: "If I buy a stock and it goes immediately against me, something is wrong with my analysis. The market is telling me I'm wrong. I need to listen."

- **Historical evidence supporting the rule:** O'Neil's research showed that winning stocks rarely fell more than 8% below their proper buy points before beginning their major advances. If a stock falls more than 8%, the probability that it was a correct purchase drops dramatically. Waiting for it to "come back" is statistically a losing bet.

- **Application to your longer-duration approach:** Your 18-month ownership horizon means the 7-8% rule needs adaptation. You're not a momentum trader cutting on weekly noise. But the *principle* translates directly:

  - **Define your maximum acceptable loss at entry, before you buy.** Not after the stock is down 20% and you're rationalising.
  - **Use the Trend Template criteria as your stop conditions,** not arbitrary percentages. If the stock breaks below the 200-day MA and the 200-day MA rolls over (Stage 2 → Stage 3 transition), that IS your stop — regardless of what percentage decline that represents.
  - **Your existing 30-day shot clock on deterioration** (from investment-strategy.md) is the right framework. Minervini would say 30 days is too generous — but for a fundamental investor with longer holding periods, it's reasonable IF you actually enforce it. The BFF case suggests you didn't.

- [+++] **The key insight for you:** You have a 30-day shot clock rule. You have an "exit not reduce" rule on TI deterioration. You have the ACH threshold principle. **The problem is not that you lack sell rules — it's that you don't enforce them when the emotional pressure of loss aversion and FOFR kicks in.** Minervini's contribution here isn't a new rule; it's the *psychological architecture* to make existing rules enforceable.

You're deploying a lower absolute capital amount, but you're increasing relative commitment to a deteriorating setup. Minervini would say: get out entirely. ### 2.3 The "Sell Half at Double the Risk" Rule

- Minervini's trade management formula: **When a stock's gain equals twice your original stop-loss distance, sell half the position and move your stop to breakeven.** Example: Buy at £100, stop at £93 (7% risk). When stock reaches £114 (2× the £7 risk), sell half and move stop to £100. You now hold a "free" position with zero capital at risk.

- This is a fundamentally different mental model from "let your winners run" as traditionally understood. Minervini is saying: **finance your risk first, then let the remainder compound.** The first half-sell is not profit-taking — it's risk elimination.

- **Application to your approach:** Adapt this for your longer time horizons. When a position has appreciated by enough to cover the maximum loss you defined at entry, consider *top-slicing* to eliminate your risk on the original capital deployed. This is consistent with your own note: "There's a beguiling but incorrect naivety about not top-slicing big winners. The nuance is when." Minervini's answer to "when" is: when the gain finances the risk.

- Your own sizing framework already includes the 80% of max sizing principle and the two-thirds sizing for worried positions. Minervini's "sell half at 2x risk" is a complementary rule that provides a mechanical trigger for *when* to execute the top-slice.

### 2.4 Progressive Exposure — The Antidote to Going Too Big Too Soon

- One of Minervini's most powerful concepts for your situation: **progressive exposure**. He doesn't commit full position size on day one. Instead:

  1. **Test position (25-30% of intended size):** Enter with a small amount to "get feedback" from the market. If the stock immediately goes against you, cut for a tiny loss. If it confirms, add more.
  2. **Confirmation add (to 50-60%):** When the stock proves the thesis — price holds above the buy point, volume confirms, fundamentals continue to improve. Add at a higher price.
  3. **Full position (75-100%):** When the case is validated, the trend is confirmed, and the stock is in clear Stage 2 momentum. Now you have your full intended size — but most of it was added at higher prices, which means your average cost is higher but your confidence is far higher too.

- **Why this is critical for you [++]:** Your journal explicitly identifies "avoid going too big too soon in unformed theses" as a sizing rule. Your UHPYHQI category (0-5% sizing) exists precisely for this reason. But the pattern you document is that excitement about a thesis pulls you to full sizing before the thesis is validated.

  Minervini's progressive exposure framework gives you the **mechanical discipline** to honour your own rule. It turns "be careful with initial sizing" from a vague intention into a structured protocol: 3% test position → 6% on confirmation → 10% on full validation. Each step requires the stock to have *proved itself* at the previous size before you add more.

- **Your positive example — Greggs [+]:** "Trusted own judgment despite Pedro's concerns, stock up 25% YTD." If Greggs was added progressively — small initial position, confirmed by price action, added on confirmation — this is the progressive exposure framework working correctly.

- **Your negative example — BFF [---]:** The endowment bias and commitment bias documented in the BFF case suggest the position was sized based on conviction in the thesis, not on price confirmation. When the thesis began to deteriorate, the full-sized position amplified the loss and the psychological resistance to exiting.

### 2.5 The "Leader vs Laggard" Distinction

- Minervini is emphatic: **buy only the leaders, never the laggards.** A leader is a stock with the highest relative strength in its sector — the one making new highs first, pulling back the least, and advancing the most. A laggard is a stock that "looks cheap" relative to the sector leader but is cheap for a reason.

- O'Neil reinforces this: the CAN SLIM system requires stocks to have a Relative Strength rating of 80+ (top 20% of all stocks by price performance). Stocks with RS below 70 are rejected regardless of fundamentals.

- **Application to your approach:** Your RS & Breadth Dashboard is already built to answer this question. But the *behavioural challenge* is that your style (GARP + event-driven) naturally pulls you toward "oversold quality" setups — stocks that have declined and therefore look attractive on valuation. Minervini would challenge this directly: **a stock that has declined significantly is, by definition, a laggard.** The decline itself is evidence of institutional selling, deteriorating sentiment, or fundamental problems that haven't yet been fully revealed.

- **The resolution for your approach:** Not every oversold stock is wrong to buy — your third investment profile ("oversold high-quality compounders") is a legitimate edge. But Minervini's framework adds a **timing filter**: don't buy the oversold compounder until it has *stopped going down* and begun to base (Stage 1) or, ideally, begun to advance (early Stage 2). The fundamental work should be done during Stage 1 so you're ready to deploy capital at the Stage 2 breakout. This is what you did well with the telecoms ("+34% vs +4%"). It's what you failed to do with BFF.

- Your own RS Dashboard gives you a mechanical way to implement this. If a stock's Mansfield RS is negative and falling across all timeframes, it is a laggard. If its breadth metric (% above 50d MA) is below 50% for its sector peers, the entire sector is weak. Don't fight the tape.

## PART 3: THE PSYCHOLOGY OF CUTTING LOSSES — MINERVINI'S MENTAL GAME

### 3.1 Why Smart People Hold Losers

- Minervini addresses this directly in his Schwager interview: **"The real fear, and this is what keeps people from selling, is that you're going to sell it and it's going to turn around and go back up. Psychologically, being wrong twice is a very powerful force, and it makes us hold onto a position, even when it's clear it should be sold."**

- This is exactly the Fear of Future Regret (FOFR) pattern documented in your BFF lesson. The specific psychological sequence is:

  1. You buy with conviction (thesis feels strong).
  2. The stock declines. Your thesis hasn't "changed" — or so you tell yourself.
  3. You hold, rationalising: "The market is wrong. The thesis is intact. It's just noise."
  4. The stock declines further. Now you're down 15-20%. Cutting feels like admitting failure.
  5. The FOFR kicks in: "If I sell now and it bounces, I'll have been wrong twice — wrong to buy AND wrong to sell." This is psychologically devastating, so you hold.
  6. The stock continues to deteriorate. By the time you finally exit, the loss is 30-40% and months of opportunity cost have been consumed.

- [+++] **This is the single most important pattern to break.** Minervini's entire system is designed to prevent step 3 from ever becoming step 4. The stop is set at entry. The stop is honoured mechanically. There is no step 3 because the stock has already been sold at step 2.

### 3.2 Minervini's Reframe: Loss-Cutting as Skill, Not Failure

- Minervini reframes the psychology of loss-cutting fundamentally. Most investors (especially fundamental investors with deep analytical conviction) experience a stopped-out position as a failure. Minervini's reframe: **"Taking a small loss is the most professional thing you can do. It's a skill, not a failure. The amateur holds. The professional cuts."**

- In his Schwager interview: "Jack [Schwager] said, 'Mark, this is great, but it's what all the successful traders say.' I said, 'Jack, of course that's what they say, you're interviewing Market Wizards, not Market Losers.'"

### 3.3 The Asymmetry of Losses — Minervini's Core Mathematical Argument

- Minervini hammers this point relentlessly: **the mathematics of losses are asymmetric.** A 10% loss requires an 11% gain to break even. A 20% loss requires a 25% gain. A 33% loss requires a 50% gain. A 50% loss requires a 100% gain.

- The implication: every percentage point of additional loss you allow makes recovery exponentially harder. A 7% loss that you cut quickly is trivially recoverable. A 25% loss that you held through is a significant setback that requires a much larger winner to offset.

- **Your track record context:** Your documented track record shows ~8.3% CAGR over 11 years with a -54.75% maximum drawdown. That -55% drawdown required a 122% gain to recover. The compounding effect over 11 years is enormous.

- **The 3:1 profit/loss ratio:** O'Neil's formulation: if you take 20-25% profits on winners and cut losses at 7-8%, you can be profitable even if only half your picks are correct. This is the mathematical foundation of Minervini's entire system. You don't need to be right more often; you need to ensure that your average winner is substantially larger than your average loser. Cutting losses quickly is what makes this ratio possible.
