## PART 2: THE EXIT DISCIPLINE FRAMEWORK [~8 minutes]

Let's get into the core mechanical system. This is the engineering that makes everything else work.

The foundation is stage analysis. This comes from Stan Weinstein's work in the late eighties, and Minervini built on top of it. Every stock, at any given moment, is in one of four stages.

Stage 1 is basing. The stock moves sideways around its two-hundred-day moving average. No clear trend. Smart money is quietly accumulating. This is where you do your research — but you do not deploy capital. As Minervini says: don't try to catch the bottom.

Stage 2 is advancing. The stock breaks above the moving averages and begins trending higher. Higher highs, higher lows. Volume expands on advances, contracts on pullbacks. This is the only stage to own stocks. All superperformance moves occur in Stage 2. Minervini's entire system is designed to buy early in Stage 2 and sell before Stage 3 completes.

Stage 3 is distribution. The advance stalls. Volatility increases. Moving averages flatten. Volume surges on declines and fades on rallies. The fifty-day breaks below the one-fifty. Smart money is distributing to retail. This is where you must sell — or at minimum, reduce aggressively.

And here's where it gets personal. Because Stage 3 is where most fundamental investors rationalise holding. The thesis hasn't changed, right? The numbers look fine. The conference call was solid. Sell-side hasn't downgraded. But the price is telling you something the thesis hasn't caught up with yet.

Then there's Stage 4. Declining. Below all major moving averages. Each rally fails at a lower level. Never buy in Stage 4. Never hold through Stage 4.

[PAUSE]

Weinstein put it bluntly — and he was deliberately blunt, because he knew how seductive the "it's cheap now" argument is. He said: "The thirty-week moving average is your guiding light. If the stock is below it and the moving average is declining, the stock is in Stage 4. Get out. Period."

Now, let's talk about the specific rules.

Rule one: the seven-to-eight percent maximum loss. Both Minervini and O'Neil enforce this as a hard, non-negotiable ceiling. Cut every loss at seven to eight percent below your purchase price. No exceptions. No rationalisation. No "waiting a few days to see."

O'Neil in boldface: "Always, without exception, limit losses to seven or eight percent of your cost."

Minervini actually cuts even tighter — four to five percent when the setup doesn't immediately confirm. He says: "If I buy a stock and it goes immediately against me, something is wrong with my analysis. The market is telling me I'm wrong. I need to listen."

Now, your eighteen-month horizon means the raw percentage rule needs adaptation. You're not a momentum trader cutting on weekly noise. But the principle translates directly. Define your maximum acceptable loss at entry — before you buy. Not after the stock is down twenty percent and you're rationalising. Use the Trend Template criteria as your stop conditions, not arbitrary percentages. If the stock breaks below the two-hundred-day and the two-hundred-day rolls over — that Stage 2 to Stage 3 transition — that is your stop. Regardless of what percentage that represents.

[PAUSE]

And here's the key insight — and I want you to sit with this one. You already have a thirty-day shot clock on deterioration. You already have an "exit not reduce" rule on trend indicator deterioration. You already have the ACH threshold principle. The problem is not that you lack sell rules. It's that you don't enforce them when the emotional pressure of loss aversion and fear of future regret kicks in.

Minervini's contribution here isn't a new rule. It's the psychological architecture to make existing rules enforceable.

And the mathematics back this up relentlessly. O'Neil's research showed that winning stocks rarely fell more than eight percent below their proper buy points before beginning their major advances. If a stock falls more than eight percent, the probability that it was a correct purchase drops dramatically. Waiting for it to "come back" is statistically a losing bet. The stock doesn't know what you paid for it. The stock doesn't know your thesis. The stock is just a price, and the price is telling you something.

[PAUSE]

Rule two: never average down. Minervini almost always only adds to a position at a higher price, not lower. O'Neil says the same thing. Carefully average up, not down.

Now, your journal captures the tension here. You've written that you don't like binary, fully exiting. You like the concept of fifteen percent being full-sized and three percent being monitoring size. And as a portfolio construction principle for positions that have been validated, that's reasonable. But it becomes dangerous when applied to positions in distress.

Here's the critical distinction. Reducing a proven winner from fifteen percent to ten percent because valuation is stretched — that's prudent portfolio management. Keep doing that. But reducing a deteriorating position from ten percent to five percent instead of exiting — that's disguised averaging down. You're deploying a lower absolute capital amount, but you're increasing relative commitment to a deteriorating setup. Minervini would say: get out entirely. Re-enter at a higher price if and when the stock proves itself again.

Rule three: sell half at double the risk. This is Minervini's trade management formula. When a stock's gain equals twice your original stop-loss distance, sell half and move your stop to breakeven. Buy at a hundred, stop at ninety-three — that's seven percent risk. When the stock reaches a hundred and fourteen — twice the seven-pound risk — sell half and move the stop to a hundred. You now hold a free position with zero capital at risk.

This reframes everything. The first half-sell isn't profit-taking. It's risk elimination. For your concentrated approach, adapt this: when a position appreciates by enough to cover the maximum loss you defined at entry, top-slice twenty-five to thirty percent and move your mental stop to breakeven.

And then there's progressive exposure — possibly the most important concept for your specific situation.

Minervini doesn't commit full position size on day one. He enters with a test position — twenty-five to thirty percent of intended size. Gets feedback from the market. If it goes against him, he cuts for a tiny loss. If it confirms, he adds. Then he adds again on full validation. By the time he's at full size, most of the capital was added at higher prices. Average cost is higher, but confidence is far higher too.

Your journal explicitly identifies "avoid going too big too soon in unformed theses" as a sizing rule. Your UHPYHQI category — zero to five percent sizing — exists for exactly this reason. But the pattern you've documented is that excitement about a thesis pulls you to full sizing before the thesis is validated.

Progressive exposure turns "be careful with initial sizing" from a vague intention into a structured protocol. Three percent test position. Six percent on confirmation. Ten to twelve percent on full validation. Each step requires the stock to have proved itself at the previous size before you add more.

Think about what this does to the psychology of loss. If you enter at three percent and the stock fails immediately, your loss is zero-point-two percent of the portfolio. That's a rounding error. That's genuinely the cost of doing business. You barely notice it. You move on. But if you enter at ten or twelve percent because the thesis is exciting and the stock fails, suddenly you're looking at a one percent portfolio hit before you've even had time to validate anything. And now the emotional machinery kicks in — sunk cost, commitment bias, FOFR — because the loss is big enough to hurt.

Progressive exposure shrinks the initial loss to the point where cutting is psychologically painless. And painless exits build the habit that makes larger exits possible later.

Your positive example here is Greggs. You trusted your own judgment despite concerns from others. Stock up twenty-five percent year-to-date. If Greggs was added progressively — small position, confirmed by price, added on confirmation — that's the framework working. The negative example is BFF. The position was sized on conviction in the thesis, not on price confirmation. When the thesis began to deteriorate, the full-sized position amplified the loss and the psychological resistance to exiting.

[PAUSE]

And one more concept before we leave this section: the leader versus laggard distinction. Minervini is emphatic — buy only the leaders, never the laggards. A leader is the stock with the highest relative strength in its sector. Making new highs first, pulling back the least, advancing the most. A laggard is a stock that looks cheap relative to the leader — but it's cheap for a reason.

O'Neil requires a relative strength rating of eighty or above. Stocks below seventy are rejected regardless of fundamentals.

Now, your style naturally pulls you toward oversold quality setups — stocks that have declined and therefore look attractive on valuation. Minervini would challenge this directly. A stock that has declined significantly is, by definition, a laggard. The decline itself is evidence of institutional selling, deteriorating sentiment, or fundamental problems not yet fully revealed.

The resolution isn't to abandon your oversold quality pattern. It's to add a timing filter. Don't buy the oversold compounder until it has stopped going down and begun to base, or ideally, begun to advance. Do the work during Stage 1 so you're ready when Stage 2 begins. Your RS Dashboard gives you a mechanical way to implement this. If Mansfield RS is negative and falling across all timeframes, it's a laggard. Don't fight the tape.

[PAUSE]

## PART 3: THE PSYCHOLOGY — WHY WE HOLD LOSERS [~8 minutes]

Now we get into the territory that hurts. The psychology of why smart, experienced, analytical people — people who know better — hold losing positions.

Minervini addresses this directly in his Schwager interview. Listen to this quote carefully.

"The real fear, and this is what keeps people from selling, is that you're going to sell it and it's going to turn around and go back up. Psychologically, being wrong twice is a very powerful force, and it makes us hold onto a position, even when it's clear it should be sold."

[PAUSE]

Being wrong twice. That's the phrase. Wrong to buy, and then wrong to sell. That double blow to identity, to self-image, to the narrative you've built about your own analytical ability — it's devastating. And so you hold. Not because the thesis is intact. But because selling would mean admitting the thesis was wrong and then potentially watching the stock prove you wrong again.

This is exactly the Fear of Future Regret — FOFR — that you documented in your BFF lesson. And Minervini maps the emotional journey with precision. Let me walk you through it, because when you hear it described from the outside, you'll recognise every step.

Step one: Confidence. You buy with conviction. You've done the work. The four pillars check out. The thesis is strong. This is going to work.

[PAUSE]

Step two: Denial. The stock is down five to ten percent. It's just noise. The market doesn't understand the story yet. You'll be patient. This is where the Trend Template would have you out — but fundamental conviction keeps you in.

Step three: Frustration. Down ten to twenty percent. Why isn't the market seeing what you see? The numbers are fine. Sell-side is wrong. And here's where the FOFR starts to compound. You're now emotionally invested in the position being right — not because of the money, but as a validation of your analytical process. Of who you are.

[PAUSE]

Step four: Hope. Down twenty to thirty percent. It can't go much lower, right? The valuation is compelling at these levels. The thesis hasn't changed. This is the most dangerous phase. Hope masquerading as analysis. And Minervini's response to this is three words: "Hope is not a strategy."

Step five: Capitulation. Down thirty percent or worse. You can't take it anymore. You sell. By now the loss is severe, the opportunity cost is enormous, and the emotional toll has infected your decision-making on every other position in the portfolio.

[PAUSE]

The Minervini system eliminates steps two through four entirely. The stop fires at seven to eight percent — or at the Trend Template failure point — cutting directly from step one to a clean exit. There is no denial. No frustration. No hope. Because there is no discretion. The rule executes. And you move on.

Now here's where Minervini's reframe matters most. Most investors — especially fundamental investors with deep analytical conviction — experience a stopped-out position as a failure. Minervini flips this completely.

"Taking a small loss is the most professional thing you can do. It's a skill, not a failure. The amateur holds. The professional cuts."

[PAUSE]

Think about what that does to your self-image. The identity work you've done with Steve Ward emphasises being on the path to becoming a great investor, with a process-based self-image. Minervini's reframe directly supports this: the act of cutting a loss is the process working correctly. It's not evidence of poor stock-picking. It's evidence of excellent risk management. The Aspirational Richard — decisive, dynamic, action-oriented, brutal about what doesn't matter — cutting a loss is that decisiveness in action.

And the mathematics are relentless. A ten percent loss requires eleven percent to break even. A twenty percent loss requires twenty-five percent. A thirty-three percent loss requires fifty percent. A fifty percent loss requires a hundred percent gain just to get back to where you started.

Every additional percentage point of loss you allow makes recovery exponentially harder. A seven percent loss that you cut quickly is trivially recoverable. A twenty-five percent loss that you held through is a significant setback that requires a much larger winner to offset.

Minervini has been stopped out over ten thousand times in thirty-plus years. Ten thousand times. And he still made a fortune with minimal drawdown. Because each of those ten thousand stop-outs was a choice to stop being wrong. A choice that kept the losses small enough that the winners could do their work.

And here's his challenge to the fear of being wrong twice: "So what? If it turns around, you can buy it back at a higher price. Your job is to protect capital, not to be right. And the probability of a Stage 4 stock reversing to new highs is dramatically lower than the probability of it continuing to decline."

[PAUSE]

Let me leave you with one more Minervini quote before we move on, because it speaks directly to the tension between high standards and loss-cutting.

"No one will ever be so good that he or she will never take a loss."

Your very high standards — "What would Chris Hohn do?" — can paradoxically become an obstacle to cutting losses. If your self-image requires being right, every loss threatens the identity. But Minervini's identity isn't "always right." The identity is "always disciplined." And Bassham's work supports this directly — performance equals self-image. If the self-image includes "I cut losses ruthlessly and unemotionally," then cutting a loss reinforces identity rather than threatening it.

That's the reframe. That's the shift.

And I want to add one more dimension to the psychology before we move on, because it comes from Minervini's third book, *Mindset Secrets for Winning*, and it speaks directly to your coaching work.

Minervini warns explicitly against the danger of perfectionism. Your Steve Ward notes capture the same tension: "Solving for perfect, not alpha-test simple version." Minervini's approach is to take many well-structured bets, cut the ones that don't work, and let the ones that do compound. Perfection paralysis — waiting for the perfect entry, the perfect thesis, the perfect risk-reward — is a form of avoidance. It feels like discipline. It feels like rigour. But it's fear wearing a lab coat.

His emotional resilience framework centres on treating challenges as feedback, not verdicts. A losing trade is information. A losing streak is calibration data. A drawdown is a system test. This maps directly to your IAJA framework: a loss is information that requires analysis and judgement about whether the system needs adjustment, followed by action.

And his core message on personal responsibility is uncompromising. Minervini grew up in extreme poverty. Dropped out of school. He takes absolute ownership of his transformation. "You become what you think about most of the time." This echoes Bassham and Ward — but with a harder edge. No nuance. No compassion. Pure accountability.

The road to success in the stock market, Minervini says, is not a system or strategy. It's within you. And it will be realised only to the extent that you are able to control and direct your emotions as you encounter challenges.

That's pure Douglas territory. But Minervini adds a practical edge that Douglas sometimes lacks: the system itself should be designed to make emotional control easier. Pre-set stops remove the need for in-the-moment willpower. Progressive exposure removes the need for conviction before evidence. The system does the emotional heavy lifting so you don't have to.

**Tension three: Concentration.**

## PART 7: THE ELEVEN RULES AND WHAT TO DO MONDAY MORNING [~5 minutes]

Right. Enough theory. Let's talk about what actually changes on Monday.

I'm going to walk through the eleven-rule exit protocol, adapted from Minervini, O'Neil, and Weinstein, but calibrated for your concentrated, fundamental, longer-duration approach.

**Entry discipline.**

Rule one. Every new position enters the portfolio with a pre-defined exit level. Not a percentage stop — a Trend Template condition. Specifically: if this stock breaks below its two-hundred-day moving average and the two-hundred-day rolls from rising to flat or declining, the shot clock begins.

Rule two. Initial position size is progressive. Three percent test position. Add to six to eight percent on price confirmation — meaning the stock holds above the buy point for four-plus weeks and makes a higher high. Full ten to twelve percent only on fundamental plus technical validation.

Rule three. No new buys in stocks failing Trend Template criteria one through five. The fundamental research can proceed, but capital waits.

**Monitoring discipline.**

Rule four. Daily RS Dashboard check as part of your morning routine. Any held position showing negative Mansfield relative strength across all timeframes triggers an immediate watchlist review. Ten minutes. Every morning. Non-negotiable.

Rule five. Weekly stage check on all positions. Any position that has transitioned from Stage 2 to Stage 3 — fifty-day below the one-fifty, widening volatility, distribution volume — the thirty-day shot clock begins.

Rule six. The Love Test, weekly. Every stock is loved here and now today, such that you'd be happy if it was twenty-five percent of the portfolio. Any position that fails this test goes on the shot clock.

**Exit discipline.**

Rule seven. Thirty days is the maximum. From the day a shot clock trigger fires, you have thirty days to either disprove the deterioration with specific, quantifiable evidence — or exit entirely. The default at day thirty is exit. Not reassess. Not reduce to monitoring size. Exit.

Rule eight. Immediate exit signals — no shot clock needed. Stock enters Stage 4. Or: a cockroach sighting — any fundamental red flag in a position that's already in Stage 3. Or: CFO or CEO assessed as bottom quartile. That's the XVIVO rule. Immediate.

Rule nine. Never average down on a deteriorating position. If the thesis is intact but the price is broken, wait for Stage 1 basing to complete, then re-enter as a new position in early Stage 2.

**Profit management.**

Rule ten. Top-slice when the gain finances the risk. When a position has appreciated by two times the distance to your stop level, sell twenty-five to thirty percent and move your mental stop to breakeven.

Rule eleven. Don't top-slice leaders in Stage 2 with accelerating fundamentals. When a stock is the sector leader, fundamentals are accelerating, relative strength is in the top decile — let it run. Only trim when Stage 3 signals appear. This is where the snowball and flywheel test applies: is the business getting stronger with time? If yes, hold and let compounding work.
