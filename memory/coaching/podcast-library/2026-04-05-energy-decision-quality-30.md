# Episode 5: Energy Management and Decision Quality
## Daily Coaching Podcast — 5 April 2026
### Primary: Jim Loehr | Secondary: Steve Ward, Steve Peters, James Clear
### ~30 minutes (~4,500 words)

---

[OPEN]

I want to start with a reframe that's going to challenge how you think about your edge as an investor.

You've optimised a lot of things. Your process is tight — frameworks, checklists, Minervini's mechanical exits, sector rotation logic, position sizing rules. Your analytical capability is real. You've had a concentrated, long-only portfolio with meaningful outperformance. You understand the difference between noise and signal. You know how to structure decision-making under uncertainty.

But here's what Jim Loehr discovered working with the absolute elite across sport and corporate performance: none of those optimisations matter if you're trying to implement them from a depleted state.

[PAUSE]

And I'm bringing this up now because you've shown me something clear. You know instinctively when your decision quality drops. You've documented it. When your sleep is poor, you miss signals. When you're carrying unresolved tensions — the Edward situation, unclear priorities, over-analysis sitting like background static — your decisions get worse. When you're context-switching every twenty minutes, your concentration fractures. You feel it.

What you may not have fully connected is that this isn't a willpower problem or a discipline problem. It's an energy problem. And energy, as Loehr defines it, is actually quite mechanical.

[PAUSE]

Let me start with Loehr's background, because it gives the insight its force. He spent decades as a performance psychologist working first with elite athletes — tennis players, Olympic competitors — and then moved into what he calls the corporate athlete space. High-performing executives, traders, surgeons, military leaders. People whose work demands sustained cognitive performance under pressure. People, in other words, very much like you.

His central discovery was this: the best performers were not managing time. They were managing energy. They had sophisticated time management, certainly, but underneath it was an even more sophisticated energy management system. And without the energy system, time management just burns you out faster. You become more efficient at depleting yourself.

The thesis is simple and devastating: energy, not time, is the fundamental currency of high performance. The same hours can yield vastly different results depending on the quality and management of energy brought to them. An hour of fully-engaged work in what he calls the Ideal Performance State produces fundamentally different outcomes than an hour of depleted, distracted work — regardless of the clock time consumed.

He puts it like this: "We live in a world that celebrates work and activity, ignores renewal and recovery, and fails to recognize that both are necessary for sustained high performance."

[PAUSE]

Energy in Loehr's framework has four dimensions. They're not metaphorical. They're quite literal, they build on each other like a pyramid, and they're all measurable.

At the base sits physical energy. Sleep, nutrition, movement, hydration, breathing patterns. This is the quantity dimension. It determines your raw capacity. Your brain is an energy system. When you're depleted physically — when you've had poor sleep, when you're skipping meals, when you're sedentary — your cognitive capacity drops. Measurably. Your prefrontal cortex, which does the heavy lifting for complex analysis, for holding multiple variables simultaneously, for overriding emotional impulses — it's simply not available at full capacity when the physical foundation is weak. You're not being dramatic when you say you miss signals after a bad night's sleep. You actually do.

[PAUSE]

Above that sits emotional energy. The quality dimension. How positive or negative your engagement feels. Your optimism, your sense of agency, your freedom from chronic anxiety. When you're emotionally depleted, you lose access to your gut feel. Your intuition — which is a form of pattern recognition running faster than conscious analysis — goes offline. You become more dependent on explicit logic, which is slower and more error-prone. You second-guess good calls because you're not emotionally grounded enough to trust them. You cling to losing positions because actually making the change feels like admitting failure, and failure in a depleted emotional state feels catastrophic rather than informational.

Third is mental energy. The focus dimension. The ability to sustain concentration, to hold complexity, to think in chains of consequence rather than reacting to the immediate stimulus. When mental energy drains, you become tactical instead of strategic. You over-focus on the wrong variables. You lose the ability to maintain the filter that separates signal from noise. And for you — running a concentrated portfolio of five to fifteen positions, where each position requires genuine deep-dive attention — mental energy is not optional. It is the engine of the entire operation.

And at the apex sits spiritual energy. [slower] Not religious. I mean the coherence between your actions and who you've decided you want to be. The force dimension — the sense of purpose and alignment with deeply held values. You describe yourself as a champion investment athlete with an offensive spirit. When you're living that — when your actions match that identity, when you're moving forward with conviction and energy — you're fuelled by something deeper than willpower. Loehr says purpose becomes a more powerful and enduring source of energy when it moves from negative to positive, external to internal, and self to others. "I am compounding capital to build family independence" is more sustaining than "I need to not lose money." The former is spiritual energy. The latter is fear dressed up as discipline.

[PAUSE]

Here is the critical integration principle. A deficiency at any level undermines the entire structure. You cannot sustain mental focus on a foundation of poor sleep. You cannot access spiritual purpose through a fog of emotional anxiety. You cannot produce emotionally grounded decisions when your physical tank is empty. The pyramid holds or it wobbles. And when it wobbles, the decisions that come out of it wobble too.

Loehr describes the Ideal Performance State — the condition of peak performance — as relaxed yet alert, high energy yet calm, confident yet humble, focused yet flexible. It sounds paradoxical. It's not. It's what happens when all four dimensions are charged. You've experienced it. Those days where you wake rested, your mind is clear, your priorities are obvious, and your decisions feel fluid and natural rather than forced. That is the IPS. And it is cultivable. It depends on managing all four dimensions deliberately. It is not mystical. It is engineering.

[PAUSE]

Now here's the mechanic that changes everything. Loehr called it the oscillation principle.

He discovered it wiring elite tennis players with heart rate monitors. The best competitors — not the ones with the best serves or the fastest footwork, but the ones who won most consistently — were the ones who recovered most effectively in the twenty-five seconds between points. Their heart rates would spike during play, then drop fifteen to twenty percent during the changeover. They had specific routines. A particular way of walking back to the baseline. A breathing pattern. A physical reset. It looked like a ritual because it was a ritual.

The weaker players stayed elevated. They carried the stress of the last point into the next one. Over the course of a match, this accumulated. By the third set, they were making errors that had nothing to do with skill and everything to do with fatigue — physical, emotional, and mental.

The formula is this: stress plus recovery equals growth. That's the equation. Not stress alone. Not endless intensity. Stress, followed by recovery, produces adaptation. The body and brain grow stronger in the recovery phase, not the stress phase.

The corollary is equally important: stress without recovery equals breakdown. [with emphasis] Not explosive breakdown. You don't hit a wall and collapse. You gradually operate at seventy percent of your capacity and start to think that's normal. You normalise the depleted state. Your baseline drops. Your decision-making slows. Your tolerance for volatility shrinks. Your conviction weakens. And you attribute all of that to market conditions or to a change in your edge, when actually you're just running on fumes.

The counter-intuitive insight — and this is central to Loehr's whole model — is that the real enemy of high performance is not stress but the absence of disciplined, intermittent recovery. Chronic stress without recovery erodes capacity. Strategic stress followed by genuine recovery builds capacity. It is the same principle behind all effective training, whether physical or cognitive.

[PAUSE]

Now translate this to your investing day.

You are a solo investor running a concentrated portfolio across European equities. There is no team to absorb your fluctuations. No analyst to take the morning research block when you've had a bad night. No co-PM to make the sizing call when your emotional energy is low. The full weight of research, monitoring, decision-making, and execution falls on one nervous system. Yours.

You work in what you call Blue Head — intense, engaged, relentless — and you don't build in structured recovery. So you never get the Green Head decompression that actually consolidates the learning and clears the mental junk. You've told me yourself: "I oscillate fluidly between intense blue head and green head. Full engagement or full decompression. No half measures."

That's actually your energy pattern recognised. The problem is you're not timing it structurally. You Blue Head hard until you break, then you Green Head until you're restless, then you Blue Head again. The transitions are reactive, not planned. That's oscillation, but it's chaotic oscillation. And chaotic oscillation is far less efficient than structured oscillation.

Loehr studied this in the corporate athlete context. High-performing professionals face demands analogous to elite athletes: intense, sustained cognitive and emotional demands. High stakes. Periods of extreme focus followed by unmet recovery needs. Risk of burnout from chronic stress. The training principles are identical. Build capacity through deliberate stress-recovery cycles, not continuous intensity. Use specific rituals to manage energy. Treat recovery as essential performance infrastructure, not indulgence. Train incrementally, pushing beyond normal limits while managing recovery.

[PAUSE]

Steve Ward's 4Cs — commitment, concentration, confidence, control — they all decay when you're depleted, and they decay in a specific order. This is where Loehr's energy framework connects directly to your performance architecture.

Control is the first to go. Control is the gap between stimulus and response. Something happens — a position drops five percent, you get an email from Edward, the market rotates against your positioning — and you have a choice about how you respond. When you're well-rested, well-fed, physically solid, that gap is wide. You see the stimulus. You feel a reaction rising up. And you watch it without being controlled by it.

Steve Peters calls that Observer mode. Your Observer self — the Human, the rational, evidence-based, values-aligned brain — watching your Chimp self — the limbic system, the emotional, reactive, survival-focused brain — getting activated. Peters tells us that the Chimp processes information five times faster and approximately five times more powerfully than the Human. Under normal conditions, the Human has time to catch up, to assess, to respond rather than react. That gap, that watching capacity — it's where all your good decisions live.

But that Observer capacity is energy-expensive. The prefrontal cortex requires glucose, requires sleep, requires recovery to function at full power. When you're running on fumes, the gap collapses. Stimulus goes straight to response. The Chimp moves before your Observer even sees it coming. You lose control.

[slower] And the Chimp activates faster under specific conditions that you know well: fatigue, hunger, poor sleep, unresolved interpersonal tension. You have described exactly when your Chimp runs hot. That's not character weakness. That's biochemistry. Peters is explicit about this — you are responsible for managing your Chimp, and managing it starts with managing the conditions that activate it. Energy management is Chimp management.

[PAUSE]

Once control starts to go, confidence follows. Small setbacks become catastrophic in your mind. A losing position isn't data anymore — it's proof that you're wrong, that the whole process is broken. You catastrophise. Ward identified this pattern explicitly — "bad returns equals no job" — that is a catastrophising thought that emerges under depletion, not under clarity. When you're sharp and rested, the same losing position is just information. An input to your process. Nothing more.

Then your concentration narrows. You over-focus on the thing that's hurting. The position that's down. The sector that's not working. You lose the ability to hold multiple variables simultaneously. So you miss early warning signals in the rest of your portfolio. You miss new ideas entirely because all your bandwidth is consumed by the one thing that's painful right now.

And finally, commitment itself degrades. It stops being a structural decision — "This is how I invest" — and becomes white-knuckle discipline. You're white-knuckling your process because you don't trust it anymore. And white-knuckle discipline burns out. It is the opposite of the offensive spirit you've identified as your core identity. Ward is direct about this. He says: "When you decide to do something, remind yourself that it is commitment, not motivation, that matters." But commitment requires fuel. The fuel is energy. Without energy, commitment becomes grinding. And grinding is not the same as performing.

[PAUSE]

Here's something I want you to sit with for a moment. Your mechanical exit system — Minervini's setup, the Trend Template, the thirty-day shot clock, the seven-to-eight percent stop — is partly an energy intervention. You may not have designed it that way, but that is what it does.

Think about the single most draining experience in your investing life. Sitting with a deteriorating position, debating whether to act. Turning the analysis over and over. Feeling the tension of an unresolved decision. That is an energy black hole. It drains all three of your documented energy sinks simultaneously: internal tension, unclear priorities, over-analysis. It chews up bandwidth every single day until it's resolved. It activates your Chimp because the uncertainty feels like a threat. It degrades your concentration because it occupies background cycles even when you're trying to focus on something else.

[with emphasis] When the mechanical system fires and you cut the position at minus seven percent, what happens? The tension evaporates. The priority stack clears. The analytical loop breaks. You have freed mental bandwidth. You have recovered energy. The stop-loss is not just a risk management tool. It is a recovery tool. Cutting a losing position is an energy management intervention disguised as portfolio discipline.

This is why Minervini's system and Loehr's framework are natural partners. Minervini gives you the engineering — the rules, the triggers, the mechanical enforcement. Loehr explains why those rules work at a deeper level. They work because they protect your energy. They prevent the slow accumulation of unresolved tension that erodes your entire performance pyramid.

And consider the inverse. When you override the mechanical system — when you hold past the shot clock, when you rationalise staying in a position that has failed the Trend Template — you are not just taking on portfolio risk. You are taking on energy risk. You are choosing to carry an unresolved position that will drain your physical, emotional, and mental energy every single day it remains open. The cost isn't just the potential additional loss. The cost is the degradation of every other decision you make while that tension sits in the background.

[PAUSE]

Now. Let's talk about what recovery actually looks like, because this is where most people — and I suspect you — get it wrong.

The research, Loehr's and validated across dozens of subsequent studies, is clear: thirty percent of your waking day should be recovery. Not optional. Not when you feel like it. Thirty percent. That's roughly five hours if you're awake sixteen hours. Not sleep, which is separate and also non-negotiable. Five hours of actual, deliberate recovery built into the architecture of your day.

Ward puts the same number on it. He talks about shoulder periods — warm up at the start of the day, wind down at the end. Don't go from sleep straight to stress and back to sleep. Build in five-minute refresh, reset, recalibrate, recover breaks between work blocks. The thirty percent isn't soft. It's performance engineering.

[PAUSE]

Loehr identifies five sources of corporate restoration. Eat something. Hydrate. Move physically. Change channels mentally. Change channels emotionally. These are not complex interventions. They are simple, specific, and they work because they target different dimensions of the energy pyramid.

But here is the key principle that separates effective recovery from fake recovery. [slower] Recovery must be deliberate and specific. "Take a break" is not a ritual. "Walk outside for ten minutes focusing on breathing and three observations of my environment" is a ritual. Specificity matters because it triggers automaticity through repetition. And automaticity frees cognitive resources — which is the whole point.

Staring at your phone in bed is not recovery. That's context-switching without the work. A walk where you're actually observing the environment is recovery. A conversation where you're present — not half-thinking about a position — is recovery. Movement without checking email is recovery. Reading something unrelated to markets is recovery. Your ten-minute eyes-closed sessions that you've mentioned? Those are recovery. Deep sleep is recovery. Pure and simple.

[PAUSE]

Your morning routine sits at the centre of this entire framework. It is not a nice-to-have. It is not a habit. It is an energy investment that compounds through every decision you make for the rest of the day.

Think about what a good morning routine does through Loehr's four dimensions. Physical: movement, food, hydration — your body is activated and fuelled. Emotional: intention-setting, a moment of gratitude or connection — your emotional tone is positive and grounded. Mental: a clear priority for the day, one or two things that matter — your focus is set. Spiritual: a moment of connection to who you want to be, to your purpose — your actions for the day are aligned with your identity.

That's not thirty minutes of habit. That's thirty minutes of priming the entire performance pyramid. And the effect carries through hours.

James Clear is right about this. The environment design piece — making the right thing easy, the wrong thing hard — is how you protect your energy without relying on willpower. Put the training shoes by the door. Prepare the food the night before. Block the calendar so deep work happens without interruption. Design the environment so that protecting your energy is the default, not the exception.

Clear's identity question is crucial for you. "I am the type of person who protects my energy." Once you accept that identity — and it aligns perfectly with your champion athlete self-image, with your investment athlete framing — then recovery practices aren't discipline. They're coherence. They're who you are. Every time you honour the morning routine, you're building evidence for that identity. Every time you skip it, you're eroding it. Bassham would say: "That's like me" when you protect your energy. Make it like you.

You've told me your morning routine has slipped. For a long time now. And you've told me meditation is aspirational but not consistently done. That's an energy crisis signal. Because when you skip the morning routine, you're starting your day from a deficit. You're reactive from hour one. And reactivity compounds through the day. One depleted decision leads to another, which leads to another. The morning routine is not one decision. It is the upstream decision that shapes every decision after it.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's wild, and I know you're thinking it so I'll address it directly. You probably think recovery is soft. Indulgent. That it slows you down. That the hard-chargers are the ones who win and they don't take breaks.

That's exactly wrong.

Recovery is where the adaptation happens. Your brain consolidates learning during rest. Your emotional nervous system recalibrates. Your Chimp settles down — Peters is clear that exercise and recovery are the primary Chimp management tools. Your confidence rebuilds because you're not grinding it down with continuous stress. Your body repairs the micro-damage from sustained cognitive intensity. And when you come back into the next stress block, you're operating at higher capacity than before.

Loehr defines emotional toughness not as rigidity but as emotional flexibility and resilience. "Toughness depends on emotional flexibility, responsiveness and strength." True toughness is the ability to feel emotions fully, process them quickly, and shift emotional state deliberately. That processing and shifting requires energy. Without recovery, you lose the flexibility. You become brittle. And brittle breaks.

But only — [with emphasis] — only if the oscillation is real. Not fake recovery. Real recovery. Structured, deliberate, specific. Built into the architecture of the day, not bolted on when you're already broken.

[PAUSE]

Let me bring this back to your current context because it matters for right now, not just in theory.

You're operating in a risk-off posture. Iran conflict driving sector-level analysis. You're rotating into non-cyclical revenues, domestic EU footprint, banks, insurance, cable companies, healthcare. You're avoiding European chemicals, consumer discretionary, beverages. Your framework is intellectually cogent. Your sector analysis is sharp.

But it's also energetically expensive. You're holding more tension than normal. Unresolved questions about Edward sitting in the background. Priorities that aren't crystal clear. Over-analysis as a way to manage anxiety about markets you perceive as more uncertain than usual. Context-switching between new pipeline ideas — DKSH, VOD, NSIS — and monitoring existing positions.

When you're running that configuration while also depleted — when your physical foundation is weak, when your emotional tank is low, when you haven't had real recovery — your decision quality takes a hit you probably haven't attributed correctly. You notice it as "I'm being more cautious" or "The market is more difficult" or "My edge has shifted." But actually, [slower] you're tired. A tired investor becomes overly conservative. Overly focused on risk. Overly analytical as a substitute for decisive action. That might look like prudence. It's not. It's fatigue.

And because you identify as an offensive spirit, the fatigue creates internal tension. You're not living up to your own identity. So you push harder. Which depletes you further. Which widens the gap. It's a negative spiral, and the entry point is always the same: insufficient recovery.

Loehr would call this a failure of oscillation. You are stuck in the stress phase. The recovery phase has been squeezed out by the very demands that make recovery essential. And the longer you stay in that state, the more normal it feels, until you forget what operating at full capacity actually looks like. Your twelve-to-thirteen-hour days are not evidence of commitment. They may be evidence that you've lost the oscillation that makes shorter, sharper engagement possible.

Maybe it's writing down the three things that actually matter this week and explicitly parking everything else. Maybe it's blocking your calendar so deep work happens in ninety-minute blocks with genuine recovery between them. Maybe it's putting your shoes by the door and committing to a fifteen-minute walk before you look at a screen.

One drain. One interrupt.

The oscillation between stress and recovery is the mechanism that builds capacity. Loehr proved it in sport. Ward reinforces it in trading. Peters explains the neuroscience behind it. Energy first. Then decide. Every time.
