## The Foundation: Five Fundamental Truths (3:00–6:00)

Before we hit the five fears, let's lock in the ground truth. Douglas built everything on five things that are absolutely true about markets. Not maybe true. Absolutely.

First: anything can happen. At any moment. In any market. You cannot predict it. You cannot see it coming. This isn't pessimism. It's just mathematics. The number of variables is infinite. Your knowledge is finite. Therefore, surprise is not a failure of analysis. Surprise is the baseline.

Second: you don't need to know what happens next to make money. This one destroys most investors' psychology. We're wired to predict. We're wired to control. Douglas says neither is required for an edge. Your edge might be: "When condition A appears and condition B is true, the market pays me." You don't need to know why. You don't need to know what comes next. Just the probability.

Third: the distribution of wins and losses for any given edge is random. You might be right on nine out of ten trades and still have the two biggest losses hit you back-to-back. Or you might be right on four out of ten and win big on those four. The order is random. The edge is in the probability, not the sequence.

Fourth: an edge is nothing more than a higher probability. That's it. If you're right 55% of the time and you risk less on losers than you make on winners, you have an edge. Fifty-five percent! That's not genius. That's just slightly better than a coin flip. And yet most investors need to be 90% right to feel comfortable.

Fifth: every moment in the market is unique. [PAUSE] This one is subtle. It means you cannot assume that what worked yesterday will work today. Not because you're wrong. Because the variables shift. New information arrives. Correlations break. This is why rules-based systems fail over time. This is why you need Observer mode. You need to look freshly at the evidence each time.

These five truths sit underneath everything Douglas built. And they sit underneath the five fears too.

## Fear 1: Fear of Being Wrong (6:00–13:30)

Let's start with the fear that probably lives loudest in your head.

You know the Kainos trade. Built the hiring indicator. You watched the indicator like a hawk. It was your thesis proof. And then it reversed. The thesis was weakening. But instead of updating — instead of saying, "Okay, the evidence has changed, I need to rethink this" — you blamed the indicator. You doubled down on the belief that the indicator was flawed instead of accepting that your thesis might be wrong.

Here's what you wrote in your journal: "We assumed low-balling. Then indicator went negative and we blamed the indicator instead of updating thesis."

That's the fear of being wrong in action. Not the fear of the loss. The fear of being wrong about the thesis. The fear that if you admit this one is failing, it means your process is broken. It means you don't understand companies. It means you're not good at this.

[slower]

Douglas would say: that's the misunderstanding. Being wrong on one stock doesn't mean your process is wrong. Your process has a random distribution of wins and losses. Some theses will fail. That's not a flaw in the system. That's the system working.

Think about it this way. If you built a medical diagnostic test that's 80% accurate, you expect it to be wrong 20% of the time. You don't say, "The test is broken, let me blame the lab." You say, "That's how an 80% accurate test works." The same applies to your stock picking. You have an edge. That edge has a distribution. Some of your theses will be wrong. Kainos was in the random distribution. That's all.

The antidote is something you already know: strong views, weakly held. You build the thesis. You build the indicator. You have conviction. But the moment the evidence contradicts you, you update. You don't grip. You don't blame the indicator. You say, "The evidence changed. I was wrong. What's next?"

[PAUSE]

And here's the evidence that you've beaten this fear. The Protector trade. You built a thesis about management quality. You believed in the founder. And then the evidence showed that the belief was misplaced. What did you do? You exited. Forty percent loss. And you wrote in your journal: "That's what accepting you're wrong looks like."

That's not something you wrote because you were devastated. That's something you wrote because you'd done the thing. You'd gone from fear of being wrong to acceptance of wrong. You'd moved from the chimp brain — "I can't be wrong" — to the Observer brain — "I can be wrong and I can update and I can move on."

You've done this. You can do it again. Every time the fear shows up, remember Protector. Remember the journal entry. Remember that you already know how to be wrong and feel fine about it.

## Fear 2: Fear of Losing Money (13:30–19:30)

This one is sneaky because it disguises itself as prudence.

BFF. You built a thesis. The thesis was good. But then the share price fell. Ten, fifteen percent. And you froze. Not because you thought the thesis was wrong. Because you didn't want to lock in the loss. The money was already gone. The loss was already real. But your brain kept telling you: if I hold, maybe it comes back. If I sell, I've locked it in.

This is fear of losing money in its purest form. And it's completely disconnected from investing. It's purely emotional. Because you were holding the loss, not the thesis. You were holding hope, not a position.

Here's Douglas on this: "Every edge has a random distribution of wins and losses." Some of your positions will go down. That's not a failure. That's the distribution. The only question is: does the evidence support recovery? If it does, hold. If it doesn't, the loss is already real. The only person losing anything else is you, in opportunity cost, in capital trapped, in emotional energy.

The antidote is Douglas's third principle of consistency: "I objectively identify my edges. I predefine the risk. I completely accept the risk or I am willing to let go of the trade."

[with emphasis]

Notice the word predefine. Before you enter, you decide: what is the maximum I'm willing to lose? Not the maximum market price can fall. The maximum you're willing to lose. Maybe it's 15%. Maybe it's 25%. Then, when you hit that stop, the loss isn't a surprise. It's a pre-accepted business expense. Like insurance. You knew it might happen. You accounted for it. So when it does, there's no emotional shock. There's no "locked in" feeling. There's just: I paid the premium. Now what's next?

And you've proven you can do this. Corbion. You took the loss early. You didn't wait for maximum damage. You didn't tell yourself stories about recovery. You said, "The evidence is weak. I'm out." And you felt fine. Because you'd pre-accepted the risk.

So when BFF shows up again — when you're sitting on a loss and the chimp is whispering, "Just hold a bit longer" — remember Corbion. Remember that you've already proven you can accept a loss and move on. The loss isn't failure. It's data. It's the system working.

## Fear 3: Fear of Missing Out (19:30–25:00)

This one is active right now. And it's worth naming.

You're in risk-off mode. March 2026, Iran crisis backdrop. You're targeting recurring non-cyclical revenues, domestic EU footprint, banks, insurance, cable companies, healthcare. You're avoiding European chemicals, consumer discretionary, beverages. You've made a thesis-driven decision to de-risk. And somewhere in the back of your head, the FOMO is whispering: what if it's a false alarm? What if the rally starts tomorrow and I'm on the sidelines?

This is fear of missing out. And it's particularly vicious in the context of a concentrated portfolio where your competitor might make a move you don't, or the market might move without you.

[PAUSE]

Here's what Douglas would say: your edge is not in timing the bottom. Your edge is not in calling the turn. Your edge is in process. If the evidence says risk-off, then risk-off is the right move. Missing a rally is less costly than catching a crash. You don't need to know what happens next to make money. You need to trust the process that's telling you what the evidence is right now.

Douglas's third fundamental truth: you don't need to know what happens next. So when the FOMO whispers, "What if the market rallies?" the answer is: I don't know. I can't know. But I know the evidence right now. And I trust that evidence more than I trust my fear.

The antidote is this: your edge is in execution, not prediction. You've built a system. The system is saying: risk-off. Trust it. If you de-risk and the market rallies, you've missed a move. That hurts. But you lived. You still have capital. You can redeploy when the evidence shifts back. If you stay aggressive and you hit a crash, you don't recover. You're gone.

[with emphasis]

And here's the beautiful thing: you're already beating this fear. You're already de-risked. The discipline is already working. The FOMO is there — I can hear it in your journal — but you're not acting on it. You're staying true to the evidence. That's the antidote working in real time.

## Fear 4: Fear of Leaving Money on the Table (25:00–32:00)

Greggs. You exited with 6.6% gain. The stock went up forty percent more after you sold.

[slower, with warmth]

Now, the chimp is telling you something right now. The chimp is telling you that you left money on the table. That you failed to maximize. That a good investor would have held. But we need to reframe this completely.

You exited Greggs for a reason. Let's be honest about what that reason was. It wasn't because your thesis had been achieved. It was because someone else was afraid. Someone else's fear made you smaller. That's the error. Not the exit. The reason.

Douglas would say: the antidote isn't "never sell winners." The antidote is "sell for the right reason." The right reasons are: your thesis is achieved, the evidence has changed, or you need the capital for a better opportunity. The wrong reason is: I'm afraid of giving back gains. That's the chimp. That's hope and fear, not investing.

When you sell because your thesis is achieved, there's nothing left on the table. The table was never yours to begin with. You were supposed to exit when you got what you came for. If the stock keeps going up, that's randomness. That's the distribution. And the stock you bought instead, the one you funded with the Greggs proceeds, that's where your upside lives.

But let's talk about Instalco. You trimmed 4% on a broker inflation note. The stock rallied 30% after. And you wrote something really insightful in your journal: "Feels mature to take risk off but that's the chimp brain."

[with emphasis]

You identified it yourself. You knew in the moment that the trim felt mature, felt prudent, but underneath it was the chimp. Fear disguising itself as discipline. And here's the thing — you called it out. You named it. You didn't beat yourself up about the 30% rally. You said, "I know what that was. That was fear." That's Observer mode.

The antidote is this: the discomfort of a winner running after you sell is the cost of a disciplined system. Douglas says any given outcome is random. You will sell winners that keep winning. You will buy losers. That's the distribution. The system works over a hundred decisions, not one. If you're right 55% of the time and you size properly, the system wins. Individual trades are noise.

So when the chimp shows up again and tells you that you left money on the table, you answer it with Instalco. You say: I identified this. I named it. I made the decision from process, not fear. The next trade is where my focus lives.

## Fear 5: Fear of Not Being Enough (32:00–39:00)

This is the deepest one. Because this isn't about a single trade. This is about identity.

Steve Ward's coaching reframed something massive for you. Instead of "I am a great investor" or "I am failing," the frame became: "I am on the path to becoming a great investor." Not there. Not failing. On the path. Progressive. Daily. Skill-building instead of outcome-judging.

That's completely compatible with Douglas. Because Douglas says: you don't need to be a great investor on this one trade. You need to execute process on this one trade. The greatness accumulates from the consistency. From doing the right thing repeatedly, even when it's uncomfortable. Especially when it's uncomfortable.

The fear of not being enough lives in your identity. It's the voice that says: I should be making more money. I should be doing better. I should have seen Greggs's run. I should have held longer. I should be less afraid. I should be more confident. I should be further along by now.

[PAUSE]

That's the chimp talking. The chimp measures your worth in immediate outcomes. The chimp says: your last trade defines you. Your year-to-date return defines you. Whether you're up or down right now defines you.

Douglas would say — and Steve Ward would agree — your worth is in the process. Not the outcome of any single trade. The process. Are you thinking clearly? Are you identifying your edges honestly? Are you predefining risk? Are you updating when evidence changes? Are you executing with discipline even when it's uncomfortable? That's where your value lives.

And here's the evidence. You have these identity statements. You've written them down. You say: "I am a champion investment athlete." "The Finn Russell of stock pickers." These aren't accidents. You didn't write these because you thought you were better than everyone else. You wrote them because you've built evidence. You've executed in tough conditions. You've taken losses and kept going. You've had drawdowns and recovered. You've shown up.

[with emphasis]

The fear of inadequacy is the chimp. The evidence is the Observer. And the Observer is the truth-telling machine.

Every time this fear shows up — every time you feel like you should be doing better or being more or knowing more — you remember this: you are on the path. Today's job is to execute today's process. Not to be a great investor on this trade. To execute the system. The greatness will be the cumulative outcome of a hundred decisions made with discipline.

## The Master Antidote: Probabilistic Thinking (39:00–42:30)

[with deliberate pacing]

Douglas's genius was this: he identified the one mental move that makes all five fears dissolve.

Probabilistic thinking.

Not hoping for the best. Not fearing the worst. Thinking in probabilities. Thinking in distributions. Thinking like a scientist, not like a gambler.

Here's how it works against each fear:

Fear of being wrong? Probabilistic thinking says: I have an edge that's 55% accurate. Fifty-five! That means I'm wrong 45% of the time. Wrongness is baked into the model. It's not a failure. It's expected.

Fear of losing money? Probabilistic thinking says: my losses are distributed. Some will be small. Some will be large. I predefine my maximum loss. Then I accept the distribution. It's not personal.

Fear of missing out? Probabilistic thinking says: I cannot predict the bottom. I can only identify my edge in the current environment. If the edge is risk-off, risk-off is the right move. I will miss some moves. That's the distribution of a disciplined system.

Fear of leaving money on the table? Probabilistic thinking says: I will sell winners that keep winning. That's the distribution. But I'll also hold winners through my optimal exit price. The system works over time, not on any single trade.

Fear of not being enough? Probabilistic thinking says: my value is in the process, not any single outcome. A thousand-trade career has a distribution of wins and losses. My job is to execute the process each time. The distribution is mine to earn.

[PAUSE]

You've actually already said this. I want to read something back to you. You said: "I have completely emotionally internalised that the market is a voting machine in the short term and I'll be in drawdown at least 50% of the time."

That's probabilistic thinking. That's you at your best. You've integrated the idea that drawdowns are coming. You've accepted it. You've made peace with it. The 2.0 version — and this is worth working toward — is applying that same thinking to each individual position. Not just your portfolio. Each position. When you sit with a stock that's down 20%, you can feel okay about it because you know: 20% down is part of the distribution for a disciplined investor. It's not a failure. It's the system working.

## Your Three Voices: Building the Practice (42:30–44:30)

Here's how to operationalize this. Steve Peters gave you a framework. Three voices. The Chimp, the Computer, and the Human.

The Chimp speaks in fear. It says: sell now, you're going to lose it all. Or: don't sell, you'll lock it in. Or: everyone else is making more money. The Chimp is fast, emotional, and wrong about the future.

The Computer speaks in logic and process. It says: I have an edge. This is how I execute it. These are my rules. These are my pre-accepted losses. This is how I know when to hold and when to sell. The Computer is slow, systematic, and right about probability.

The Human adds wisdom. It integrates the Chimp's emotions and the Computer's logic and says: I understand the fear. It's real. And I've built a system that accounts for it. I'm going to trust the system.

When Fear 1 shows up (being wrong), you hear the Chimp: "You got this wrong, you're not good at this." You respond with the Computer: "I have a process that's 55% accurate. This is a distribution outcome. Next." Then the Human: "I've done this before. I know how to recover."

When Fear 2 shows up (losing money), the Chimp says: "Don't lock it in." The Computer says: "I predefine max loss. I've hit it. I exit." The Human says: "I've taken losses before and felt fine because I'd pre-accepted the risk. This is the same."

You already have all three voices. You just need to make them louder.
