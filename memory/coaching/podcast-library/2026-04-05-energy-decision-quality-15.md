# Episode 5: Energy Management and Decision Quality
## Daily Coaching Podcast — 5 April 2026
### Primary: Jim Loehr | Secondary: Steve Ward, Steve Peters, James Clear
### ~15 minutes (~2,250 words)

---

[OPEN]

Right. Let's talk about something that's going to feel obvious once you hear it, but probably isn't woven into how you're making investment decisions right now.

Energy. Not time. Energy.

[PAUSE]

You've optimised time relentlessly. Your process is tight. You've got frameworks, checklists, Minervini's mechanical exits, sector rotation logic, position sizing rules. Your analytical capability is real. But here's what Jim Loehr discovered working with the best athletes in the world: time management is a waste of your mental capacity if you don't have energy management underneath it.

And the reason I'm bringing this up now is because you know this about yourself. You've told me. You've documented it. You've got periods where you're running on fumes — unresolved tensions with Edward, unclear priorities sitting like static in the background, over-analysis eating you alive, context-switching every twenty minutes — and when that happens, your decisions get worse. Not by accident. By thermodynamics.

Loehr's central thesis, developed across decades of work with elite performers, is devastatingly simple. Energy, not time, is the fundamental currency of high performance. The same hours can yield vastly different results depending on the quality and management of energy brought to them. An hour of fully-engaged work in what he calls the Ideal Performance State produces fundamentally different outcomes than an hour of depleted, distracted work — regardless of the clock time consumed.

[PAUSE]

Let me be precise about what Loehr means by energy. It's not caffeine. It's not enthusiasm. It's four things, and they're mechanical. They build on each other like a pyramid.

At the base sits physical energy. Your sleep, your nutrition, your movement, your breathing. This is the quantity dimension — it determines your raw capacity. Without it, your brain simply doesn't work at full power. You've documented this yourself. When you sleep poorly, you miss signals. You over-focus on small noise. You get reactive. That's not a character problem. That's a depleted prefrontal cortex.

Above that sits emotional energy. The quality dimension. Your optimism, your sense of agency, your freedom from chronic anxiety. When you're emotionally depleted, you lose access to your gut feel — that intuitive pattern recognition that runs faster than conscious analysis. You second-guess good calls. You cling to losers because changing course feels like admitting failure. The emotional colouring of your engagement affects everything you produce.

Third is mental energy. Focus. The ability to hold multiple variables in mind simultaneously. To say no to distractions. To think in chains of consequence instead of reacting to the immediate stimulus. When mental energy drains, you can't do your job. You become tactical instead of strategic. You over-focus on the wrong variables. You lose the forest entirely.

And at the apex sits spiritual energy. [slower] Not religious. I mean the sense that what you're doing matters. That you're aligned with who you want to be. You describe yourself as a champion investment athlete with an offensive spirit. When you're living that — when your actions match that identity — you're energised. When you're not, when you're defending or ostriching or waiting, you're running on willpower instead of energy. And willpower is expensive. It runs out.

Here is the critical principle. A deficiency at any level undermines the entire structure. You cannot sustain mental focus on a foundation of poor sleep. You cannot access spiritual purpose through a fog of emotional anxiety. The pyramid holds or it wobbles. And when it wobbles, the decisions that come out of it wobble too.

Loehr puts it like this: "To be fully engaged, we must be physically energized, emotionally connected, mentally focused, and spiritually aligned with a purpose beyond our immediate self-interest." That is the Ideal Performance State. Not a mystical flow state you stumble into. A cultivable condition that depends on managing all four dimensions deliberately. Relaxed yet alert. High energy yet calm. Confident yet humble. Focused yet flexible. That is where your best investment decisions live. And you cannot get there from depleted.

[PAUSE]

Now here's the mechanic that most people miss. Loehr called it the oscillation principle. He discovered it wiring elite tennis players with heart rate monitors. The best competitors — not the ones with the best serves or the fastest footwork, but the ones who won most consistently — were the ones who recovered most effectively in the twenty-five seconds between points. Their heart rates would spike during play, then drop fifteen to twenty percent during the changeover. They had specific routines. A particular way of walking back to the baseline. A breathing pattern. A physical reset.

The weaker players stayed elevated. They carried the stress of the last point into the next one. And by the third set, they were making errors that had nothing to do with skill and everything to do with accumulated fatigue.

The formula is this: stress plus recovery equals growth. Stress without recovery equals breakdown. And the counter-intuitive insight is that the real enemy of high performance is not stress but the absence of disciplined, intermittent recovery. Chronic stress without recovery erodes capacity. Strategic stress followed by recovery builds it.

[PAUSE]

Now translate this to your investing day.

You are a solo investor running a concentrated portfolio. There is no team to absorb your fluctuations. No analyst to take the morning research block when you've had a bad night. No co-PM to make the sizing call when your emotional energy is low. The full weight falls on one nervous system. Yours.

Your body and your brain are not built to be at high intensity for eight hours straight. No human is. You work in what you call Blue Head — intense, engaged, relentless — and you don't build in the recovery. So you never get the Green Head decompression that actually consolidates the learning and clears the mental junk. You've normalised depleted as your baseline.

Loehr studied this pattern in what he calls the "corporate athlete." The comparison to sport is not metaphorical. You face intense, sustained cognitive and emotional demands. High stakes. Periods of extreme focus followed by unmet recovery needs. Risk of burnout from chronic stress. The training principles are identical. Build capacity through deliberate stress-recovery cycles, not continuous intensity. Use specific rituals to manage energy. Treat recovery as essential performance infrastructure, not indulgence.

[PAUSE]

Steve Ward's 4Cs — commitment, concentration, confidence, control — all decay when you're depleted, and they decay in a specific order.

Control goes first. You lose the gap between stimulus and response. Something hits you — a position down five percent, an email from Edward, a news cycle — and suddenly you're reacting before you've thought it through. Steve Peters calls this the Chimp taking over. Your limbic system processes information five times faster and approximately five times more powerfully than your rational brain. Under normal conditions, the Human — your Observer, your evidence-based self — has time to catch up. But under fatigue, the gap collapses. The Chimp fires before your Observer even sees it coming.

[slower] When you're well-rested, well-fed, when your physical foundation is solid — you get that gap. You feel the Chimp activate, and you watch it without being it. That Observer mode is where your best decisions live. But it's energy-expensive. When you're running on fumes, the Chimp moves faster than you can observe. That's not a character flaw. That's physiology. The prefrontal cortex requires energy to function. When energy is low, the Chimp runs the show.

Then confidence starts to crack. Small setbacks become catastrophic. A losing position isn't data anymore — it's proof that you're wrong, that the whole process is broken. You catastrophise. Ward identified this pattern in you — "bad returns equals no job." That thinking doesn't emerge when you're sharp and clear. It emerges when you're depleted.

Then concentration narrows. You over-focus on current pain. You miss early warning signals in other parts of your portfolio because all your bandwidth is on the one thing hurting. You lose the forest.

And finally commitment itself degrades. It stops being a structural decision — "This is how I invest" — and becomes white-knuckle discipline. And white-knuckle discipline burns out. It is the opposite of the offensive spirit you've identified as your core identity.

Ward is clear about this. He says: "When you decide to do something, remind yourself that it is commitment, not motivation, that matters." But commitment requires fuel. It requires the 4Cs operating together. And the fuel is energy. Without energy, commitment becomes grinding. Grinding is not the same as performing. Grinding is survival. You are not trying to survive. You are trying to compound at twenty percent.

[PAUSE]

Here's something specific to you that you may not have fully recognised. Your mechanical exit system — Minervini's setup, the Trend Template, the thirty-day shot clock, the seven-to-eight percent stop — is partly an energy intervention.

Think about what happens when you're sitting with a deteriorating position, debating whether to act. That is an energy black hole. It drains all three of your documented energy sinks simultaneously: internal tension, unclear priorities, over-analysis. It chews up bandwidth every single day until it's resolved.

[with emphasis] When the mechanical system fires and you cut the position, what happens? The tension evaporates. The priority stack clears. The analytical loop breaks. You have freed mental bandwidth. The stop-loss is not just a risk management tool. It is a recovery tool. Cutting a losing position is an energy management intervention disguised as discipline.

[PAUSE]

Now. Recovery. What does it actually look like?

The research — Loehr's and validated across dozens of subsequent studies — says thirty percent of your waking day should be recovery. Not optional. Not when you feel like it. Thirty percent. That's roughly five hours if you're awake sixteen hours. Not sleep, which is separate and also non-negotiable. Five hours of actual recovery built into your day.

Ward puts the same number on it. Thirty percent recovery per day. Create shoulder periods — warm up at the start of the day, wind down at the end. Don't go from sleep straight to stress and back to sleep. Build in five-minute refresh, reset, recalibrate, recover breaks throughout the day.

Your morning routine sits at the centre of this. It's not a nice-to-have. It's an energy investment that compounds through every decision you make for the rest of the day. James Clear is right about this — environment design is more powerful than willpower. Make the right thing easy. Put the training shoes by the door. Prepare the food the night before. Block the calendar so deep work happens without interruption. Design the environment so that protecting your energy is the default, not the exception.

You've told me your morning routine has slipped. For a long time now. That's not a habit problem. That's an energy crisis signal. Because when you skip the morning routine, you're starting from a deficit. You're reactive from hour one. And reactivity compounds through the day. One depleted decision leads to another, which leads to another. The morning routine is not one decision. It is the upstream decision that shapes every decision after it.

Clear's identity question is crucial here. "I am the type of person who protects my energy." Once you accept that identity — and it aligns perfectly with your champion athlete self-image — then recovery practices aren't discipline. They're coherence. They're who you are.

[PAUSE]

Here's your takeaway. One thing. This week.

[with emphasis] Identify your single biggest energy drain. Not your longest to-do item. Your energy drain. The thing that's silently consuming bandwidth in the background. For you, that might be the unresolved Edward tension. It might be the context-switching between new ideas and monitoring. It might be the ostriching that happens when you're low on gas. It might be the absent morning routine.

Pick one. Just one.

And make one decision about it. Not to solve it permanently. Just to interrupt it. Maybe that's scheduling the conversation. Maybe it's writing down the three things that actually matter this week and parking everything else. Maybe it's putting your shoes by the door and committing to a fifteen-minute walk before anything else.

One drain. One interrupt.

[beat]

Because you cannot perform at your best when depleted. And recovery isn't optional. It's not indulgent. It's not soft. It is where the actual performance gains are consolidated. The oscillation between stress and recovery is the mechanism that builds capacity. Without it, you're just eroding.

Energy first. Then decide.
