Let me set the context first.

You own a stock in Stage 2. The stock has just broken out of a bases and is advancing. Earnings are growing. The chart is clean. The relative strength is positive. You're making money. This is good.

[PAUSE]

[PAUSE]

The pullbacks serve a purpose. They shake out the weak holders. They reset sentiment. They let institutions accumulate more shares without driving the stock up. They create the technical conditions for the next leg of the advance. If you understand this, you stop viewing pullbacks as failures. You start viewing them as resets.

But — and this is critical — not all pullbacks are healthy. Some pullbacks are the first signal that the stage is changing. That the stock is moving from Stage 2 (advance) into Stage 3 (deterioration) into Stage 4 (severe decline). And if you can't tell the difference, you'll make one of two errors.

[PAUSE]

## What Healthy Pullbacks Look Like — The Diagnostic Signals — 8 min

A healthy pullback in Stage 2 has specific characteristics. I'm going to cover each one because they work together. When all of them line up, you have your answer: hold, maybe even add. When they don't, you have your answer too: exit.

[PAUSE]

**First: depth is contained.**

Minervini has explicit guidelines here. A normal pullback in Stage 2 is ten to twenty percent from the recent high. The very first correction after a stock breaks out of a major base can be deeper — twenty to twenty-five percent — because that's when the distribution from weak holders is heaviest. But after that, healthy pullbacks contract. Each successive pullback should be shallower than the last.

Here's the key threshold: if the pullback exceeds twenty-five to thirty percent from the high, something has changed. That's no longer a normal correction. That's a crack in the stage itself. The supply has increased. The demand has weakened. You're at the boundary between healthy and deteriorating.

[PAUSE]

For you, this means when a position pulls back more than fifteen percent from its recent high, you start paying attention. You start looking at the other signals. If it pulls back twenty to twenty-five percent, you're in the yellow zone. If it exceeds thirty percent, you're in red until the other signals tell you otherwise.

**Second: the thirty-week moving average holds as support.**

This is maybe the most important signal. Minervini uses the one-hundred-fifty-day — which is roughly equivalent to the thirty-week — because it represents the long-term trend. In a healthy Stage 2, the stock is advancing, which means the thirty-week MA is rising. When the stock pulls back, it pulls back TO that moving average, not through it. It may dip slightly below for a day or two — that's noise — but it closes back above. The MA itself continues to rise, or at minimum stays flat.

[PAUSE]

The moment the stock closes below a rising thirty-week MA, you stop. You watch. The moment the MA itself flattens and starts to roll over, you know the trend is weakening. And the moment the MA turns downward? Stage 2 is over. Stage 3 has begun.

This is exactly what Avanza showed you. Every pullback — and there were many — tested that moving average, found support, and bounced. The MA stayed rising throughout. That's your reference case. When a pullback looks like that, you hold.

**Third: volume contracts during the pullback.**

This is the X-ray of institutional behaviour. In a healthy pullback, volume dries up. Why? Because the institutions that own the stock aren't selling. They're holding. The volume you see is retail panic sellers and short-term traders cashing in small profits. But the big money is patient. They're waiting for the dip to be done. They're ready to buy if it gets too cheap.

[PAUSE]

When you look at your chart, what you're looking for is declining volume during the decline. The stock drops, but the volume bar gets smaller. That tells you there's no institutional distribution. That tells you supply is running out. And when the stock bounces, volume should return — volume on the ADVANCE. That's institutions buying the dip. That's the textbook signal for a healthy pullback.

Now, the inverse. If volume EXPANDS while the stock is declining, institutions are dumping shares. They're selling into any bounce. That's distribution. That's not a pullback. That's the beginning of deterioration. And if volume fails to return when the stock tries to bounce, that confirms it. The institutions have left. The next advance won't have fuel.

**Fourth: the VCP — volatility contraction pattern — continues.**

This is Minervini's signature concept. In healthy Stage 2, each successive pullback is shallower than the last. First pullback: minus twenty percent. Second pullback: minus fifteen percent. Third: minus ten percent. The volatility is contracting. Supply is drying up. The stock is consolidating its gains at a higher level each time.

[PAUSE]

If you plot this on your chart, you see a pattern of gradually narrowing waves. Each pullback doesn't go as deep. Each advance builds from a higher floor. This is the pattern of institutional accumulation — steady, controlled, patient. It's the opposite of the chaotic distribution pattern of Stage 3.

So here's the diagnostic question: is this pullback shallower than the last one, or deeper? If it's deeper — if it exceeds the depth of the previous pullback — the contraction has reversed. The pattern has broken. And that's a deterioration signal.

**Fifth: relative strength holds.**

You have Mansfield RS. You watch it religiously. In a healthy pullback, even if the stock is down, the RS should hold positive or dip only slightly. Why? Because the stock is underperforming less than the market or its sector. The selling is sector-wide or market-wide, not specific to your stock.

[PAUSE]

If the stock is falling harder than the market during the pullback, that's a red flag. That means institutional sellers are more aggressive in this name than in the broader index. That's specific weakness. That's not a healthy pullback. That's deterioration.

So you watch the RS line. If it's drifting downward during the pullback, you've got a warning. If it turns negative — if the stock is in bottom quartile for RS — you've got a red alert.

**Sixth: recovery is swift.**

Healthy pullbacks resolve. They don't linger. Minervini's guidance: three to eight weeks. A stock pulls back, tests support, consolidates for a few weeks, and then advances again. If the pullback extends beyond eight to ten weeks without resolution, something is wrong. The stock is either building a new base (moving from Stage 2 back into Stage 1) or deteriorating (moving from Stage 2 into Stage 3).

So when a pullback starts, set a mental shot clock. Thirty to sixty days to resolve. If the stock hasn't reclaimed the fifty-day MA by then, or if volume hasn't returned on an advance attempt, you've got a problem.

**Seventh: the fundamental context supports continuation.**

Here's the reset. All of this technical analysis only means something if the fundamental thesis is still intact. If earnings estimates are being cut, if management guidance turns negative, if a competitor launches something disruptive, if the margin trend reverses — if any of that happens during a pullback, the pullback isn't healthy. It's deterioration dressed up as noise.

[PAUSE]

So you check: are earnings estimates stable or rising? Has management issued any guidance? Is there any competitive threat emerging? Is the thesis intact? If yes to all of these, the pullback is technical. If no to any of them, the pullback is fundamental, and it's a deterioration signal.

All seven of these signals together paint a picture. When they all line up — contained depth, MA holding, volume contracting, VCP intact, RS holding, swift recovery, fundamentals intact — you have your answer. This is a healthy pullback. You hold. Maybe you add.

## What Deterioration Looks Like — The Stage 2 to Stage 3 Transition — 8 min

Now let's flip the coin. When does a pullback become deterioration?

[PAUSE]

Minervini and Weinstein have spent their careers learning to read the earliest signals. They don't wait for the stock to crash fifty percent. They exit when the stage is changing, when the tape is telling them the story isn't going to end well. Let's walk through the warning signs.

**First: the thirty-week MA flattens and turns down.**

This is the most lagging signal, but it's also the most decisive. When the moving average stops rising and starts to flatten, the long-term trend is weakening. Institutions are not accumulating anymore. They're neutral. And the moment the MA turns downward, the stage has changed. Stage 2 is over.

By the time the MA turns down, you've usually lost some return. This is why the other signals matter — they fire earlier. But when the MA does turn, it's your final confirmation. The stage is changing.

**Second: volume surges on declines, fades on rallies.**

This is the mirror image of the healthy pullback. Instead of declining volume on the pullback, you see expanding volume. The stock pulls back and the volume bars are getting bigger. That's institutional selling. That's distribution.

[PAUSE]

And then on the rally attempt — because the stock will try to bounce — the volume fades. The bounce comes on lower volume. That tells you there's no institutional buying on the dip. The big money isn't there anymore. They've already left. This X-ray of volume is the most reliable read of institutional behaviour.

**Third: resistance forms at lower levels. Lower highs appear.**

In Stage 2, each rally makes a new high. The stock is advancing. But in early Stage 3, the rallies fail. The stock can't break out. It makes a new high, then rolls over. The next rally attempt fails at the level of the previous high. You start to see lower highs. You start to see a resistance level forming at a price that used to be support.

This is the visual signature of deterioration. The momentum has stalled. The stock is running out of energy. Each attempt to advance succeeds less than the last.

**Fourth: the fifty-day MA crosses below the one-hundred-fifty-day.**

This is the death cross. The opposite of the golden cross. Short-term momentum is now negative relative to the long-term trend. The stock's near-term direction has turned down. The stage transition is confirmed.

By the time this happens, you've usually lost ten to fifteen percent from the top. But when you see this cross, you know: the stage is changing. This is a mechanical signal, and it works.

**Fifth: deeper corrections that don't recover.**

In healthy Stage 2, pullbacks are shallow and swift. In deteriorating Stage 3, the pullbacks get deeper and they don't bounce. The stock pulls back twenty-five or thirty percent from the high. You wait for the bounce. It comes, but it's weak. It fails at the previous high. The stock rolls back over. Now it's base-building at a lower level.

[PAUSE]

Or worse, the stock just grinds lower. There's no sharp correction. There's just a slow, persistent fade. Day after day, week after week, the stock is slightly weaker. That's deterioration without drama. And it's just as dangerous.

**Sixth: relative strength breaks down.**

The RS line, which was holding positive in the healthy pullback, turns negative. Your stock drops from top quartile to bottom half. It's underperforming the sector. It's underperforming the market. Institutions have decided to go somewhere else.

**Seventh: fundamental cracks appear.**

Earnings estimates get cut. Management guides down. A competitor releases something disruptive. The gross margin trend reverses. The return on capital compresses. The competitive advantages that you saw in Stage 2 are no longer evident.

When the fundamental deterioration CONFIRMS the technical deterioration, Stage 3 is certain. You're not waiting anymore. You're exiting.

**Eighth: Minervini's climax top signals.**

Minervini has also identified specific patterns at the END of an extended Stage 2 advance. These are late signals, but they're dramatic. The stock gaps up on massive volume after weeks of advance — that's an exhaustion gap. Institutions are handing final shares to the last retail buyers. The widest daily range of the entire move happens. The largest single-day point gain happens. These are final blowoff moves. And they usually precede a sharp reversal.

[PAUSE]

If you see these signals — exhaustion gap, widest range, largest point move — you're at the peak of Stage 2. The deterioration is about to start. These are your last chance signals to exit before the stage turns.

## The Decision Framework — Hold, Watch, or Exit — 5 min

Let's pull all of this together into a decision framework. You're looking at a position. It's pulled back. Your Chimp is uncomfortable. You need to decide: do I hold, do I watch, or do I exit?

[PAUSE]

**Scenario one: HOLD (and potentially add).**

The pullback is ten to twenty percent. The thirty-week MA is rising and acting as support — the stock is testing it and holding above it. Volume is contracting on the pullback. The RS is holding positive. The VCP is contracting — this pullback is shallower than the last one. The fundamental thesis is intact. No earnings cuts, no management guidance issues, no competitive surprise.

When all of these line up, you have your answer: this is a healthy pullback. You hold. You might even add. This is Avanza. This is the textbook signal.

**Scenario two: WATCH CLOSELY (yellow alert).**

The pullback is reaching twenty to twenty-five percent. The thirty-week MA is flattening — it's no longer clearly rising, but it hasn't turned down yet. Volume is mixed — some expansion on the decline, some contraction. The RS is weakening but not yet negative. The fundamentals are stable, but you're starting to see some estimate cuts.

[PAUSE]

This is the ambiguous zone. The pullback is getting deeper. The technical picture is becoming less clear. The deterioration signals are appearing, but they're not yet confirmed. This is where you set the shot clock. You give it thirty days. You ask: will the stock reclaim the fifty-day MA within thirty days on returning volume? Will the RS stabilise? Will the earnings be stabilised?

If yes to all of those, you're back in the HOLD scenario. If no, you're moving to the next scenario.

**Scenario three: EXIT (red alert).**

The pullback exceeds twenty-five to thirty percent. The thirty-week MA has turned down or is flat and rolling over. Volume expanded on the declines. RS is negative. The fifty-day MA has crossed below the one-fifty. You're seeing lower highs. The earnings estimates are being cut. The management tone is weakening.

[PAUSE]

This is Stage 3. The stage has changed. The stock doesn't know you own it. It doesn't care about your thesis. It doesn't care about the price you paid. You exit. You don't debate. You don't average down. You execute.

Now, here's what matters about this framework for you specifically. Both of your documented errors — the Instalco trim and the BFF hold — come from the same source: emotional response to ambiguity. When the picture is unclear, you guess. You feel.

[PAUSE]

This framework converts guessing into observation. You don't ask "how do I feel about this pullback?" You ask: "What are the five diagnostic signals telling me?" Depth. Moving average. Volume. RS. Fundamental context. You observe these signals. They add up to a verdict. You execute based on the verdict. The framework decides. Your emotions execute.

This is what it means to move from Chimp-driven to Observer-driven. You've still got the conviction. You've still got the thesis. But you've got objective signals that trigger your decision-making. You're not rationalising anymore. You're reading.
