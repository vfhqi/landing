## PART ONE: WHAT STAGE 1 LOOKS LIKE (5 minutes)

[Slower pace. Educational tone. Building foundation.]

Let's define Stage 1 first, because you need to recognize it to know when you're leaving it.

Stage 1 is basing. The stock is moving sideways—oscillating in a narrow range—around its 150-day moving average, also called the 30-week moving average. And here's the key structural characteristic: the moving average itself is flat or declining gently. The stock isn't trending. It's stuck.

Think about what this looks like on a chart. You draw a horizontal line around 100. The stock bounces between 95 and 105 for months. Maybe longer. Six months. Nine months. A year. Each time it hits 95, it finds buyers. Each time it hits 105, it meets sellers. The price action is utterly boring. Predictable even.

Volume during Stage 1 is characteristically low and contracting. Nobody's interested. The stock isn't on the radar of momentum traders. Institutions aren't building positions—or if they are, they're doing it quietly, gradually, without moving the price. The daily volume might average 50,000 shares. Then it drops to 30,000. Then 25,000. The stock is literally losing attention. In markets, that's actually a sign of something important happening beneath the surface.

[BEAT]

Here's where this connects to your process. For fundamental investors—and you are a fundamental investor—Stage 1 is where the real work happens. The stock price is telling you something: the world doesn't yet believe the thesis. Or more precisely, the world hasn't noticed the thesis at all. This is your edge.

You're doing your research during Stage 1. You're building the financial model. You're running the IR calls. You're reading the filings. You're developing conviction. You're stress-testing the bear case. You're essentially answering the question: is this business better than the market thinks?

If the answer is yes—if you've genuinely found something the market is mispricing—then you want to own it. And the best time to own it is while it's still in Stage 1, while nobody else has noticed.

But here's the problem. Stage 1 is boring. It can last a very long time. Minervini has studied thousands of stocks, and some of the best ones—the ones that eventually advance 100%, 200%, 300%—spend 9 months, 12 months, even 18 months in Stage 1. During that time, the fundamental case is crystallizing, the business is executing, but the stock price isn't moving. This tests your conviction.

[PAUSE]

The deeper insight, though, is this: the length and depth of Stage 1 directly correlates with the magnitude of the eventual advance. Minervini's rule: "The longer the base, the bigger the space." A stock that spends 12 months consolidating—really wringing out the negativity, exhausting the sellers, proving the business thesis over and over in quarters—that stock, when it finally breaks out, often advances 50%, 75%, 100%, sometimes more. Because the base is doing the work. The base is saying: everyone who was going to sell at these prices has already sold. The buyers are building positions. Supply is drying up.

**Signal One: The 30-Week Moving Average Starts to Rise**

This is the earliest structural signal. Remember, during Stage 1, the 30-week MA is flat or declining. The stock is oscillating around it, but the MA itself isn't moving. Then one day—and this takes weeks to manifest, not days—the moving average starts to turn upward. Gently at first. Maybe 1% per week. But the direction is changing. The MA is going from horizontal to up-sloping.

Why does this matter? Because the moving average is a smoothed view of what's happening beneath the surface. When it turns up, it's saying: the underlying momentum has shifted. More recent weeks are stronger than earlier weeks. This is the machine starting.

**Signal Two: Price Closes Above the 30-Week MA and Stays Above It**

This is confirmation of Signal One. It's not enough for the MA to turn up. The stock needs to close above the MA—not for one day, but for multiple weeks. And crucially, early in Stage 2, the stock often pulls back to test the MA. This is where amateur traders get shaken out. The stock breaks above, rallies 8%, then pulls back to touch the MA. They panic. They sell. That's when Minervini buys.

The test of the 30-week MA is a feature, not a bug. It's the market testing whether supply is really exhausted. The bounce off that test—the stock pulling back to the MA and then rallying again—that's your confirmation that Stage 2 is real.

**Signal Three: The 50-Day MA Crosses Above the 150-Day MA**

This is the golden cross. Shorter-term momentum (50-day) is now above longer-term trend (150-day). When this happens, it's saying: the near-term direction has shifted above the long-term direction. Momentum is winning. This cross is a classic technical signal, and yes, everyone knows it. But that's why it works. When it happens on volume, institutions notice.

**Signal Four: Volume Expansion on the Breakout**

Minervini is adamant about this: the breakout must come on above-average volume. Not just any volume. Above-average daily volume. This shows institutional commitment. Someone with size is saying: I'm willing to buy at these prices, visible, at scale.

O'Neil's rule: at least 40% to 50% above average daily volume. So if the stock normally trades 200,000 shares per day, the breakout day should see 280,000 to 300,000 shares minimum. Without this, the breakout is suspect. It's not a real institutional move. It's retail enthusiasm. False breakout.

**Signal Five: Relative Strength Improvement**

Why does this matter? Because it's the market voting on the thesis. The stock is outperforming because fundamentals are becoming visible, or momentum is building, or both. Relative strength in early Stage 2 is a powerful indicator that the market is starting to agree with your thesis.

**Signal Six: Volatility Contraction Pattern—VCP**

This is Minervini's signature move. During Stage 1, especially the later stages, the stock's price swings contract. The first minor correction down: maybe the stock falls 20%. Second correction: 15%. ## PART FIVE: PROGRESSIVE EXPOSURE AT STAGE 2 ENTRY (4 minutes)

[Practical, risk-management focus.]

So the Trend Template passes. The stock hits all eight criteria. What's your entry? How big do you go?

The answer is: you don't deploy your full position on day one. You size progressively.

**Step One: Test Position**

Your first entry is 25-30% of your intended full position size. If you think the stock should be 8% of portfolio, your first buy is 2-2.5%. You're testing whether the breakout is real. You're giving the stock room to breathe. You're not fully committed.

Why? Because the first bounce after a breakout is sometimes noise. The stock might rally 5% on the breakout day, then pull back 3% the next day. That's fine. But if it keeps pulling back and closes below your breakout level, you need to know that the move might be false. You haven't overcommitted.

**Step Two: Confirmation and Scaling**

If the stock holds the breakout level over the next few days, if volume sustains, if the stock closes above the moving averages for multiple weeks, you scale up. You add to 50-60% of intended size. You're confirming that the thesis is valid and the technical setup is real.

**Step Three: Full Deployment**

Only when the thesis is validated *and* the technical setup confirms do you go to full size. This might take 4 weeks. It might take 8 weeks. That's fine. You're not in a race.
