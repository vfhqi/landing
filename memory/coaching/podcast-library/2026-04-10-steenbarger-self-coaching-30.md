## THE PERFORMANCE LOOP: PLAN, ACT, REVIEW, REFINE

Steenbarger's core system is deceptively simple. It's a loop: Plan, Act, Review, Refine.

**Plan.** You decide what you're going to do. You set criteria for success. You identify what could go wrong. You size the position. You set exit triggers.

**Act.** You execute the plan. Not the plan you wanted to execute in hindsight. The plan you made in advance. This is where discipline lives—not in cleverness, but in following through on what you decided when you were calm and rational.

**Review.** You look back at what happened. Not to judge yourself emotionally. But to extract information. Did the thesis play out? Did you execute your plan? Did something change that you missed?

**Refine.** You update your mental models. You adjust your process. You practice harder on the things that are breaking.

And then the loop repeats.

This is why you have a journal. This is the whole point of deliberate practice.

You actually have a real example of this loop working in your track record: Avanza. You had a CEO meeting. You noted in your journal that the call was "vague because I was vague on the numbers." You made a mental note: keep IR calls very financial-centric. Must have a model ready. Next CEO meeting? You did better. You had a model. The calls got more concrete. The decision-making got clearer.

That's the loop: Act (vague call) → Review ("I was vague because I was unprepared") → Refine ("Create SOP: always have a model ready for IR calls") → Act differently next time.

Steenbarger would say most investors never complete this loop. They act. They review emotionally ("I lost money, I'm bad at this"). And they don't refine. They just hope next time is different.

## THE PERFORMANCE JOURNAL: DESIGN PRINCIPLES

Which brings us to the performance journal. Steenbarger spent decades studying how investors journal. And his findings are pretty clear: Most journals don't work.

Why? Because they're designed wrong.

Most journals are emotional venting. "I'm frustrated with the market. The fed is making bad decisions. My portfolio is down. I made a stupid trade." This feels good. It's cathartic. And it teaches you nothing.

Steenbarger's performance journal has a specific design. It's not venting. It's information extraction. The goal is to identify patterns in your own thinking and behavior that predict success or failure.

Here's what the performance journal includes:

**Situation.** What was the objective fact? Stock went down. Earnings came out. Management commentary shifted. Not "the market is stupid." The concrete fact.

**Decision/Action.** What did you do? Hold. Exit. Add. Trim. Stay calm. Panic. What actually happened?

**Rationale.** Why did you think this was the right thing to do? This is important, because it forces you to articulate your thinking in real time. Not in hindsight.

**Outcome.** What happened next? Six months later, where is the stock?

**Reflection.** What did you learn? Not "I was right" or "I was wrong." But: What would a more disciplined investor do? What mental process did you use that worked? What mental process led you astray?

The journal is not about being right. It's about feedback. It's about training your mind to recognize what good thinking looks like, even when the outcome is uncertain.

You actually have a great example of this from Kainos. Your team built a hiring indicator. The CFO said muted things about hiring. Your team assumed he was low-balling. Then the indicator went negative. And you wrote in your journal something like: "CFO saying muted comments on hiring. We assumed low-balling. Then indicator went negative and we blamed the indicator instead of updating thesis."

That's a journal entry. That's extracting real information about a real pattern. You were fighting the data because you liked the company. You were rationalizing. The lesson: When multiple independent signals all point the same direction, update your thesis. Don't blame the signal.

## THE THREE-COLUMN COGNITIVE JOURNAL

Now, Steenbarger has a specific tool for the journal. It comes from cognitive therapy. It's called the three-column journal. And it looks like this:

**Column One: Situation.** Objective fact. Nothing more.

**Column Two: Automatic Thought.** The thought that popped into your head. The story you told yourself. This is where your biases live.

**Column Three: Realistic Re-evaluation.** What's a more realistic interpretation of the situation?

Let me show you how this works with an example from your track record.

**Situation:** Ocado went up 100 percent. You owned it. It was a winner.

**Automatic Thought:** "My thesis was brilliant. I saw something the market missed. I'm a great investor."

**Realistic Re-evaluation:** "COVID pulled forward demand for online grocery by six months. Ocado benefited from exogenous timing. Maybe we were partly lucky. It went up 100 percent purely due to the COVID pull-forward. The stock moved for reasons outside my control, not because my analysis was superior."

Do you see what happened? The first thought is ego-driven. It's a story. It sets you up for overconfidence. The realistic re-evaluation is humble. It acknowledges luck. And it teaches you something: in your next investment, you need to distinguish between moves caused by your good analysis and moves caused by lucky external timing.

That's what the three-column journal does. It breaks you of the automatic thoughts—the stories—that sabotage you.

Here's another example, this one from BFF. This is a stock you held for years. It was a winner. But then things started to deteriorate. Your journal entries say things like: "It feels mature and calm to take risk off, but that's the chimp brain tricking you."

Let's use three-column thinking:

**Situation:** BFF had an IR call that felt "diagnostic and super weird." Management mentioned some hiring concerns. You felt uneasy.

**Automatic Thought:** "It's just one off call. Maybe I'm overreacting. The business is still fundamentally sound. I shouldn't panic like some amateur."

**Realistic Re-evaluation:** "My gut says something is wrong. Gut signals are often the first thing to pick up on inconsistency. The call was strange. That strangeness is data. When one cockroach appears, more are hiding in the dark. I should investigate quickly before dismissing my own intuition."

That's the three-column journal in action. It takes the automatic thought ("don't be stupid, don't panic") and replaces it with something more realistic: "Your intuition is an information processor. Trust it."

## SOLUTION-FOCUSED SELF-COACHING

Now, Steenbarger's framework also includes something called solution-focused self-coaching. This is borrowed from brief therapy. And the idea is this: Most people, when they're stuck, focus on what's wrong. "I panic. I overthink. I don't act." And they get more stuck.

Solution-focused thinking asks a different question: When does this NOT happen? When do I perform well?

Let me give you an example. You have a problem pattern: analysis paralysis. You gather details. You run models. You read reports. You loop on uncertainty. And you don't act. Keywords is a case study. Your team saw slowdown. You saw slowdown. Clear signals. But you "drowned in unnecessary detail" instead of pulling the capital reallocation trigger.

But here's the question Steenbarger would ask: When have you acted decisively without getting stuck in analysis?

And the answer is: Corbion, January 2022. Your journal says this: You cut Corbion. Fast. No drama. You saw deterioration. You exited. Done.

So what was different? What were the conditions that let you act decisively there, but not in Keywords?

Maybe it was: Corbion deterioration was visible and undeniable. The signal was strong. In Keywords, the signal was murkier. You could talk yourself into it.

Maybe it was: Corbion needed no detailed analysis. The thesis broke. Move on. Keywords is complex. AI, semiconductors, pricing dynamics. Lots of room for analysis.

Or maybe it was: With Corbion, you trusted your judgment call immediately. With Keywords, you waited for team consensus or external validation.

This is solution-focused self-coaching. You're not trying to fix your weakness. You're trying to understand your strength and do more of it.

Steenbarger would say: Identify the conditions under which you perform best. Then deliberately structure your work to create those conditions more often.

## COGNITIVE JOURNAL EXAMPLE: KAINOS

Let me walk through one more complete example using the three-column journal, because I want this to be concrete.

**Situation:** Kainos, February 2026. Hiring indicator you and your team built turned negative. CFO had been saying muted things about hiring in quarterly calls. Your team had assumed the CFO was low-balling.

**Automatic Thoughts:** "The indicator is probably lagging the reality. These things are imperfect. The CFO might be conservative. The hiring might be coming back." And then, when the indicator stayed negative: "Maybe the indicator itself is broken. Maybe we built it wrong. Maybe our thesis was right and we're just looking at bad data."

**Realistic Re-evaluation:** "Multiple independent signals are pointing the same direction. CFO commentary was muted. Hiring indicator is negative. When two separate data sources both say the same thing, that's not coincidence. That's evidence. The thesis has changed. I should update my conviction and exit, not spend more time trying to figure out why the data might be wrong."

That's the cognitive journal. You're breaking the automatic thought ("the data is wrong; my thesis is right") and replacing it with realistic thinking ("the data is telling me the thesis has changed").

And the performance benefit is this: Next time you see conflicting data and your company thesis, you'll process it faster. You'll recognize the pattern. You'll make the exit decision sooner. You'll avoid the extended loss period.

That's what Steenbarger is talking about. It's not about being smarter. It's about being faster.

## BURFORD AND THE OODA LOOP

One more example, because I want to tie this to something Minervini taught you. You've written about Burford: "Share price OODA loop faster than mine equals red line."

What does that mean? The OODA loop is Observe-Orient-Decide-Act. It's the decision cycle. Minervini taught you that stock prices move in their own OODA loops. The price trends. The sentiment shifts. The narrative evolves. And if your OODA loop is slower than the stock's price OODA loop, you're always buying the top and selling the bottom.

Burford is a case where the price moved faster than your analysis. The stock decided before you did. You were still evaluating. The price was already repricing.

Steenbarger's solution is deliberate practice on this specific skill. You journal about it. You ask yourself: What could I have done to speed up my OODA loop on Burford? Did I need more data, or did I need to make decisions faster with the data I had? Was I gathering information when I should have been deciding? Am I analyzing to gain conviction, or am I analyzing to delay a decision?

That's the self-coaching question.

## THE WEEKLY REVIEW

Let me just touch on one more thing. Steenbarger emphasizes a weekly review. Not quarterly. Weekly.

Here's what a weekly review might look like:

**What went well this week?** Don't just mean P&L. Which decisions did you make well? Where did you stay disciplined? Where did you catch yourself before a mistake?

**What went poorly?** Where did you break your own rules? Where did you get emotionally reactive? Where did analysis substitute for decision-making?

**One thing to improve.** Based on this week, what's one specific thing you're going to practice next week?

You don't have this routine, as far as I know. You have a journal. But you're not doing structured weekly reviews where you extract the pattern and commit to one thing to practice.

That's low-hanging fruit. One hour per week. Structured reflection. One deliberate practice target. That could move the needle significantly.

## FINAL SYNTHESIS: PERFORMANCE DISCIPLINE

So here's the synthesis. Brett Steenbarger's framework is fundamentally this: Investing performance is a discipline, not a gift. It's not about being smarter than everyone else. It's about executing your process with consistency and learning from feedback.

For you specifically, it means:

**One:** Formalize your journal. Use the three-column cognitive work. Situation, automatic thought, realistic re-evaluation. Do this for major decisions.

**Two:** Build deliberate practice into your week. Not just journal for journaling's sake. But identify one specific weakness and practice it deliberately.

**Three:** Do a weekly review. Extract the pattern. Identify exceptions. Ask: When do I perform best? What are the conditions?

**Four:** Trust your own judgment completely. You're a solo investor now. That's your architecture. Own it.

**Five:** When a thesis breaks, exit quickly. Don't spend time trying to figure out why the data might be wrong. Update your conviction and reallocate.

And the ultimate goal is this: Become someone who can make good investing decisions consistently, without external help. Because you don't have external help. You have yourself, your journal, and your discipline.

That's what Steenbarger teaches. That's what solo investing demands.
