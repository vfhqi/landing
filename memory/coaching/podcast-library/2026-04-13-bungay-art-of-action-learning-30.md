### LESSON 1: The Three Gaps Are Why You Hold Losers Too Long (~640 words)

Bungay begins with a simple observation. There are three distinct reasons why intentions and actions diverge in organisations. And they're not all about people being lazy or dumb.

The first is the Knowledge Gap. This is when you don't know what to do because you don't have the information, or because the information is confused, or because you have so much information that the signal is buried in the noise. XVIVO is your textbook Knowledge Gap. You entered that position with a thesis. The thesis was sound. Execution not potential — judge companies on what they do, not what they could do. And then execution started to slip. But it wasn't a clean signal. There were better days and worse days. There was management commentary that suggested awareness. There were quarterly numbers that muddled the picture. After a hundred and thirty hours of analysis, you couldn't definitively say: the game is over. So you held.

The Knowledge Gap requires a time boundary. At some point, even intelligent analysis has diminishing returns. Bungay's military analogy is stark: at some point, you order the attack or you don't. You don't wait for perfect intelligence in war. You act on sufficient intelligence. In your investing, you need to declare: this is a triage tier. This is an ESA. This is a deep-dive. And at each tier, there's a decision deadline. If you can't answer the key question by the deadline, the default is no, not maybe.

The second is the Alignment Gap. This is when you know what you should do, but you don't do it. You know the rule. You understand the thesis. You've written it down. And yet you don't execute. This is where BFF lives. You have an ACH framework. You understand that competing hypotheses need to be disproved, not merely questioned. And in January, BFF's IR call was super weird. Hiring problems. No respect for great people. Bad capital allocation history. These are cockroach signals — one problem implies more in the dark spaces. You knew the rule. You knew the signal. You should have exited. You didn't.

Why? Bungay's answer is subtle. It's not because you're irrational. It's because you have a constraint, and the constraint wasn't explicit enough. The constraint wasn't "exit when ACH threshold is breached." The constraint was "exit when you're certain ACH is breached and you've thought carefully about it and you've considered the risk of being wrong." That's not a constraint. That's a permission structure. It lets you decide whether to execute your rule every single time. And when endowment bias and commitment bias are pushing one direction, you always find a reason to wait.

The Alignment Gap requires a constraint, not an instruction. Instructions are brittle. They depend on your judgment in the moment. Constraints are robust. They're pre-commitments designed when you were calm. A constraint sounds like: "If cockroach signal observed, start 30-day shot clock." Not: "Think carefully about whether to exit." Not: "Consider the risk-reward." A constraint is: when this happens, this follows.

The third is the Effects Gap. This is when people know what to do, they intend to do it, but it doesn't happen because the signal got lost between detection and action. Kainos in 2020. Your team saw something was wrong with the product development cadence. The signal was there. But it didn't escalate to you in a way that triggered decision-making. So the position stayed, and it bled. Keywords the same way. There were early signals of management drift that didn't reach threshold for escalation.

The Effects Gap requires automatic escalation. It requires a process where certain triggers fire without judgment. Not "tell Richard if you notice something." That's too vague. It's "if revenue growth decelerates below 25% YoY, automatic escalation." Signals don't drift — they escalate.

So: the Knowledge Gap needs a time boundary. The Alignment Gap needs a constraint. The Effects Gap needs escalation protocol. Three different gaps. Three completely different fixes. This is why generic "more discipline" doesn't work. You're not disciplined. You're executing the wrong type of fix.

### LESSON 2: Your Best Rules Are Already Bungay — Make Them Structural (~640 words)

Here's the uncomfortable truth. You already know how to solve the three gaps. You've already built the right structures. They're just not structural enough. They exist as mental models, not as operating system components.

Your cockroach rule is pure Bungay. "Cockroaches are never alone. Once you see one, assume there are two or three more in the dark spaces." This is directed opportunism. Act on sufficient signal, not certain signal. That's exactly Bungay's thesis. But it's a mental model. It's something you know. It's not something that fires automatically when the signal hits.

Your position sizing rule — "never hold more than five percent of AUM if I'm holding more than eight percent of the float" — that's Bungay too. That's a friction-aware constraint. It acknowledges that liquidity matters. It acknowledges that your ability to exit constrains your entry. But it's not enforced. It's a guideline. Fasadgruppen was your learning engine there: "We've snookered ourselves with liquidity." The lesson was live. But the constraint wasn't structural.

Your one-page thesis rule is Bungay. "Good plan executed beats great plan in the drawer." You know this. You repeat it. It's in your speech. But when you're uncertain, you add detail. You write case files. You run more scenarios. The mental model says "one page." The execution says "three pages, come back to one page later." That's not a constraint. That's a suggestion.

And here's what Bungay doesn't say but implies: structural constraints feel uncomfortable. They feel like you're tying your own hands. Because you are. Bungay's whole point is that in high-uncertainty environments, the person who can commit to constraints in advance outperforms the person who preserves optionality. You preserve optionality, and you find reasons to wait, to hold, to hope.

So let's make three of your rules structural this week. Not as a discussion. As a change to your operating system.

Rule one: Knowledge Gap trigger. "If I cannot decide whether to promote from triage to ESA within 14 calendar days, the default is no. I do not wait for more data. I park the idea and move on." This solves the XVIVO problem. Fourteen days, two independent research passes, one KQ memo. If you can't decide, you've told you everything you need to know about the idea. It's not sufficiently obvious to justify your time.

Rule two: Alignment Gap constraint. "If cockroach signal is observed, I start a thirty-day exit clock immediately. No analysis override. No complexity exemption. Thirty days to close." This solves the BFF problem. One problem detected, assume two more, exit within the shot clock.

Rule three: Effects Gap escalation. "If a current holding misses revenue guidance, or guides down guidance, or loses a major customer, I get the full KQ memo from Watson within twenty-four hours. Not a summary. The full analysis. And we decide within forty-eight hours." This solves the Kainos, Keywords problem.

Three rules. Three different gaps. Three different fixes. But they're not rules if they're optional. They're only rules if they're structural. If they're built into your process. If they fire automatically.

Bungay's argument is that this, paradoxically, gives you more optionality over time. Because you're not in constant decision mode. You're not in constant analysis mode. You're in execution mode. And execution mode is faster, clearer, and less prone to the kind of emotional reasoning that held you in BFF too long.

### LESSON 3: Constraints Beat Instructions — The "Do Not" List (~640 words)

Your ESA framework is thirteen categories. Fifteen dimensions of analysis. It's comprehensive. It's thorough. It's also an instruction. It tells you what to do. And instructions are brittle, because they depend on your judgment in the moment.

Bungay flips this. Instead of instructions, use constraints. Instead of "to do" lists, use "do not" lists.

S4 Capital in 2018. You had an accounting insight that flagged potential risk. That was a cockroach. Your instruction was "run more analysis." Your actual behaviour was "hold and monitor." Your constraint should have been "first cockroach signal within 14 days, exit automatically." The loss would have been smaller. Not because you'd have had better analysis, but because you'd have had less time to rationalize.

Greggs. You had a clear investment thesis. And then you found yourself deferring to your partner's view on a position management decision. That's a delegation that should have been constrained. "I am the decision-maker for add/trim/exit calls on every position. Non-negotiable." But that constraint didn't exist. So execution got fuzzy. And you lost the opportunity.

Instalco. You'd sized into a position. Then liquidity moved against you. Your thesis was sound — "it'll recover" — but your position was illiquid. You wanted to trim. But trimming meant realizing a loss, and the loss felt premature. So you held. And held. Until you were forced to sell at the worst time. The constraint you needed: "If a position trades below 20-day average volume for three consecutive days, automatic trim to below 3% AUM, regardless of thesis." No instruction. No judgment call. A do-not list. "Do not let illiquidity trap me."

This is subtle but important. Instructions require judgment. Constraints remove judgment. Bungay argues that removing judgment in advance — when you're calm — outperforms preserving judgment in the moment — when you're pressured.

And here's where it gets psychological. You know this intuitively. You know that humans are terrible at staying rational under pressure. You've lived it. 2022 drawdown. You held winners that were bleeding. You held losers waiting for recovery. The Chimp brain — as Steve Ward would say — was running the show. And the Chimp brain doesn't care about your thesis. It cares about pain and fear.

Bungay's answer isn't to make you smarter. It's to make you less able to override your constraints. Not because you're stupid, but because you're human. The Chimp brain is real. The endowment bias is real. The commitment bias is real. So you pre-commit.

Here's what a "do not" list looks like for your current portfolio:

- Do not hold any single position above 5% of AUM if that position is above 8% of the float
- Do not hold any position that has missed revenue guidance without a full KQ re-analysis within 48 hours
- Do not hold any position where the IR function has shifted materially (new IR hire, communication style change, messaging change) without explicit re-underwriting
- Do not override a cockroach signal with additional analysis
- Do not hold any position through a 25%+ drawdown without re-running the ACH matrix

These aren't instructions. They're not "consider whether..." They're not "think about whether to...". They're constraints. Bright lines. Do not cross. And the reason they work is that they're pre-commitments. You write them down when you're calm. You don't re-negotiate them when you're scared.

### LESSON 4: Back-Brief Everything — Especially Your Own Thesis (~640 words)

Bungay's back-brief concept is simple. Tell me what you heard me say. Before you execute. And if I hear something different from what I meant, we resolve it before you act.

You use this with Watson on research tasks. You should use this with yourself on thesis monitoring.

Corbion. Your thesis was "durable business, inflation-proof margin profile, management team clearly communicating no drama." Companies say "no drama" to start with. Then they start to point out the issues. Small issues first. Then bigger issues. Slowly the narrative shifts. But if you'd back-briefed — written the thesis, re-read the latest management commentary against it, asked "does this confirm or contradict?" — the narrative shift would have been obvious. You'd have seen it in real time, not in retrospect.

The back-brief forces clarity. It forces the question: does the world I see now match the world my thesis assumed?

Fasadgruppen. Your thesis was "execution-driven consolidation play, quality team, Swedish construction market tailwind." The liquidity constraint was assumed but not written. "Liquidity is sufficient to exit even if thesis fails." But you didn't back-brief the position sizing. You didn't write "I'm holding 11% of AUM in a stock that's 15% of float." You didn't read that against your constraint. So by the time you realized the liquidity trap, you were locked in.

A back-brief would have been: Position in FG: 11% of AUM, 15% of float. Current float: £150m. My position: £16.5m. If thesis fails, I need to exit in a 10-day period to minimize market impact. My 10-day average volume: £3.2m. So I'm looking at a 5-day exit minimum. Current bid-ask spread: 2.5%. So I'm leaving £412k on the table in spread alone. Is that acceptable? The thesis said "liquidity is not a concern." Back-brief says "liquidity is a massive concern." Thesis breached. Exit.

But you didn't do it. Because back-briefing was something you did with Watson on research tasks, not something you did with yourself on position management.

Here's what a structured back-brief looks like. One sentence thesis per position. Three events that would invalidate it. Check: has any event occurred? This week, for one stock. Pick DCC. Write the thesis. "DCC is a high-quality consolidator in fragmented supply-chain markets, with pricing power and cost synergies driving 15%+ ROIC." Invalidation events: (1) Major customer loss of >10% of revenue. (2) Cost-inflation impact on margin greater than expected in current guidance. (3) CEO or CFO departure. Check the last three months of filings, commentary, news. Has any of these occurred? No? Then thesis intact, position confirmed. Hold. Yes? Then thesis breached, and we escalate. Simple.

Do this for one stock per day. Five days. Five positions. You'll find that half of them have events you didn't consciously register. Not because you're inattentive. Because you're busy. And busy investors don't back-brief. They hope that the thesis is still intact and they get surprised when it breaks.

Bungay's argument here is that execution isn't about intelligence. It's about ritual. It's about the discipline of writing thesis, reading news, confirming or escalating. It takes forty minutes per position per week. And it saves the kind of hundred-thirty-hour XVIVO spirals that come when signals weren't registered.

### LESSON 5: The Executive's Trinity — Directing, Managing, Leading (~640 words)

Bungay spends the second half of "The Art of Action" on a framework that's been lurking in the background of everything we've discussed. In any complex system — an organization, a portfolio, a team — there are three functions. Directing, Managing, and Leading. They're different. They require different skills. And if you collapse them, you fail.

**Directing** is setting intent and constraints. "This is our objective. Here's the boundary. Go." For your portfolio, directing is writing the thesis, setting the cockroach rule, setting the exit clock. It's the act of clarity.

**Managing** is monitoring execution against the intent. "Are we staying within constraint? Is the signal still clear? Do we need to adjust?" Managing is checking whether the thesis has been breached. It's escalating when the signal hits.

**Leading** is the emotional/psychological function. It's the capacity to execute the constraint even when fear or hope is pushing the other direction. Leading is exiting when the ACH threshold is breached even though the stock is down 10% and the Chimp brain is saying "wait." Leading is the part where you overcome yourself.

Let me illustrate with the stocks that cost you the most.

BFF. Your directing was fine. "Exit on cockroach signal." Clear. Your managing was fine. You detected the signal. "IR weird. Bad hiring. Capital allocation risk." Clear. But your leading failed. You couldn't execute under emotional pressure. You held the position past the constraint because the Chimp brain was running threat-avoidance. The loss felt too real. Waiting felt safer. This is a leading failure disguised as a managing failure.

XVIVO. Your directing was incomplete. "Thesis is execution not potential." Yes, but unclear exit rule. "What does execution failure look like? How many hours before you decide?" Those constraints weren't set. So when managing detected signals (execution slipping), there was no pre-commitment directing to follow. Managing took over. And managing spiralled into a hundred-thirty-hour rabbit hole. This is a directing failure that escalated into a managing death spiral.

Instalco. Your directing was sound. "Liquidity matters. Position size constrained by float." Clear. Your managing was sound. "Liquidity has declined. Position is illiquid." Detected. But your leading failed. You couldn't execute the trim because the trim meant realizing a loss, and the Chimp brain was saying "wait, it'll recover." You held through the worst of it before exiting. This is another leading failure.

And here's where Bungay's framework reveals something subtle. Your portfolio isn't failing because you lack intelligence or skill at analysis. You have track record: 8.3% CAGR, 96 stocks, 11.5 years. Your directing and managing are strong. Your failure is structural on the leading dimension. You can set constraints. You can detect signals. You can't always execute when the Chimp brain is loud.

This is where HPC coaching comes in. Bungay leaves this gap open. His framework is about directing and managing. The emotional layer — the leading function — that's Steve Ward territory. That's the 4Cs. That's thought management and recovery protocol and self-image work.

Here's the resolution. Bungay's structural fixes plus HPC's emotional fixes together close the knowing-doing gap. You use constraints (Bungay) to pre-commit when you're calm. You use coaching (Steve Ward) to execute the constraint when you're pressured. They're complementary. Bungay gives you the architecture. HPC gives you the psychology to follow it.

## THE PRODUCTIVE TENSION (~400 words)

Bungay challenges you directly on one point. Your instinct, under uncertainty, is to add structure. Another framework. Another layer of analysis. Another dimension to the ESA checklist. This is natural. The more uncertain you feel, the more you want to control through knowledge.

But Bungay says this is backwards. More structure doesn't help when you're already structured. More knowledge doesn't help when you're already analytically skilled. What helps is simplicity. What helps is constraint. What helps is ritual.

Your SOP density is high. Your analytical frameworks are deep. Your systems are comprehensive. But they're instructions. They're designed to tell you what to do. And instructions fail under pressure because they require judgment, and judgment fails.

The resolution isn't to add more analysis. It's to simplify. The checklists should be "Do Not" lists. When you find yourself adding a dimension to ESA, ask: is this helping me avoid mistakes? Or is it giving me permission to wait? Your cockroach rule is already there. Your one-page rule is already there. Your liquidity constraint is already there. You don't need new rules. You need to make the existing rules structural.

Here's the tension Bungay creates. In uncertain environments, the sophistication that got you here — the ability to hold complexity, to synthesize many dimensions, to think deeply — that sophistication is now your weakness. It gives you permission to wait. It gives you optionality. It gives the Chimp brain room to maneuver. And optionality, under pressure, becomes rationalization.

The managers who outperform in uncertain times aren't smarter. They're simpler. They set constraints. They run ritual. They check. They execute. No analysis override.

Bungay's frame is military. In wartime, the commander who waits for perfect intelligence loses. The commander who acts on sufficient intelligence, with clear constraints, wins. Your portfolio is wartime, not peacetime. Iran conflict, March 2026. Macro uncertainty. Risk-off. This is exactly when constraint, not analysis, wins. This is exactly when simplicity beats sophistication.

## SINGLE ACTIONABLE TAKEAWAY (~300 words)

This week, one protocol. One stock per day, five days.

Pick your current active holdings: DCC, CARLB, CRBN, FEVR, FLTR, XVIVO, CVSG, HTWS. Pick one.

**Step one:** Write the thesis in one sentence. Not three sentences. One. If you can't do it, the thesis isn't clear enough. Go back and clarify.

**Step two:** Identify three events that would invalidate the thesis. These are your cockroach signals. Not "market declines." Not "multiple compression." Specific, business-level events. Revenue miss. Customer loss. Management change. Margin deterioration. Pick three.

**Step three:** Check the last three months of filings, commentary, news against those three events. Use the OODA loop: Observe (has any event occurred?) → Orient (does it match my invalidation criteria?) → Decide (thesis still intact?) → Act (hold or escalate?).

**Step four:** Write your answer. Thesis intact or breached? If intact, confirm and move on. If breached, escalate immediately — full KQ memo, back-brief, decision within 48 hours.

Five days. One stock per day. This isn't more analysis. This is ritual. This is the discipline that Bungay says outperforms complexity. You already know how to do this. You know the rules. What's missing is the structure that makes it automatic.

Do this for one week. Then we build the next layer: making the escalation protocol automatic. Making the cockroach rule structural. Making "ACH threshold = exit" non-negotiable.
