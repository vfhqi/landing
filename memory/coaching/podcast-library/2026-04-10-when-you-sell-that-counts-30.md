# Podcast: "It's When You Sell That Counts" — Cassidy's Framework Applied

[INTRO MUSIC FADES]

Welcome back. I'm Watson, Richard's AI coach. And today we're tackling one of the most uncomfortable truths in professional investing: the sale is often harder than the purchase. Most managers spend 80% of their energy on entry—research, thesis development, building conviction. And maybe 5% on the exit. That imbalance is catastrophic.

Today's episode is built on Donald Cassidy's masterwork on selling: the recognition that buying is exciting, psychologically reinforcing, feels like you're making progress. Selling, by contrast, feels like failure. Your thesis was wrong, or the market moved faster than you thought, or the business deteriorated. None of those feel good. So we avoid them. We rationalize. We hope the story comes back. And meanwhile, the clock is ticking on our capital.

What makes this particularly relevant for a solo, concentrated portfolio manager like Richard—who runs 5-15 positions at a time with no team pressure to rebalance—is that there's no automatic forcing function. Nobody will override your decision. You have to police yourself. And that requires a framework.

Cassidy gives us one. And Richard's 96-stock track record gives us the case studies to prove it works.

## The Psychological Asymmetry: Why Selling Feels Wrong When It's Right

Let's start with the hardest part: the psychology. Cassidy identifies something called the disposition effect. It's hardwired into us by prospect theory. We are more sensitive to loss than gain. Twice as sensitive, roughly. Which means that when a stock is down, the psychological pain of realizing that loss is enormous. The stock that's up 20%? We want to sell it, lock in the gain, feel the dopamine hit. The stock that's down? We're desperate to hold it and get back to breakeven.

This is backwards. This is exactly wrong.

Richard learned this in spectacular fashion in January 2022, when his portfolio was down 54% from peak by July. His holdings included Kainos, S4 Capital, Medartis, Avanza, and Xvivo—five positions that each represented 12-18% of the portfolio. Combined, they were 67% in growth names. And when rates rose and multiples compressed, they got hammered. Kainos alone was down 18% in a straight line by mid-January with no news on Bloomberg. Pure multiple compression.

The question Richard had to ask himself—and didn't, or didn't ask it clearly enough—was: Would I buy these today at these prices? That's Cassidy's core test. Not "are they down from where I bought them?" Not "might they come back?" Simply: In today's world, given today's rates, today's growth prospects, today's management changes, would I deploy fresh capital into this name?

The answer, for most of those positions in January 2022, was no. But the positions stayed. Because admitting that is psychologically harder than holding and hoping.

## Three Types of Sales: Each One Has Its Own Psychological Tax

Cassidy divides selling into three categories. And each one triggers a different emotional response.

**First: Selling losers. This is the hardest.**

This is what he means by the disposition effect. You bought Kainos because the hiring indicator looked positive. You built a thesis around sustainable 40-50% growth. You sized it to 14% of your portfolio. Then the hiring indicator reversed. The CFO gave muted commentary. The stock was down 18% in January. You had a choice: sit with the loss, or realize it.

Cassidy's insight: "Every day you hold a loser, you're making a new buy decision at today's price."

Richard learned this painfully with Kainos. He held it for 44 months. He had an early signal—the hiring indicator reversal in 2024—but his team blamed the indicator instead of updating the thesis. Quote: "We assumed low-balling. Then indicator went negative and we blamed the indicator instead of updating thesis."

That's the disposition effect in action. You don't want to admit the indicator is telling you something real. You don't want to face the loss. So you rationalize.

But Cassidy's test is clean: Would I buy this today? At a time when a hiring indicator I built to forecast revenue inflection has reversed, and the CFO is guiding conservatively, and growth is slowing? No. I would not buy this today. So I should not hold it today.

**Second: Selling winners. This is the counterintuitive kind.**

Richard had this problem with Greggs in 2021. Stock was up, performing well, had optionality. His team member Pedro was nervous. Richard deferred to the nervousness and sold. Six months later, the stock was up another 25%. He'd exited too early.

The lesson Richard writes: "I am the decision maker. I've paid for this lesson in hard P&L."

But here's the subtlety Cassidy brings: selling a winner isn't wrong when the thesis has changed. It's only wrong when the thesis is still valid but you're exiting because of price. The Greggs exit was the latter. The thesis (quality, predictable earnings, modest multiple) was still intact. Richard sold because of team nervousness, which is Cassidy's "Raider behavior"—selling because of someone else's fear.

But Richard had a different experience with Ocado. He exited after a 100% move. He later reflected: "Maybe we were partly lucky last year and did loads of work into Ocado but ultimately it went up 100% purely due to the COVID pull forward, and there wasn't much more science than that."

That's different. The thesis—compounder, margin expansion, recurring revenue—was correct in direction. But the move was exogenous. COVID pulled forward three years of demand in three months. The thesis didn't explain the return. Luck did. So acknowledging that and being cautious about holding further is intelligent self-awareness.

Avanza, by contrast, is the anti-Raider. Richard entered Avanza years ago, built the position through strength and weakness, held it for 72 months, and the answer to "would I buy today?" stayed YES for all six years. That position generated plus-170 basis points. It's still held. When the test keeps passing, you hold.

**Third: Selling to switch. The opportunity cost frame.**

This is where Richard made a critical mistake in January 2022. He cut Basicfit from 12% to 8%, trying to reduce concentration. He was right to cut it. But where did the capital go? He redistributed it to S4, Xvivo, Kainos, and Medartis. He was selling a moderately healthy business to double down on fragile ones.

Cassidy's framework here is clear: Selling to switch is only intelligent if you're switching into something better. Not just "more concentrated," but demonstrably better risk/reward. Richard's move was backwards. He said to himself, "I need to diversify," then added to the worst-positioned bets.

The right move would have been: Cut Basicfit, use proceeds to increase Corbion (where he saw inflation messaging pattern and cut fast) or shift to higher-quality positions.

## The Six Sell Signals Cassidy Outlines: When to Act

Cassidy identifies six concrete sell signals. Not wishy-washy, not "feel-bad signals." Hard signals.

**First: Fundamental deterioration.** The business gets worse. Not the multiple, not sentiment. The business itself deteriorates.

This is what happened with S4 Capital in 2022. The flash trading update didn't move the price. That's the warning sign. Flash updates are supposed to move stocks when they contain news. When they don't, it means the street has priced something in that management hasn't disclosed. In S4's case, it was accounting issues. EPS cuts of 13% in three months. The business had fundamentally deteriorated.

Cassidy's signal: When flash updates don't move prices, something is breaking. Act.

**Second: Thesis violation.** Your original thesis is invalidated. Not merely challenged, invalidated.

Richard had this with Keywords. He had built a thesis around a search-marketing platform with defendable customer switching costs and pricing power. Then AI appeared. AI could potentially disrupt the entire premise. His team saw this coming—AI slowdown, market slowdown, risk of tool redundancy. Quote: "Team saw AI slowdown plus market slowdown but didn't shout. I didn't default to risk-off. Drowning in unnecessary detail and analysis."

The thesis was broken. The test "would I buy this today knowing that AI is shifting the SEM landscape?" is no longer hell-yes, it's maybe-at-a-discount. When the answer drops from hell-yeah to maybe, Cassidy says you exit.

**Third: Target achievement.** You've made your return. You're at the target that justified the risk.

This is underused as a sell signal. Richard doesn't have a great example here, but the principle is sound. You buy a stock at 30x earnings expecting it to re-rate to 20x and grow into the multiple. That's your thesis. If you hit your return target and the stock is now properly valued, you're done. Sell. Redeploy the capital. Management will chase ever-higher returns and blow it.

In practice, this signals discipline. The moment you've made your case return, the stock transitions from "concentrated bet" to "fair-valued hold." At that point, it makes sense to take profits and hunt for new opportunities with asymmetric risk/reward. Cassidy notes that many blowups happen after the initial thesis has been validated—managers try to squeeze another 50% from a stock that's already proven them right.

**Fourth: Better opportunity elsewhere.** The relative opportunity set has shifted. Your capital is better deployed elsewhere.

This is Cassidy's switching framework. It requires you to compare not to your entry price, but to the opportunity cost of holding. In April 2025, Richard was early on a position and identified a better opportunity in a cyclical compounder. The decision: continue compounding a good thing, or stop it and attack something with higher upside? Better opportunity wins.

**Fifth: Excessive valuation.** The stock is priced for perfection. The risk/reward has inverted.

Richard faced this with Fasadgruppen. Position got trapped because it had less than $2 million in average daily volume. He couldn't exit efficiently without market impact. By his own measure—a $2 million ADV minimum—this position violated his liquidity floor. He said: "We've snookered ourselves with liquidity in Fasadgruppen and Xvivo."

When liquidity dries up and you can't exit without significant slippage, the valuation isn't just expensive—it's a liability trap. Sell.

**Sixth: Management change.** The person who made the business run is gone.

This is where Richard's track record shows real learning. CEO changes that are lateral moves—board member stepping in to stabilize—can be fine. But CEO departures during crisis, or departures of key operational leaders, are yellow flags. Management quality red flags—bad hiring, no respect for great people, poor IR—compound quickly.

Richard notes of one position: "Bad hiring, no respect for great people, bad IR. Lots here." That's a six-signal event. Management fundamentally changed in ways that matter.

## The "Would I Buy This Today?" Test: Strips Away All the Noise

Cassidy gives us a clean tool to filter through all the psychological static. He calls it the "would I buy this today?" test. Not "would I have bought this two years ago?" Not "do I like this business structurally?" Would I, right now, in today's market, given today's rates and today's growth profile and today's management, deploy fresh capital?

The beauty of this test is it forces you to think on a clean slate. It strips anchoring bias. It strips sunk-cost bias. It makes you compare against today's opportunity set, not yesterday's confidence.

Richard used a version of this in 2023 when he rebuilt his portfolio post-drawdown. He classified every stock into a simple quadrant: high-conviction holds (hell-yeah), moderate-conviction holds (interested but watching), speculative holds (illiquid, no simple thesis, drowning in detail), and sells.

The positions that didn't pass "would I buy today?" got cut or right-sized. Xvivo was one. Quote: "Too much detail, no simple ACH invalidation guide. Learning all that stuff was trees not wood. Narrow frame distraction."

Xvivo's case was complex: three geographies, three regulatory regimes, three product lines, a CFO in the bottom quartile, and a kidney US error signaling execution risk. Would he buy that today, knowing the CFO is weak and the execution is shaky? No. It failed the test.

Burford was another test case. Richard ran the framework: "Would I buy this today knowing that the stock's OODA loop—opinion to decision to action loop—is faster than my analytical cycle? Knowing that SR litigation finance has zero competitive advantage despite optionality?" No.

## The Emotional Stages: Denial → Bargaining → Rationalisation → Capitulation

Cassidy describes an emotional arc that every position holder goes through. It maps to Kubler-Ross's grief stages, which is fitting because you are grieving a mistake.

**Denial:** The stock is down. This is temporary. It's a temporary setback. The thesis is still intact.

**Bargaining:** Okay, the stock is down more. But if I add at these levels, I'll dollar-cost-average into the better prices.

**Rationalisation:** The stock is still down but now I have extensive research and analysis showing that the underlying business is sound. I've spent 30 hours on this. I must understand something the market doesn't.

**Capitulation:** Okay, I was wrong. I'm out. (Often at the worst possible moment.)

Richard's XVIVO holding is a masterclass in this arc. He held it for five years despite complexity and execution risk. In Jan 2022, with a 3% position representing 10% of the company's shares, he couldn't exit without crater impact. That's the bargaining stage ("I'm trapped"). In 2023, he reanalyzed the position constantly, building models, trying to understand the CFO weakness and product errors. That's rationalisation. By 2026, he'd exited. Capitulation.

The emotional work in investing isn't to win the game—it's to exit the game when the board has changed.

## Pre-commitment: Set the Sell Criteria at Purchase, Not Under Stress

Here's Cassidy's non-negotiable rule: you set your sell criteria when you buy. Not when the stock is down 30%. Not when the thesis is violated and you're emotionally exhausted. You set them in advance.

The reason is simple: your thinking is clearest at entry. You haven't fallen in love with the position. You haven't built months of sunk-cost analysis. You're objective. You can ask: "What would make this wrong?" And you can write down the answers.

Richard does this imperfectly, but he's learned the value. On Corbion, he had built a pre-commitment to skepticism about inflation messaging. When companies say "no drama" initially, he'd learned from experience, they follow up with admissions of pain. Quote: "Companies often say 'no drama' to start with, then they start to point out the issues. So be sceptical."

That's a rule. Pre-committed. In January 2022, when Corbion's inflation messaging pattern appeared—"no drama" language followed by admissions—he cut fast. No analysis paralysis. The criteria were set. The signal appeared. He acted.

This is the difference between Cassidy-discipline and reactive decision-making. When the market panics and your position is down 30%, you're emotionally flooded. Your amygdala is screaming. Your analytical mind is foggy. You can't make good decisions under that condition. So you don't try. You follow the rules you set when your thinking was clear.

Richard now does this with trigger cards. Every position has pre-written sell triggers: "If hiring indicator goes negative, we have five trading days to exit." "If CFO quality signal emerges, automatic review." "If FCF turns negative despite revenue growth, thesis violation."

These are Cassidy's sell signals, written down in advance, waiting for the signal to fire. They're not suggestions. They're decision rules. When the rule fires, you execute. No rationalisation. No "let me think about this more." The thinking is done. You're just executing.

One additional benefit: trigger cards force you to be specific. You can't write "exit if things get bad." You have to define what "bad" means. That specificity is what makes the framework operational. It prevents analysis paralysis because you know in advance what bad looks like.

## The Cockroach Principle: When You See One Problem, Assume There Are More

Richard learned a critical lesson from BFF Holdings. The position looked structurally sound. Capital freed up, depobank closing, CEO buying. Then in January 2026, an IR call felt "diagnostic and was super weird." Richard's intuition flagged it. But he held.

Weeks later, the problems cascaded. Bad hiring. No respect for great people. Poor capital allocation history. Poor IR communication. One cockroach—the weird IR call—had revealed multiple cockroaches in the dark.

Cassidy doesn't name it this way, but it's implicit in his framework: when one signal breaks, assume others have broken too. They're just not visible yet. The CFO weakness might signal deeper issues. The hiring delay might indicate management chaos. The one product miss might indicate execution risk across the board.

This is why the "would I buy today?" test is so powerful. It forces you to think holistically. Yes, the stock is down and the multiple is attractive. But would I really buy knowing that the IR felt weird? That hiring is weak? That capital allocation has been poor? No. If I'm honest, no.

Hope is not a strategy. Cassidy is clear on this. When the cockroach appears, you act on the assumption that it's signaling other problems. You don't wait for the full infestation to reveal itself.

## Learning from 96 Stocks: Patterns Across a Full Track Record

What makes Richard's track record valuable here is the breadth. He's held 96 stocks over 11.5 years. He's experienced wins, losses, complexity traps, liquidity disasters, management failures, and thesis violations. Cassidy's framework isn't abstract. It's embedded in 7,160 basis points of actual P&L.

His best performer is Avanza at 170 basis points. The holding period: 72 months. The answer to "would I buy today?" stayed yes the entire time. That's not luck. That's a thesis that kept validating.

Think about that for a moment. 72 months is six years. Six years of quarterly earnings, multiple expansions, competitive dynamics shifting, management transitions. And the answer kept being yes. Not "it's okay." Not "it's holding up." Hell-yeah, I would buy this today.

That's what the filter does. It separates the true compounders—the positions where the thesis keeps getting stronger—from the positions where you're just holding because the loss is psychologically painful.

His worst performer is Fasadgruppen at minus-70 basis points. The lesson: "We've snookered ourselves with liquidity." Entry criteria violated at exit. He'd broken his own rule (minimum $2 million ADV). The position became a trap. When you can't exit without market impact, you're not holding a position anymore—you're managing a liability.

This is a crucial Cassidy insight: You can make a thesis mistake. You can enter a position and realize six months in that you were wrong. That's painful but fixable. What's not fixable is getting trapped by your own risk parameters. Richard's rule was clear: minimum $2 million ADV. Fasadgruppen violated that. By staying, he turned a bad position into a liquidity prison.

The two positions that tested him most were XVIVO and Medartis. Quote on Medartis: "A bunch of sexy sounding ideas that are thrown at a wall can catch a bid. But it's a poor risk/reward as an investment."

Medartis had CEO risk, NSI deal deterioration, lower extremities expansion failure. Multiple changes happening simultaneously. Thesis violation. But the position lived because analysis kept substituting for decision-making. If Richard had asked the clean question earlier—"Would I buy this today with all these moving parts and no simple invalidation rule?"—he would have exited sooner.

The pattern is consistent across all three of these challenging positions: Avanza (kept passing the test, held for 72 months, won big), Fasadgruppen (broke liquidity rules, trapped, lost 70 bps), Medartis (multiple changes, complexity explosion, analysis paralysis, lost 40 bps).

The exits that work are the ones that follow clear criteria. The exits that fail are the ones where you're hoping the next quarter will be different.

## The Real Cost: Opportunity Cost of Capital

One thing Cassidy emphasizes that Richard's track record really brings to life is opportunity cost. Every dollar held in a position that's deteriorating is a dollar not deployed in something with real upside.

In January 2022, Richard had roughly 40-45% of the portfolio in deteriorating positions. Not blown-up positions. Not obviously broken. Just deteriorating. S4 down 16%. Kainos down 18%. Medartis down 7%. None were obviously terminal. But the pattern was clear to anyone asking: "Would I buy this today?"

The cost: From January 2022 to July 2022 (when the portfolio reached max drawdown), these positions compounded losses. Meanwhile, other names—quality defensives, cyclical compounders, cash—were available for deployment at much better valuations. Corbion, which he cut fast, went on to stabilize and outperform.

Cassidy's lesson on this is stark: Holding a loser costs you twice. First, the direct loss. Second, the opportunity cost of not deploying that capital elsewhere. The second cost is often much larger than the first.

Richard's 2022 experience proves this. If he'd exited 20% of the "would I buy today?" failures and deployed to higher-conviction names, the 2022 P&L would have been dramatically different. Not positive—the year was a drawdown year regardless—but less damaging.

## The Integration: Cassidy in Richard's Operating System

Here's how Cassidy's framework integrates into Richard's practical system:

First, at entry: Set the sell criteria. Not loosely—specifically. "If hiring turns negative, we have one month to monitor, then we cut." "If CFO messaging turns negative on pricing power, automatic review." "If customer concentration hits 40%, reduction to 25%."

Second, in portfolio: Run the "would I buy today?" test quarterly. Not with the intention of selling everything, but with clear eyes. The positions that still pass are holds. The positions that don't are reviews.

Third, in monitoring: Watch for the six signals. Fundamental deterioration, thesis violation, target achievement, better opportunity, excessive valuation, management change. When one fires, don't wait for three. Act.

Fourth, in psychology: When you're tempted to rationalize, ask: "Am I in denial? Bargaining? Rationalisation?" Name the stage. It helps you exit it.

Fifth, in decision-making: Remember that every day you hold a loser, you're making a fresh buy decision at today's price. What would you choose today? And crucially: what else could you be buying?

## The Disposition Effect in Action: The 2022 Drawdown

To truly understand what Cassidy is saying, let's zoom into Richard's 2022 experience. In January, his portfolio was concentrated in exactly the positions most vulnerable to rate shock. And he faced the full force of the disposition effect.

The psychological trap works like this: You enter Medartis because NSI deal looks promising. You size it to 14% of AUM. It's down 7% in January. What do you do? Do you liquidate and realize the loss? Or do you average down, hoping for a reversion?

The disposition effect says: You'll hold. You'll likely average down. Because the pain of realizing the loss is psychologically overwhelming.

But here's the Cassidy framework working: If you ask "would I buy Medartis today at this price, given what I now know about NSI execution?" The answer shifts. NSI didn't work as expected. The lower extremities expansion was failing. The CEO wasn't proven. The answer is: "No, I would not buy this today."

When the answer to that question shifts from yes to no, the position should shift from hold to review. And reviews, when done ruthlessly, become exits.

Richard's lesson: He held most of these positions through 2022 and into early 2023. By mid-2023, he'd rebuilt his framework and right-sized. The cost: millions in P&L that could have been preserved if he'd applied the test earlier.

But the learning went in: Now, when rates move, when multiples compress, when CFOs guide down, Richard asks the clean question immediately. Not "will it come back?" But "would I buy this today?"

## The Actionable Takeaway

Here's what Cassidy distills it to. Here's what Richard's track record proves:

The entry is exciting. The thesis-building is stimulating. The position establishment is the moment you feel like you're making progress. But the return is made in the exit. The portfolio is built by those who can sell.

Most managers can't. They're attached to being right. They're anchored to their entry price. They're hoping that complexity will resolve into simplicity, and losses will revert to gains.

Cassidy's framework says: no. You set the criteria in advance. You ask the clean question—would I buy this today?—without emotional attachment. You recognize the patterns. You act.

And you remember: Hope is not a strategy.

The best investors aren't those with the best theses. They're those with the clearest criteria for when to exit.

In concentrated portfolios especially, this discipline becomes existential. With 5-15 positions, each one represents 6-20% of capital. A single position that stays too long—one Xvivo, one Medartis, one Kainos held through deterioration—can drag down a year of outperformance. The power of good exits is that they preserve optionality. They free capital to attack new opportunities. They prevent the slow bleed of capital into positions that have fundamentally changed.

Cassidy's framework isn't about perfectionism. It's about ruthlessness in execution. Set the criteria. Run the test. Act on the answer. That's the difference between a manager who sells and one who just holds.
