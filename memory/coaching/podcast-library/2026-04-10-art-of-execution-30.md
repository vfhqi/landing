If I asked you what determined your investment returns over the past decade, you'd probably say something about stock picking. You know, the quality of your research, the depth of your due diligence, the quality of the businesses you identified early. The typical story: great analyst finds great companies, buys them, holds them, gets rich.

Position management. Not stock selection. How you manage positions after you buy them determines whether you make money or lose money. Even if you're brilliant at picking stocks, you can get the position management catastrophically wrong and destroy returns. Conversely, if you're mediocre at stock picking but disciplined about position management, you can construct real wealth.

Freeman-Shor identified five distinct tribes — five patterns of how humans behave when they own a position that moves against them, or when they own a position that's winning. These tribes aren't personality types. They're behaviours. Habits. Ways of thinking about capital allocation under pressure.

The five tribes are: Rabbits, Assassins, Raiders, Connoisseurs, and Hunters. And here's what matters: your 96-stock track record — my track record — maps very clearly onto these five categories. We've made money with four of them. We've lost substantial money with one of them. Let me show you where you are in this framework, and more importantly, where you need to be.

## THE FIVE TRIBES FRAMEWORK (5 MIN)

Let's start with the architecture. Freeman-Shor built this on a simple observation: when you own a position and it starts to move against you, or when it's clearly a winner, humans have very predictable responses. These responses cluster into five distinct patterns.

**The Rabbit.** [PAUSE] A Rabbit freezes. A loser comes into the portfolio. The thesis breaks. The driver is gone. The Rabbit does nothing. Holds. Waits. Hopes. Maybe rationalises the position — "It's down so much it has to recover," or "The market doesn't understand this yet," or "We just need to wait for the right catalyst." The Rabbit is paralysed by loss aversion. He doesn't want to actualise the loss, so he doesn't act. Rabbits are the most destructive tribe because they turn tactical errors into strategic disasters.

**The Assassin.** [PAUSE] An Assassin cuts. Fast. The thesis breaks, the driver deteriorates, the warning lights flash — the Assassin accepts the loss and moves on. Clean exit. Regret-free. The Assassin has developed a process: thesis in, thesis out. Deterioration detected, position exited. It's not emotional for the Assassin. It's systemic.

**The Raider.** [PAUSE] A Raider takes profits too early. A stock comes into the portfolio. It runs. Up 20%, 30%, 40%. The Raider gets nervous. "We've made our money. Let's lock it in. Take the win." So the Raider sells. Half the position. Or the whole thing. What the Raider doesn't realise is that the real money in equities comes from the disproportionate tail — the 20% of positions that deliver 80% of returns. Raiders are leaving returns on the table.

**The Connoisseur.** [PAUSE] A Connoisseur adds to winners. But with discipline. Not just any winner — the Connoisseur adds to positions where the thesis is getting *stronger*, where the case is crystallising, where the quality is becoming clearer. The Connoisseur is willing to let winners run and willing to add size when conviction deepens. Connoisseurs are the most profitable tribe.

**The Hunter.** [PAUSE] A Hunter averages down. But with a clear plan. Not blind averaging. The Hunter has a thesis: this company is oversold, this is technical not fundamental, this is an opportunity to add size *because* the thesis is unchanged but the price is lower. Hunters are the riskiest tribe, but they can be extremely profitable if the thesis is sound and the Hunter has genuine conviction.

Here's what Freeman-Shor discovered: the best stock pickers in the world — people picking stocks at 65% accuracy — were still making money because they were Assassins or Connoisseurs on the losing positions, and Raiders on the winners. Position management more than offset bad stock selection.

Conversely, mediocre stock pickers who were Rabbits — holding losers forever — destroyed wealth no matter how many winners they picked.

The implication is stark. [PAUSE] Position management determines returns. Not stock selection.

## RABBIT CASE STUDIES — THE PARALYSIS PATTERN (8 MIN)

Let me show you what Rabbit behaviour looks like in practice. I've been a Rabbit. Multiple times. And it cost me real money.

**Kainos.** [PAUSE] Kainos was a 14% position at the start of 2022. A high-growth consulting services company. Irish. Beautiful business model, beautiful people. In January 2022, down 18% straight-line. No news. Just multiple compression — growth stocks getting hit by interest rate shock.

Now here's the interesting part. My thesis on Kainos was never "this is a flawless business." It was: "High growth, great culture, strong execution." At some point in early 2022, I noticed something. The hiring bottleneck was becoming a genuine constraint on growth. The CFO's commentary shifted. The tone changed. And I didn't act.

I was a Rabbit. I didn't sell. I didn't add. I just sat with the deterioration. I even built an indicator — a "hiring indicator" — to track whether hiring was accelerating or decelerating. The indicator went negative. And here's what I did: I blamed the indicator. I said, "The indicator doesn't work." I didn't update my thesis. I defended the position based on historical conviction, not current evidence.

That's Rabbit behaviour.

The journal entry from the time is stark: "CFO giving muted comments on hiring. We assumed low-balling. Then indicator went negative and we blamed the indicator instead of updating the thesis." [PAUSE] That's the Rabbit in pure form. Overconfidence in past analysis. Resistance to invalidation. Blame the signal, not the business.

Kainos ended up a -7 basis point position. 44 months held. Not a disaster, but dead money for nearly four years.

**S4 Capital.** [PAUSE] S4 was even worse. 14% of AUM at the start of 2022. Digital services, advertising, marketing. Acquired a bunch of small agencies and tried to build a roll-up. Down 16% in January alone. Down 35% from peak by the end of the month. 13% EPS cuts in the final three months of January alone.

And here's the key insight: flash trading update. Management provided a trading update mid-crisis. It moved the stock zero. That's not a red flag. That's a screaming siren. When the market ignores your management communication, something is very wrong.

I classified S4 in real time as "fragile." Not a flywheel. Dependent on M&A integration execution. Accounting issues underneath. I sat there and watched it crater. And I Rabbit'd it.

The real learning from S4 is portfolio-level. I had 67% of the portfolio in growth names at the start of 2022: S4 (14%), Kainos (14%), Medartis (14%), Avanza (13%), Xvivo (12%). All of them got hit. Not because they were bad companies. But because I had created a concentration trap. A Rabbit trap. Frozen. Couldn't exit easily due to liquidity. Couldn't add due to conviction loss.

That portfolio hit -54.75% max drawdown by October 2023.

**Fasadgruppen.** [PAUSE] Fasadgruppen is the Rabbit's worst nightmare. 11.47% peak position. I held it for 34 months. The return: -70 basis points. It lost 70 basis points. Not 70%. Seventy basis points. For nearly three years.

The core issue: liquidity trap. Thin stock. My position was meaningful relative to the trading volume. So when the thesis broke and I wanted to exit, I couldn't. Not without moving the price dramatically or accepting a truly punitive execution cost.

The real quote from my journal: "We've snookered ourselves with liquidity in Fasadgruppen and Xvivo." I was trapped. Rabbit'd in the truest sense: frozen not just by psychology but by logistics.

**Keywords.** [PAUSE] Keywords is perhaps the most instructive Rabbit case. 14.96% peak position. Held 25 months. Break-even exit. AI slowdown hit the search marketing platform. The market saw it. My team saw it. And I didn't act.

The journal quote cuts deep: "Team saw AI slowdown and market slowdown but didn't shout. I didn't default to risk-off. Drowning in unnecessary detail and analysis. If it's not 'hell yeah,' it's a no."

We had signal. Multiple signals. And we buried ourselves in analysis. More data. More models. More forecasts. Instead of acting on the signal, we treated the signal as a data point requiring further investigation. Analysis paralysis. Classic Rabbit.

The worst part: I explicitly state the process failure: "If it's not 'hell yeah,' it's a no. Keywords wasn't a 'hell yeah.' It was a 'maybe.' Maybe should have triggered automatic exit. It didn't."

So here's the Rabbit summary: Rabbits freeze. They rationalise. They blame indicators instead of updating thesis. They get trapped by liquidity. They drown in analysis instead of acting on signals. And the cost is that a 40% position hit becomes a 44-month dead-money holding.

The antidote is the Assassin.

## ASSASSIN & RAIDER CASES (6 MIN)

**Corbion and the Assassin move.** [PAUSE] Corbion was a biochemistry company. Great business. Organic materials, bioplastics, biochemistry. Real edge in the market. At some point in late 2021, inflation started showing up in costs.

Now, here's the subtlety. Management initially said everything was fine. "No drama. We're managing through." This is a pattern. I documented it: "Companies often say 'no drama' to start with, then they start to point out the issues."

I saw the pattern. I didn't wait for the issues to become undeniable. In January 2022, I cut Corbion to 8%. Clean. Decisive. Assassin move. The logic: inflation is showing up in costs, management is downplaying, this is going to get worse before it gets better, reduce the position.

Did I leave money on the table? Almost certainly. Corbion probably recovered. But I made the call based on deteriorating signals, not after maximum damage. That's Assassin discipline.

And here's what makes the Corbion Assassin move so instructive: I documented a pattern that applies far beyond one stock. "Companies often say 'no drama' to start with, then they start to point out the issues." That pattern — management reassurance followed by gradual acknowledgement — showed up in multiple positions across my track record. It's the inflation denier archetype. The company doesn't lie. But it sequences truth. First quarter: "We're managing through." Second quarter: "There's some pressure." Third quarter: "We're seeing significant headwinds." By the third quarter, the stock is down thirty percent and you're a Rabbit holding a broken thesis. The Assassin cuts at the first quarter signal. Not the third.

The Corbion decision also illustrates something Freeman-Shor emphasises: the cost of being wrong as an Assassin is small. I cut to 8%. If Corbion recovered immediately, I'd missed some upside on a reduced position. The cost of being wrong as a Rabbit is catastrophic. You hold the full position through all three quarters of deterioration. The asymmetry favours the Assassin every single time.

**Protector and the clean exit.** [PAUSE] Protector was a Norwegian insurer. Entry price: 40-50 NOK. March 2020, market panic. CEO resigned. I couldn't build conviction with the UK team. The business felt like a 6/10 quality company, not the 7/10 I thought at entry. Exit at 25 NOK. 40% loss.

The stock went on to 95 NOK. +280% foregone return.

On paper, disaster. But here's the journal reflection: "On the surface, very bad trading decision. But in that time, CEO resigned. Spoke to UK team and struggled to get conviction. I over-estimated the quality at investment date. So annoying to miss out on returns, but I feel fine with the logic."

That's Assassin discipline. The exit logic was sound given information at the time. I'd misjudged quality. The CEO resignation signalled deeper issues. Decision-making was defensible. Regret-free exit.

Assassins don't regret. Because the logic is right.

**Greggs: The Raider case.** [PAUSE] Greggs is the Raider in pure form. Bakery chain. I wanted to own it. My analyst was nervous. Wanted to defer. So I deferred. Took a 5.92% position. Held 19 months. Exit return: +6.6%.

But the stock went on to +25% YTD, then +15% more in November-December 2020. +40% total opportunity cost.

The lesson is brutal and I've documented it clearly: "I am the decision maker. Always form my own judgment on a stock. Never abrogate to the analyst for judgment. I've paid for this lesson in hard P&L."

What happened? I sized too small. And I exited too early. Not because the thesis broke. Because I let analyst nervousness override my conviction. Pure Raider behaviour. Take the tiny win and move on.

The psychological lesson: Analysts are advisors. Portfolio managers are decision-makers. If the PM cedes judgment to the analyst, the PM isn't doing the job. Greggs cost me 40% in opportunity cost because I sized like a Raider and exited like a Raider.

The Raider trap is subtler than the Rabbit trap because it feels like discipline. You took a profit. You managed risk. You were prudent. But prudence that systematically cuts your winners short is just as destructive as paralysis that holds your losers too long. The maths is merciless: if you cut every winner at ten percent and hold every loser to minus thirty, you need to be right seventy percent of the time just to break even. The Connoisseur who lets winners run to fifty or a hundred percent and cuts losers at ten percent can be wrong sixty percent of the time and still compound. Position management changes the required hit rate.

## CONNOISSEUR & HUNTER CASES (6 MIN)

**Avanza: The long-term compounder.** [PAUSE] Avanza is the Connoisseur case. Nordic fintech. Trading platform. Recurring revenue. Held for 72 months. Return: +170 basis points. Top winner.

The Connoisseur approach: multiple entries over 6+ years. Scaled through weakness. Resistant to 2022-23 drawdown. When the thesis got stronger — when the business model crystallised, when recurring revenue became clearer — I added size.

Avanza started 2022 at 13% of AUM. Down 9% in January despite earnings upgrades of +6%. Peers were down more. Flatex down 8%. Nordnet down 10%. Avanza relative strength suggested the quality of the business was holding up even in a rate shock environment.

Did I panic? No. Did I add? No. I held. Let it work.

The Connoisseur discipline: you don't add to every winner. You add when the thesis is strengthening, when conviction is deepening, when you're learning new things that make the case better.

But here's the subtlety: I made a mistake with Avanza in 2026. CEO call was vague. Because I was vague on the numbers. The journal quote is direct: "Not my Avanza with CEO meeting — vague, because I was vague on the numbers. Lesson: Keep IR calls very financial-centric. Must have a model ready (at minimum sell-side)."

Process learning: Connoisseurs do homework. You can't scale into positions unless you understand the financials deeply. Vague IR calls mean you're winging it. Connoisseurs don't wing it.

**Entain and GVC: The Hunter thesis.** [PAUSE] Entain and GVC are Hunter cases. Entain: March 2020, market panic. Oversold. Not because of fundamentals. Because the whole market was crashing.

The journal insight is key: "When there is a market-level sell-off, do not exit stocks because of mediocre quality. A lesson from March 2020 from Protector and Entain. They were oversold. The stock will recover due to technical pressure, not my fundamental concerns. Baby and bathwater."

The Hunter sees the panic. Recognises that the thesis is unchanged. Sees the price is lower. Opportunity to add.

GVC Holdings: gaming consolidation play. Entry during multiple compression. Consolidator with pricing power. Held 33 months. +140 basis points. The Hunter case: identified genuine consolidation opportunity, added into weakness, let the cycle play out.

The Hunter is riskiest. Because the Hunter is betting that the thesis is right and the market is wrong about price. If the thesis is right, you make disproportionate returns. If the thesis is wrong, you lose disproportionately.

But here's the discipline: the Hunter doesn't average down blindly. The Hunter has a thesis. The thesis has a proof point. When that proof point is tested and holds, the Hunter adds.

**Instalco: The emotional overreaction.** [PAUSE] Instalco is the case that doesn't fit neatly into any single tribe — and that's precisely why it's instructive. In July 2021, a broker note flagged raw materials inflation in Nordic installation services. My analyst was nervous. I was nervous. So I trimmed four percent of the position.

The stock went up thirty percent the following month.

The journal entry is brutal in its honesty: "Panicky trim. Team member nervous, I was nervous. Broker note on raw materials. Sold into strength based on anxiety, not analysis." That's not Rabbit behaviour — I acted. It's not Assassin behaviour — the thesis hadn't broken. It's something worse: impulsive action driven by fear. The mirror image of the Rabbit's paralysis.

Freeman-Shor doesn't name this tribe explicitly, but it sits between Raider and something else entirely. Call it the Reactive. The Reactive doesn't freeze like a Rabbit, doesn't calculate like an Assassin, doesn't wait for crystallisation like a Connoisseur. The Reactive just moves — fast, emotionally, without reference to the system. The cost in Instalco wasn't the four percent trim. It was the thirty percent foregone return that followed, plus the psychological damage of watching a position you sold into strength climb relentlessly.

The antidote? Pre-committed decision rules. If I'd had a rule — "no position change unless thesis drivers have changed, regardless of broker commentary" — I'd have held. The broker note was noise. My thesis was about long-term Nordic building services consolidation. Raw materials inflation in a single quarter doesn't touch that thesis. But fear doesn't respect thesis logic. Fear respects rules.

This connects directly to Freeman-Shor's core insight: the Connoisseur and the Assassin both have systems. The Rabbit and the Reactive don't. The quality of the system is the quality of the position management.

## THE PORTFOLIO-LEVEL RABBIT TRAP (4 MIN)

[PAUSE]

I want to zoom out now. Because here's what's fascinating: not only can individual positions be Rabbits, entire portfolios can be Rabbits.

January 2022. I had 67% of the portfolio in growth names: S4 (14%), Kainos (14%), Medartis (14%), Avanza (13%), Xvivo (12%). None of these were broken theses in isolation. But collectively, I had created a Rabbit portfolio. Frozen. Unable to exit due to liquidity. Unable to add due to conviction loss. Unable to rebalance due to psychology.

When rates started rising and multiple compression hit, I wasn't diversified across position management styles. I was concentrated in Rabbit positions — illiquid, complex, high-multiple growth stocks that couldn't be exited without material cost.

The result: -54.75% max drawdown by October 2023.

The learning forced me to reconstruct the portfolio with explicit position management discipline. I now target what I call "progressive exposure": 3% initial position. Proof point at month 4-6. If thesis holds, scale to 7%. If thesis strengthens further, scale to 11%. If thesis crystallises, scale to 15%.

This forces Connoisseur and Assassin discipline. You're always making the case stronger before you're increasing size. And you're always ready to exit because the position is sized small relative to conviction.

It also prevents the Rabbit trap. Because at 3%, you can exit without material cost. The position is sized small. The exits are clean. The capital is available to redeploy.

## THE EXECUTION LESSON (3 MIN)

[PAUSE]

Here's what Freeman-Shor discovered that so few investors internalise: position management, not stock selection, determines returns.

I've picked stocks at maybe 55% accuracy. That's not great. That's slightly above chance.

But I've been disciplined about position management. I've been an Assassin on losing positions. I've been a Connoisseur on winning positions. I've been a Hunter when genuine oversold opportunities appear.

The result: 8.3% CAGR over 10 years. 43% losing months. But positive 18-month rolling forward returns over most periods.

If I'd been a Rabbit — holding losers hoping for recovery — I would have been underwater. The track record proves it: my losing positions averaged minus twenty-two basis points. My winning positions averaged plus forty-one basis points. That asymmetry — losers small, winners large — is entirely a function of position management. I didn't pick winners twice as well as losers. I managed winners twice as well as losers. The Assassin kept losing positions short. The Connoisseur let winning positions run. That two-to-one ratio is the entire edge.

Freeman-Shor would recognise this immediately. The hit rate doesn't matter as much as the payoff ratio. And the payoff ratio is determined by how you manage positions, not how you select them.

The execution is the discipline. It's not the stock pick. It's not the research quality. It's the decision you make when the thesis breaks, when the position moves against you, when you need to rebalance.

Here's the actionable version: [PAUSE]

Develop a pre-mortem process. Before you buy a position, identify the conditions under which you exit. Not vaguely. Specifically. If the hiring indicator reverses, you're out. If the flash trading update moves nothing, you're out. If the CFO tone shifts from confident to cautious, you're out.

Write it down. Put it in your system. Because when the position is down 20%, you won't think clearly. Your lizard brain will tell you to hold. The pre-mortem reminds you: this is the condition we said would trigger exit.

Second: implement the 30-day shot clock. If a driver deteriorates, you have 30 days to either prove the driver is recoverable or exit the position. No Rabbit positions allowed past 30 days.

Third: size for optionality. Start positions small. Add only when the thesis strengthens. This forces Connoisseur discipline and prevents the Rabbit trap. A 3% position can be exited cleanly. A 15% position creates psychological baggage.

And fourth: distinguish between technical oversold and fundamental deterioration. Market panics create opportunities. The skill is knowing which is which. The Hunter recognises the panic. The Rabbit gets trapped.

Freeman-Shor proved it with decades of data. Position management. Not stock selection. That's where the returns come from.

## CLOSING (1 MIN)

[PAUSE]

So where are you? Are you a Rabbit? Hoping for recovery? Blaming indicators instead of updating thesis? Get ruthless. Cut the position. 30-day shot clock. Your capital is better deployed.

Are you a Raider? Taking profits too early? Afraid of being wrong? Hold discipline. Let winners run. The real money comes from the tail positions.

Are you a Connoisseur? Strong thesis on the upside. Adding selectively when conviction deepens. That's where wealth is built.

Or are you a Hunter? Recognising true oversold opportunities. Adding with genuine conviction when price is disconnected from value. That takes guts. But it can be extremely profitable.

The one thing you can't be is a Rabbit. Rabbits lose.

And here's the deeper lesson from Freeman-Shor that connects everything: you don't need to be one tribe all the time. The best investors are Assassins on their losers, Connoisseurs on their winners, and Hunters when genuine panic creates opportunity. The progressive exposure system — 3% entry, scaling to 7%, 11%, 15% as thesis crystallises — is exactly this multi-tribe discipline made structural. You're an Assassin at 3% because the exit cost is trivial. You become a Connoisseur as conviction deepens. And when the market panics and gives you a name you know at a price you'd never dream of, you're a Hunter.

The question isn't "which tribe am I?" It's "which tribe does this specific position need me to be right now?" And the answer changes. Avanza needed Connoisseur patience for six years. Corbion needed Assassin ruthlessness in three months. Entain needed Hunter courage in three weeks. The skill is recognising which mode the position demands and switching without ego.

That's the art of execution. Not stock selection. Execution. Ninety-six stocks, eleven and a half years, and the lesson is the same every single time: how you manage the position matters more than which position you pick.
