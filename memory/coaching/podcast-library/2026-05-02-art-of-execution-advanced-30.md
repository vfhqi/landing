**Part One: The uncomfortable arithmetic of the Connoisseur tribe**

[normal pace]

Freeman-Shor studied forty-five elite fund managers over a ten-year period, across 1,866 investments and more than 30,000 individual trades. His headline finding — that position management matters more than stock selection — gets most of the attention. But there is a secondary finding in his data that deserves significantly more attention than it receives.

Of the investors who belonged to the Connoisseur tribe — those who added to winning positions and produced multi-hundred-percent gains from their best ideas — the hit rate on individual stock selections was actually lower than the Assassin tribe. The Connoisseurs were right less often than the disciplined cutters.

[beat]

Think about what this implies. The investors who produced the largest absolute returns had a lower win rate on individual ideas. They won not because they were right more often, but because when they were right, they were right very big. And when they were wrong, they cut quickly, accepting that the miss was a small tax on the process, not an indictment of the system.

This directly challenges a belief that most excellent fundamental investors hold implicitly: that better analysis produces a higher hit rate, and that a higher hit rate is the primary driver of performance. Freeman-Shor's data says: not necessarily. The hit rate is secondary to the asymmetry ratio — how much you make on your wins relative to how much you lose on your losses.

[with emphasis]

Van Tharp's expectancy formula makes this precise. Expectancy equals (probability of win × average win R-multiple) minus (probability of loss × average loss R-multiple). If your win R-multiple is large enough — if your Connoisseur positions compound to five or ten R — you can have a sub-fifty-percent hit rate and still produce exceptional returns. The destruction of that expectancy happens not when hit rates decline, but when you cap your wins (Raider behaviour: selling too early) and allow your losses to run (Rabbit behaviour: holding losers too long). Both of these together invert the expectancy formula.

For your strategy: Avanza is the primary exhibit. Seventy-two months, 170 basis points. If we model that as a win, the R-multiple on that position was very large — probably five to ten R on the initial capital deployed. That single position's contribution to your overall expectancy is enormous. It compensates for a significant number of losses. The Connoisseur behaviour on Avanza — the patient, conviction-based, non-reactive hold through multiple pullbacks — was the primary source of that R-multiple.

Now ask: how many Avanza-scale R-multiples have been cut prematurely over the same period? How many Raider exits — good stocks sold at twenty to thirty percent gains when the full potential was one hundred to two hundred percent — occurred in positions that had the same structural characteristics as Avanza at an early stage? The gap between your actual return and your potential return lives in those truncated Connoisseur positions that were managed as Raiders.

**Part Two: The tribe-shift mechanism — why investors who know the framework still get trapped**

[slightly slower]

Freeman-Shor documents that most investors are not consistently in one tribe. They shift between tribes across positions, and even within a single position over time. An investor might enter a position as a potential Connoisseur — conviction sizing, patient hold plan — and gradually become a Rabbit as the position deteriorates, then ultimately exit as a Raider when the position partially recovers and they decide to "take something off the table."

This is the most common failure pattern: Connoisseur intent, Rabbit execution, Raider exit. Three tribes, one position. All three wrong in sequence.

[PAUSE]

The mechanism that drives these shifts is not ignorance of the framework. It is emotional state contamination across the position lifecycle. Douglas describes this from the psychology side. Steenbarger describes it from the performance side. But Freeman-Shor's data makes it visible in the returns data: the tribe-shifting produces a specific, identifiable P&L signature. Returns that should be large and positive are instead modest and positive. Returns that should be modest and negative are instead large and negative.

Here is the contamination sequence. You enter with Connoisseur intent. The position advances, confirming your thesis. You feel validated — perhaps more validated than the position's actual progress warrants. At this point, the Raider instinct activates: you have made money, you do not want to give it back, there is a voice that says "take something off the table." If you listen to this voice, you exit some of the position at a price that will later look premature.

Then the position pulls back — a normal, healthy Stage 2 pullback, the kind that Minervini says is a buying opportunity, not a selling signal. But you have already taken profits. You are now holding a reduced position at a higher average cost, and the pullback produces a gain that has been compressed by the partial Raider exit. The emotional state shifts: you feel slightly frustrated. The conviction is diluted.

If the position then begins to show genuine deterioration — stage 3 signals, fundamental cracks, cockroach emergence — you are now emotionally vulnerable to Rabbit behaviour. The frustration from the compressed win makes the current loss more painful than it would have been if you had managed it cleanly. You hesitate. The Chimp says, "Wait for the bounce" — not because the thesis is intact but because you need the position to recover to above your average cost before you can psychologically accept the exit.

[with emphasis]

This is the tribe sequence that explains the majority of P&L leakage in concentrated portfolios. And it is driven not by analytical failure but by emotional sequence dependency: each phase of the position lifecycle creates an emotional state that conditions the next phase's decisions.

The Instalco sequence shows elements of this. The trim at a good price (Raider instinct on a healthy pullback) was followed by a thirty percent advance — which means you watched the trimmed portion generate returns you did not capture. The emotional response to that — whatever frustration or FOFR it created — is exactly the kind of contamination that makes subsequent decisions on that stock, or on similar stocks, slightly less clean.

Steenbarger calls this "cumulative emotional drag." It is not visible in any individual decision. It shows up in a slight systematic bias over many decisions — a fraction too early on exits, a fraction too cautious on additions, a fraction too slow on cuts. Each fraction is defensible in isolation. Together, they represent a meaningful performance drag.

**Part Three: The Connoisseur trap — when the best tribe becomes the worst decision**

[normal pace]

Freeman-Shor celebrates the Connoisseur tribe, and rightly so. But there is a Connoisseur trap that he does not fully address — the situation where Connoisseur behaviour is being applied to the wrong archetype of stock.

The Connoisseur approach — conviction sizing, adding on confirmation, patient long-term hold — is optimal for quality compounders with durable competitive advantages and compounding value creation. It is the right approach for Avanza. It is arguably the right approach for your best holdings in the cables theme, for CARLB if the thesis crystallises, for positions where the long-term value creation is structural and not dependent on a single catalyst playing out perfectly.

[beat]

It is the wrong approach for fragile businesses (archetype 11), cockroach stocks (archetype 6), and complexity-beyond-competence cases (archetype 4). Applying Connoisseur behaviour to these archetypes produces your worst outcomes. Because the Connoisseur's defining characteristic — patient acceptance of drawdowns, confidence that the stock will be higher in eighteen to thirty-six months — is not a behavioural problem in quality compounders. It is a catastrophic error in fragile businesses that are deteriorating structurally.

XVIVO was receiving Connoisseur treatment for months — patient hold, deep analysis, continued confidence in the long-term thesis — when the correct treatment was Assassin behaviour. The fragile business archetype requires a completely different psychological stance than the quality compounder archetype. And the danger is that your natural inclination — what Bassham would call your self-image as an investor — is oriented toward the Connoisseur stance. "I am a patient, conviction-based investor who holds quality businesses through volatility." This is an accurate description of your best investments. It is a dangerous description when applied indiscriminately.

[PAUSE]

The advanced execution discipline is not knowing which tribe to belong to. It is knowing which tribe is appropriate for which archetype of position, and shifting tribe dynamically as the position's archetype classification changes.

A quality compounder that begins to show cockroach signals is no longer a quality compounder. It is a cockroach stock masquerading as a quality compounder. The archetype has shifted. And when the archetype shifts, the tribe should shift with it. From Connoisseur to Assassin. Not gradually. Immediately.

Freeman-Shor's data implies this even though he does not state it explicitly. His Assassin tribe — the disciplined cutters — are not cold, emotionless machines. They are investors who have made the archetype identification quickly and acted on it. The speed of the archetype identification is the skill, not the mechanical exit itself. The exit is just the execution of a classification decision.

Nassim Taleb's barbell principle applies here in an unexpected way. Taleb argues for extreme positions: either very safe or very aggressive, with nothing in the middle. The middle is where you absorb risk without being compensated for it. Applied to Freeman-Shor's tribes: either hold with full Connoisseur conviction (quality compounder, durable moat, structural compounder) or exit with full Assassin precision (archetype change, cockroach signal, stage deterioration). The Rabbit territory — the middle zone of "holding but not convinced, monitoring but not deciding" — is the barbell's most dangerous position. Exposure to loss without the conviction that produces the compensating gain.

**Part Four: The Hunter tribe — the advanced application most investors miss**

[slightly slower]

Freeman-Shor describes the Hunter as the investor who adds to a losing position with a plan. Not averaging down mindlessly — the Rabbit's accidental doppelganger — but deliberately adding to a quality business that has been punished by market overreaction, with a specific thesis for recovery, a specific timeframe, and a specific kill criterion if the thesis fails to materialise.

The distinction between Hunter and Rabbit is not position direction. It is planning quality. The Rabbit holds a loser without a plan because selling would mean admitting a mistake. The Hunter adds to a loser with a plan because market overreaction has created a better entry point for a thesis that remains intact.

[with emphasis]

For your strategy — GARP plus event-driven, long-term horizon, focus on quality businesses at inflection points — the Hunter tribe should be more accessible to you than it is in practice. Your "oversold high-quality compounder" investment profile is literally the Hunter opportunity: a quality business that the market has punished beyond the fundamental reality, where your edge is seeing through the overreaction.

Entain in March 2020 was Hunter territory. GVC at the consolidation phase was Hunter territory. Cable and Wireless at plus 110 basis points — built during a period of sector weakness on a thesis about structural consolidation — has elements of the Hunter approach.

But your track record also shows the Rabbit masquerading as a Hunter. BFF was held through deterioration with the narrative of Hunter behaviour — "the market hasn't understood the transformation, I'm adding at better prices" — when the archetype had already shifted from quality compounder to cockroach pattern. This is the Hunter trap: it looks like disciplined, conviction-based addition, but the conviction is based on a thesis that has already been invalidated and not acknowledged.

[beat]

Freeman-Shor's differentiator between Hunter and Rabbit is the kill criterion. The Hunter always has one. It is written before the addition is made. It is specific. It is non-negotiable. "I am adding at this price, with this thesis, and if this specific condition is not met by this specific date, I will exit." The Rabbit never has a kill criterion. The Rabbit has a hope and a rationalization. And as Annie Duke shows exhaustively, hope and rationalization are not decision tools. They are decision avoidance tools.

Your Hunters need pre-written kill criteria in the same way your initial positions do. When you add to a declining position, that addition is a new trade. It has its own entry thesis and its own exit criteria. The average cost calculation is irrelevant to the exit decision. The exit decision for the addition is: has the thesis for the addition been confirmed or invalidated?

Kahneman's work on reference points and mental accounting is relevant here. When you average down, you create a new reference point — the new average cost — and psychologically treat the combined position as if it were entered at the average cost. This is a cognitive error. The original entry and the addition are two separate trades with two separate theses. Collapsing them into a single average cost destroys the information that would tell you whether each decision was correct.

**Part Five: The execution environment — how your external conditions shape tribal behaviour**

[normal pace]

Freeman-Shor does not discuss this directly, but it is implied throughout his data: the same investor can belong to different tribes in different environmental conditions. Not because their analytical capability changes. Because their emotional processing capacity changes with context.

Decision fatigue is real. Kahneman documented it. Steenbarger has written extensively on it in the trading context. When you are making high-stakes decisions under time pressure, with incomplete information, after a period of underperformance or stress, your System 2 (deliberate, rule-based, analytical) progressively yields control to System 1 (fast, pattern-matching, emotionally driven). And System 1, in the context of loss decisions, is reliably Rabbit-mode.

[beat]

The implication: your tribal behaviour is not a fixed characteristic of you as an investor. It is a function of your execution environment at the time of the decision. This is both sobering and empowering. Sobering because it means you cannot simply decide to be an Assassin and then execute cleanly under all conditions. Empowering because it means that improving your execution environment — the timing, the emotional state, the absence of competing cognitive demands — is a direct lever on your tribal behaviour.

Jim Loehr's oscillation principle from energy management applies here. Decisions made from a depleted state produce different tribal behaviour than decisions made from a recovered state. The Rabbit tendency — holding and hoping — is significantly more pronounced when you are cognitively and emotionally depleted. Because Rabbit behaviour is the path of least cognitive resistance: it requires no decision, no action, no acceptance of a loss. It is the default state. Assassin behaviour requires active decision-making capacity that is unavailable when you are depleted.

Steve Ward's 4Cs framework — Commitment, Concentration, Confidence, Control — is directly applicable. Assassin exits require all four at high levels simultaneously. Commitment to the rule (not the position). Concentration on the current signal (not the history of the position). Confidence that the rule is right even when the outcome is uncertain. Control over the Chimp's threat response.

[with emphasis]

The protocol implication: the most important position management decisions — adds to winners, cuts of losers — should be made during your highest-energy periods, with pre-written criteria already established, never under time pressure or emotional stress. Your morning decision windows, before market open, after energy recovery, with pre-written criteria already loaded — this is your Assassin/Connoisseur window. Your low-energy afternoon, under market pressure, with live positions flashing — this is your Rabbit window. Know which window you are in before making any significant position decision.

**Part Six: The meta-level — building an execution system, not improving individual decisions**

[slowly]

Here is Freeman-Shor's deepest insight, the one that most readers miss because they are looking for tactical improvements.

He did not study forty-five elite managers and conclude: "Some investors are psychologically superior and therefore execute better." He concluded: "The investors who execute best are the ones who have built decision architectures that make excellent execution the path of least resistance, even under cognitive and emotional stress."

[PAUSE]

The distinction is profound. Improving individual decisions under stress requires extraordinary willpower, self-awareness, and emotional regulation capacity. Building decision architectures that make the correct decision automatic requires none of those things in the moment — it requires them only during the calm, analytical construction of the architecture.

The shot clock protocol. The pre-written kill criteria. The progressive exposure tranches. The 30-week MA weekly review. The "would I buy this today?" test on every holding. These are not rules you follow by force of will. They are architecture that makes the correct tribal behaviour the path of least resistance.

When BFF's kill criteria would have been pre-written and the shot clock was counting — had that architecture been in place — the Assassin exit would have been the easier decision, not the harder one. Because the decision would already have been made. It would just be waiting to be executed.

This is what Jack Schwager found across his Market Wizards interviews. The common factor in virtually all the great traders he spoke to was not intelligence, information access, or analytical capability. It was systematic approach and the absolute commitment to executing that approach regardless of emotional state. Paul Tudor Jones's famous rule "Losers average losers" is not wisdom. It is architecture: a pre-committed constraint that removes Rabbit-mode averaging from the decision set entirely. You cannot average into losers if you have structurally committed to never doing so. The constraint does the work that willpower cannot reliably provide.

[beat]

Your architecture is more developed than most investors'. The corrections log. The kill criteria template. The stage analysis overlay. Watson's meta-role surveillance. The 30-day shot clock. These are the right building blocks.

The advanced execution question is not whether the blocks exist. It is whether the architecture is complete enough, and rigid enough, to function under the specific stress conditions that produce your worst tribal behaviour. Because under normal conditions, your architecture works fine. It is under the conditions of a significant drawdown, a large position at loss, a long-running thesis that is not confirming — it is under those specific conditions that the architecture needs to be most robust, and that is precisely when it is under the most pressure.

The advanced practitioner's task: stress-test your decision architecture. For each position above five percent of the portfolio, ask: if this position goes against me by twenty percent over the next eight weeks, with fundamentals ambiguous, what does my architecture require me to do? And would the architecture's output be executable under those emotional conditions? If the honest answer is no — if you can imagine the Rabbit taking over despite the architecture — what is the specific missing element that would make the architecture robust against that scenario?

That gap between "the architecture says exit" and "I would actually exit" is the remaining work.

A quality compounder you have been managing as a Raider — exiting too early, capping the upside. That is a Connoisseur deficit.

Name the gaps. Write them down. Because the gaps are not character flaws. They are architecture deficits. And architecture is fixable.

Freeman-Shor showed across 30,000 trades and forty-five elite managers that execution is the variable. Not analysis. Not intelligence. Not even experience.

The question is not whether you understand the tribes. You do. The question is whether your architecture is strong enough to enforce the right tribal behaviour when the Chimp is loudest.

Build the architecture. Run the audit. Close the gaps.

**Part Seven: The return pattern of execution versus selection — what the data actually says**

[normal pace]

Let me give you the most challenging data point in Freeman-Shor's work. The one that deserves more attention than it gets.

He examined what would have happened to each manager's returns if, instead of their actual execution, they had simply held every stock they bought until Freeman-Shor decided to liquidate the fund. No active position management. No adding, no trimming, no cutting. Just hold whatever you buy until the final exit.

The results were counterintuitive. For the best managers — the ones in the Connoisseur and Assassin tribes — actual execution significantly outperformed the passive hold. The active management added value. For the worst managers — the Rabbits and Raiders — actual execution significantly underperformed the passive hold. The active management destroyed value.

[PAUSE]

The implication is devastating for anyone who believes that "more active management is better management." It is not. Active management by poor executors is worse than no management at all. Their interventions — the partial exits, the late cuts, the premature profit-taking — consistently moved the portfolio in the wrong direction relative to the passive baseline.

This is Taleb's point about intervention in complex systems: if the system's natural dynamics are moving in the right direction, intervention often creates more harm than good. The Rabbit's interventions — the partial trims to "take risk off," the monitoring pauses, the rationalisation cycles — are all forms of intervention in a position's natural lifecycle that produce worse outcomes than the position would have produced if left alone with clean entry criteria and pre-written exit rules.

[with emphasis]

For your portfolio: the Avanza position is the clearest illustration of non-intervention outperforming intervention. Seventy-two months. Multiple periods where the "rational" active management response would have been to reduce, trim, or exit based on valuation, sector headwinds, or short-term price weakness. You held. The non-intervention produced 170 basis points — your best outcome.

The advanced execution question is therefore not "how do I manage my positions more actively?" It is "how do I identify the positions where non-intervention is the optimal strategy, and build architecture that prevents me from interfering with them?"

The answer is the Connoisseur framework: a quality compounder with a defined thesis, no cockroach signals, stage 2 intact, Mansfield RS positive. Those conditions are the trigger for deliberate non-intervention. Not passivity. Deliberate, conscious, disciplined non-intervention. You have decided not to act. Not failed to act.

**Part Seven extension: The cross-archetype combination risk Freeman-Shor does not name**

[slightly slower]

There is one execution risk that Freeman-Shor's framework implies but never explicitly names. I want to name it, because it is the most common advanced execution failure in concentrated portfolios.

The risk is holding two positions simultaneously where one is in Connoisseur mode and one is in Rabbit mode — and the emotional dynamics of the Rabbit position contaminating the execution discipline of the Connoisseur position.

[beat]

Here is the mechanism. You have a quality compounder that is advancing well — Avanza equivalent. You have a deteriorating position that you are holding past your kill criteria — BFF equivalent. The BFF position is consuming psychological bandwidth: monitoring it, rationalising the hold, managing the emotional weight of the loss. That bandwidth consumption is not neutral. It depletes the cognitive and emotional resources available for managing the Avanza position with appropriate Connoisseur discipline.

In practice, this depletion manifests as premature trimming of the good position. You are under pressure from the bad position. The good position offers a readily available source of capital and psychological relief. Cutting it provides short-term emotional relief — "I took some profits, I'm being responsible" — while postponing the more difficult decision about the bad position. This is the classic Raider-on-the-winner, Rabbit-on-the-loser pattern that Freeman-Shor's data shows is the most common P&L-destroying combination.

Steenbarger calls this "emotional portfolio management" — making portfolio decisions based on the emotional state created by the portfolio rather than on the individual merits of each position. The antidote is structural separation: evaluate each position on its own merits, with pre-written criteria, independent of the emotional state generated by other positions. The Avanza decision and the BFF decision are analytically independent. They should also be psychologically independent.

[PAUSE]

Bassham's directive affirmations are useful here. The specific affirmation for this scenario: "I manage each position on its own merits, with clear criteria, independently." Not as a generic statement of good intentions. As a specific, practiced mental rehearsal of the separation. You imagine the bad position and the good position existing independently, with independent decision frameworks, and you feel the clean execution of each. The Chimp's urge to link them — to treat the good position as a source of relief from the bad one — is weakened each time you rehearse the separation.

The highest-performing executors in Freeman-Shor's dataset were not people with no emotional responses to their positions. They were people who had built psychological and process-level firewalls between their positions, so that the emotional state generated by one did not systematically contaminate the decisions about another.

That is the architecture of excellent execution at the portfolio level. Not stock-by-stock discipline in isolation. But portfolio-level discipline that prevents cross-contamination while maintaining the independence of each position's decision framework.

Build the firewall. Manage each position for what it is. And let the system's aggregate distribution express itself in your returns — not through the noisy, emotion-driven interventions that Freeman-Shor's worst executors were addicted to, but through the clean, pre-designed, architecture-driven decisions that his best ones made look effortless.
