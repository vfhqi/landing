## Segment 1: The Two Minds (Opening) | ~4 minutes

You know the feeling. You've done your homework on a stock. You've built the model. You've stress-tested the thesis. You've looked at the balance sheet, the competitive position, the management track record, the catalysts. You sat with your thinking long enough that it moved from doubt to conviction. The risk-reward profile made sense. So you took the position. You sized it correctly. You set your kill criteria. You had a plan.

And then market opens the next day, and your Training Mind shows up to work the position management shift.

The problem is that Training Mind shouldn't be there.

[PAUSE]

Bob Rotella, who spent decades coaching elite athletes — golfers, in particular — was obsessed with one distinction. He called it the Training Mind versus the Trusting Mind. And once you understand it, you'll see it everywhere in your portfolio. In fact, you've probably been watching it sabotage your best positions for years.

Let me explain what he meant.

The Training Mind is analytical. It's mechanical. It's conscious. It's the part of you that breaks things down into components. It questions. It doubts. It stress-tests. It models. It builds the thesis. It sets the rules. The Training Mind is where research happens. This is where your conviction should be built, not broken. You're allowed to worry in Training Mind. You're allowed to debate yourself. You're allowed to say, "I'm not sure about this," and then either fix the model or walk away.

The Trusting Mind is different entirely. It's intuitive. It's subconscious. It's the part of you that executes. It runs on pattern-matching and long practice. It doesn't second-guess itself. It doesn't re-analyse. It doesn't tinker. It takes the position that Training Mind approved, and it holds it according to the plan you wrote. The Trusting Mind executes.

Here's the insight Rotella hammered home to his athletes: your best performance happens when these two minds work in sequence, not in conflict. You do your training with Training Mind. You prepare. You build the model. You practise. You question yourself. You stress-test. And then, when it's time to perform—when it's time to step up and take the shot—you lock Training Mind in a closet and you let Trusting Mind run.

The moment Training Mind shows up during execution, everything falls apart.

[PAUSE]

And in investing, this is where you live. Training Mind contaminating Execution. Over-analysing mid-position. Doubting the thesis you had conviction in last week. Tinkering with the position just because you found one new data point. Adding conditions you never mentioned to your original kill criteria. Re-reading sell-side research and letting it shake your conviction. Getting spooked by some fellow investor's comment and suddenly running the analysis again, asking yourself whether you were right in the first place.

This is the terrain where your worst outcomes live.

## Segment 2: The Contamination Pattern | ~6 minutes

Let me walk you through what this looks like in practice, because I want you to see it as clearly as possible.

When you first learn to invest, you do the thing right. You research. You build conviction. You enter. You set your stops. You size appropriately. You know the thesis inside out — the business, the catalysts, the risks. And so when something happens in the market or in the stock, you ask one clean question: Have my kill criteria been breached? No? Then hold. Yes? Then exit. That's it. You're in Trusting Mind. You execute the plan you wrote in Training Mind.

But then something shifts. Maybe you start to question the analysis. Maybe you see new sell-side research that suggests a different interpretation. Maybe the stock drops 12 percent on no news and your confidence wobbles. Maybe you run into another investor who's more bearish than you expected. So you open the model again. You start re-analysing. You're trying to decide whether your original thesis was right or wrong.

You've just brought Training Mind into the execution phase. And now you're contaminated.

What happens next is always the same pattern. Your conviction wavers because you're not just executing the plan anymore—you're trying to validate the plan in real time. You start finding arguments against the thesis that you'd already considered and dismissed. They look scarier now because you're seeing them with fresh doubt instead of the conviction you had when you committed capital. So you start to wobble. You tinker with the position. You add new conditions. You move your stop. You wait for one more data point to confirm that you should stay in or get out.

And the longer Training Mind stays engaged in what should be a Trusting Mind execution, the worse the outcome usually is.

[PAUSE]

Rotella's athletes saw this all the time. A golfer would do all the preparation. Build the swing. Practise. Understand the mechanics. Get ready for the tournament. And then standing over the shot, instead of just executing the swing they'd practised a thousand times, they'd start thinking about the mechanics. Are my feet positioned right? Is my grip correct? Am I starting the downswing at the right moment? Am I keeping my head still? The moment those thoughts showed up, the body would stiffen. The rhythm would break. The shot would fail.

Not because the preparation was insufficient. Not because the swing was flawed. But because the golfer had brought conscious thought into a task that needed to run on subconscious competence.

Your portfolio is full of golf shots right now. Positions where you did the work. You built the thesis. You set the plan. And now you're standing over the shot trying to re-validate every part of the decision while the market moves.

Let me give you some concrete examples, because I think you'll see yourself in these.

Remember your telecoms trade? You did the work. You built the thesis over time. You understood the sector. You understood the dynamics. You had conviction on the catalysts. You entered. And then—and this is the key—you let the position breathe. You didn't second-guess yourself every time there was sector noise. You didn't re-read the sell-side research and wobble. You held according to your plan.

The position worked. You made a strong return on it.

That's what Trusting Mind looks like in execution. You did the training. You did the analysis. You built the conviction. And then you executed the plan without constantly re-validating it.

This is evidence that your system works. When you trust yourself, when you let the position run according to the thesis you built, you get good outcomes. This is not luck. This is what happens when Training Mind stays out of the execution phase.

The reason I'm highlighting this is because I want you to see that you already know how to do this. You've proven it. The Telecoms trade is a proof of concept. So when you wobble on another position, you have a template. You have a precedent. You've done it before.

## Segment 4: Greggs — The Case Where You Knew Better | ~5 minutes

Greggs is a different story.

Your analysis on Greggs was solid. The thesis was there. The business quality was there. The growth profile was there. You had conviction. You entered the position. And then Pedro got nervous. One of your investors—a smart person, no question—voiced doubts. And instead of holding the thesis you'd already validated with the Training Mind work, you started to second-guess it. You let someone else's Training Mind process contaminate your Trusting Mind execution.

The position was right. The market proved it. It went up 40 percent after you exited. 40 percent that you left on the table.

Here's what happened. Your Training Mind had done the work. Your analysis was sound. Your thesis was defensible. Your Trusting Mind knew it. But when someone else's doubts showed up—when someone else's Training Mind process got introduced into your execution space—you suddenly needed to re-validate. You weren't confident enough to say, "I've done my work. I know this thesis. I'm holding it." Instead, you started asking questions. You started to doubt. You started to analyse again.

In that moment, your Trusting Mind lost the game.

[PAUSE]

The lesson here isn't about Greggs. It's not about whether Greggs was the right trade or the wrong one. It was the right trade. The lesson is about the boundary between Training Mind and Trusting Mind execution. Your Training Mind had already done the work. Your Trusting Mind should have been protected from second-guessing. And when external doubt showed up, you should have been able to say, "I've already validated this. I'm holding the thesis I built. Next question."

This is one of the hardest distinctions to hold in a conversation-driven environment. Because you talk to other investors. You hear their perspectives. They have good ideas. They have scary perspectives too. And it's very easy to let their Training Mind process become your re-validation loop, which corrupts your Trusting Mind execution.

The fix is simple in principle, hard in practice: protect the boundary. You're not re-analysing based on someone else's nervousness. You're checking your kill criteria. If they're not breached, you hold.

## Segment 5: Kainos and the Reversed Indicator | ~5 minutes

Let's look at another one: Kainos. And this one is particularly instructive because the error is subtle.

You built an indicator. The indicator was supposed to signal when the hiring environment was deteriorating. You had conviction in the signal. You said, "When this indicator reverses, I exit." That's the Trusting Mind rule you set. That's the plan. That's what Training Mind decided was the kill criterion.

The indicator reversed. It was a clear signal. The thesis had weakened. According to the plan you wrote, you should exit.

But instead of executing that plan, you started to re-analyse the indicator. You asked, "Is the indicator reliable? Did I build it correctly? Maybe the reversal isn't as significant as I thought. Maybe I should hold and wait for more confirmation." You brought Training Mind back into a decision that was already made.

You didn't trust the tool you'd built. Which means you didn't trust yourself.

[PAUSE]

Here's what Rotella would say about this: You built that indicator for exactly this purpose. To solve a problem during Training Mind that you wouldn't want to have to solve in real time during Trusting Mind execution. You wanted a clean signal. You built it. You said, "When I see this signal, I'm out. I don't have to re-analyse. I don't have to think. I just execute."

And when the signal showed up, you threw away the tool and went back to first principles. You second-guessed the decision you'd already made.

The fix for the next time is this: when your own tool gives you a signal, trust it. You built it for this. You validated it during Training Mind. When it fires during execution, you don't get to re-analyse it. You exit. That's the rule. That's the boundary.

Because the moment you allow yourself to re-analyse your own tools mid-execution, you've opened the door to re-analysing everything. And then there are no tools left. There's only you, doubting yourself, trying to figure out whether this position should stay or go.

## Segment 6: XVIVO — The CFO Rule | ~5 minutes

XVIVO is another variation on the same theme.

You had a rule. It was a clean rule. Bottom-quartile CFO equals sell. That's a Training Mind decision. That's you saying, "This is a critical job. If the person in this role is bottom quartile, the downside risk is too high. I'm going to flag this as a kill criterion. When I find a bottom-quartile CFO, I exit."

You found a bottom-quartile CFO. Clear signal. Kill criterion breached.

But instead of executing the rule you'd written, you started to analyse. You went deeper into the CFO's track record. You tried to understand whether the bottom-quartile designation was fair. You looked for redeeming context. You went back into Training Mind when what you should have done was execute what Training Mind had already decided.

[PAUSE]

The error here is slightly different than Kainos, but the pattern is identical. You had a rule. The rule fired. Instead of executing it, you re-opened the decision.

What would have happened if you'd executed the rule when you first found it? You would have exited the position. You would have protected yourself from the downside that came later. And yes, if the CFO had turned out fine, you would have exited a position that worked. But that's the cost of the rule. That's what you agreed to when you set the criterion.

This is where Rotella gets very firm. He says: You will have losers. You will exit positions that later recover. Trusting Mind accepts this. Training Mind resists it. Training Mind wants to optimise each individual outcome. Training Mind wants to be right about every decision. Training Mind wants to hold the winners and avoid the losers after the fact.

But you're not operating in after-the-fact. You're operating in real time. And in real time, the only way to execute with conviction is to set rules in Training Mind and follow them in Trusting Mind, even when they cost you.

## Segment 7: Avanza — Preparation Meets Execution | ~5 minutes

Let me give you an example where it worked, at least in part.

Avanza is your long-term connoisseur hold. 72 months. You did the Training Mind work. You built the thesis. You understood the CEO. You understood the business model. You understood the margin structure. And you've held it through noise because your thesis hasn't changed. That's Trusting Mind working correctly.

But here's the thing you said yourself: when you got the IR call with the Avanza CEO, it went vague. "Not my Avanza CEO meeting—I was vague on the numbers because I was vague on the numbers." That wasn't a Trusting Mind execution problem. That was a Training Mind preparation problem. You hadn't done the work you needed to do before you walked into the room.

Trusting Mind doesn't mean unprepared. It means prepared plus disciplined execution. You do the training. You know the numbers. You understand the thesis. And then in the room, you're confident enough not to over-prepare. You're not re-validating. You're not second-guessing. You know what you know, and you execute the conversation cleanly.

The Avanza position worked over 72 months because your Training Mind conviction never broke. But the CEO call would have gone better if you'd brought slightly more Training Mind preparation to it before you entered. You need both. Training Mind builds the foundation. Trusting Mind executes confidently on top of it.

And then you held it according to the thesis. You didn't over-analyse when it moved. You didn't look for new reasons to panic. You executed the position because you knew the thesis inside and out.

This is what Rotella means by the two minds working in sequence. You did exceptional Training Mind work. The research was deep. The conviction was clear. And then you trusted yourself in execution. No second-guessing. No re-analysis. Just clean, confident execution on the back of solid preparation.

## Segment 9: Gallwey and the Inner Game | ~4 minutes

There's a coach named Tim Gallwey who's been thinking about this for decades. He called them Self 1 and Self 2. Self 1 is your conscious, analytical, critical, judging mind. That's your Training Mind. Self 2 is your intuitive, pattern-matching, decisive, flowing mind. That's your Trusting Mind.

Gallwey's most important insight is this formula: Performance equals Potential minus Interference.

P = Potential minus Interference.

Your potential as an investor is quite high. You've proven it. You have good instincts. You know how to research. You know how to build conviction. You know how to manage risk. You know how to execute. Your potential is there.

But you have interference. And most of that interference comes from Self 1 showing up during execution. Self 1 asking questions like, "What if this doesn't work? What if I'm wrong? What if the market knows something I don't?" Those are Self 1 questions. They're legitimate questions for Self 1 to ask during training. But during execution, they are pure interference.

[PAUSE]

Gallwey talks about "non-judgemental awareness." You notice the Self 1 chatter without engaging with it. "Oh, there's that thought again. What if this doesn't work. Interesting. I notice that thought. And I'm going to let Self 2 execute anyway, because I've already decided this."

Non-judgemental awareness. You're not fighting the doubt. You're not trying to convince yourself that you're right. You're just noticing it and letting it go. You're letting Self 2 run.

The Inner Game, Gallwey says, is the opponent inside your own head. It's more formidable than any external opponent. Because it's always present. It's always saying, "Are you sure?" And the more you engage with it, the worse you perform.

## Segment 10: Douglas — The Edge and the Random Distribution | ~4 minutes

Mark Douglas, who spent his career teaching traders about the psychology of execution, said something that's relevant here. He said: "Every edge has a random distribution of wins and losses."

Every edge. Not some edges. Every edge. Your edge—your system, your process, your thesis evaluation, your risk management—every part of it has a random distribution of outcomes.

Which means you will have losers. You will exit positions that later recover. You will hold positions through drawdowns that you could have avoided. You will be wrong sometimes, even when you're following your system correctly.

Training Mind hates this. Training Mind wants to optimize each individual outcome. Training Mind wants to be right about every trade. Training Mind wants to hold the winners and avoid the losers after the fact.

Trusting Mind accepts it.

[PAUSE]

Douglas talked about "the zone," which is a state of flow where execution is effortless. You're not thinking about outcomes. You're not trying to be right. You're just executing the system. Your hands know what to do. Your mind is quiet. You're in the flow.

You can only reach the zone in Trusting Mind. Training Mind is too loud. It won't let you be present. It's always projecting into the future, trying to predict outcomes, trying to be right.

Douglas's five fundamental truths are useful to remember here:

Anything can happen. Every moment is unique. There's a random distribution of outcomes. You don't need to know what happens next—you only need to execute your edge. Your edge is a higher probability, not a certainty.

Train your mind to accept these truths. Because the moment you need certainty, the moment you insist on knowing what happens next, the moment you try to predict the outcome before it occurs, you've locked yourself out of the zone. You're back in Training Mind, interfering with execution.

## Segment 11: The Pre-Shot Routine (Investing Adaptation) | ~4 minutes

Rotella talks about the pre-shot routine. This is something elite athletes do before they execute. They have a ritual. It's brief, it's disciplined, it's automatic. And it serves one purpose: to shift from Training Mind into Trusting Mind.

The golfer walks up to the ball. They check their alignment. They take a practice swing. They breathe. They look down the line. It takes about twenty seconds. And at the end of that ritual, they're ready. They've shifted modes. They're in Trusting Mind. And they execute.

For you, the pre-shot routine before a position management decision would look something like this:

You're considering an action. Before you act, you pause. You ask one clean question: Have my kill criteria been breached?

That's it. That's the entire routine. You check the kill criteria. You don't re-analyse the thesis. You don't look at the sell-side research again. You don't call your smartest friend to get their perspective. You just ask: Is this on my list of reasons I would exit this position?

If the answer is yes, you exit. That's Training Mind speaking. You already decided this when you were calm and thinking clearly. You execute it.

If the answer is no, you hold. And then you stop re-analysing. The decision is made. You're not coming back to it today.

[PAUSE]

This is your pre-shot routine. It's brief. It's clean. It's designed to prevent Training Mind from contaminating Trusting Mind execution. You check the criteria. You execute. Done.

The beauty of this is that it puts all the weight on the kill criteria themselves. Which means the time to think clearly is when you're building those criteria, not when you're executing. You do your thinking during training. You execute during trusting.

## Segment 12: A Note on Rotella's Commandments | ~3 minutes

Rotella wrote a book called "Golf Is Not a Game of Perfect," and in it he lays out what he calls the Ten Commandments for performing under pressure. I won't run through all of them, but some of them are worth holding:

"Play to play great, not to avoid losing."

That's about mindset. When you're in Trusting Mind, you're playing to execute your system. You're not playing defensively. You're not trying not to lose. You're executing the thesis you believe in.

"Love the challenge of the day."

You have positions in your portfolio right now. The market is moving. There are data points coming in. You're being challenged to execute the thesis you built. That challenge is the game. Love it. Don't resent it. Don't wish for certainty. Love the fact that you built a system and now you get to execute it in real conditions.

"Accept that outcomes are uncertain."

This is Douglas again. Outcomes are uncertain. Your job is not to predict them. Your job is to execute your edge. The outcome will be what it will be.

"Put no limits on yourself."

This is about conviction. You have a thesis. You have sized the position. You have set your stops. You have planned your execution. Don't second-guess the size. Don't shrink the conviction because you got nervous. Trusting Mind means you're willing to back yourself at the size you decided.

These commandments are training-era thinking. They're building the foundation. But they're also guides for how to show up in execution. They're the mindset you bring to Trusting Mind.

## Segment 13: The Integration — Where You Are Now | ~5 minutes

Let me connect this back to where you are.

You've built a good system. You know how to research. You know how to build conviction. You know how to manage risk. You know how to execute. You've proven all of it. The Telecoms trade proved it. The BFF entry proved it. The Avanza hold proved it.

But you also have a pattern: Training Mind shows up too often during execution. You re-analyse. You second-guess. You let other people's doubts become your reasons to re-validate. You tinker with positions instead of holding the thesis. You bring conscious thought into tasks that should be running on subconscious competence.

The good news is that this is entirely fixable. Because you're not missing the capability. You're missing the discipline. You know how to do the thing. You're just doing it in the wrong order—or in both orders at the same time.

The fix is simple in principle:

**Do your training with Training Mind. Build the conviction. Set the kill criteria. Do the work.** This is where you're allowed to doubt. This is where you're allowed to debate yourself. This is where you're allowed to ask hard questions and look for evidence against your thesis. This is what your research time is for.

**Then, when you enter the position, you shift into Trusting Mind. You execute according to the plan you wrote. You don't re-analyse. You don't second-guess. You don't tinker.**

[PAUSE]

And here's the hard part: you protect that boundary. When Training Mind wants to show up during execution, you notice it. You acknowledge it. And you don't let it run the show.

You check your kill criteria. Not your entire thesis. Not your conviction. Not the market outlook. Just your kill criteria: Have they been breached?

That's the boundary. Protect it. That's everything.

## Segment 14: Your Assignment | ~3 minutes

So here's what I want you to do.

Look at your portfolio right now. Look at one position where you know that Training Mind is contaminating Trusting Mind execution. There's probably more than one. Pick the one where you're most aware of the pattern. Where you know you're second-guessing yourself. Where you know you're re-analysing when you shouldn't be.

Ask yourself this question: Have my kill criteria been breached?

Just that. Not, "Is this thesis still valid?" Not, "Would I enter this position today?" Not, "What do the latest numbers tell me?" Just: Have my kill criteria been breached?

If the answer is yes, you have your action. You exit. That's what you decided when you were thinking clearly.

If the answer is no, then the action is also clear: you hold. And you stop re-analysing. You tell Training Mind, "I've already done your job. The kill criteria haven't been breached. We're holding. Come back and talk to me when something changes."

[PAUSE]

That's the boundary. That's the whole game.

Training Mind builds the thesis. Trusting Mind executes it. The separation is where your edge lives.
