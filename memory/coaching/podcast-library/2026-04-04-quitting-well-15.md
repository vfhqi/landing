## PART 1: QUITTING IS NOT FAILURE [~3 minutes]

Let me start with who Annie Duke is, because it matters. She's a former World Series of Poker champion. Four million dollars in tournament earnings. She studied cognitive science at Pennsylvania. And the thing she learned from winning — the thing that made her the best in her room at decision-making — was not how to win more hands. It was how to fold better.

Folding is quitting. Laying down a hand you worked hard to build. Walking away from investment in chips and time and emotional energy. And Duke's insight is simple but radical: the opposite of a great quitter is not a gritty person. The opposite of a great quitter is someone who won't make hard decisions when the data demands it.

[PAUSE]

You're not a grit problem. You're a decision problem. You know what to do. You're not executing it.

This is critical: Duke would reframe the entire emotional charge around quitting. Right now, holding feels like conviction. Selling feels like admission of error. But that's exactly the opposite of what's actually happening. Holding a deteriorating position when the data has shifted against you — that's not conviction. That's paralysis dressed up as conviction. That's loss aversion. That's sunk cost fallacy doing its work.

And selling, the moment the thesis is invalidated, the moment the fundamentals shift, the moment the trend breaks — that's decisiveness. That's data-driven. That's execution. That's the move that requires the most discipline.

Look at BFF. You documented the lesson: "ACH threshold breached = EXIT; don't wait until getting closer." You even named it "walking towards fire." You knew what you needed to do. And you didn't do it. Why?

Three overlapping biases. Duke calls them the sunk cost, endowment, and status quo triad.

Sunk cost: you've already invested the capital, the time, the emotional energy, the research hours. And your brain — trying to be helpful — says: well, if I sell now, all that becomes a real loss. So let's hold and hope it comes back. The past investment feels like it should influence the future decision. But it shouldn't. The only thing that matters is: given what I know now, does this position have better risk-reward than my next idea? If no, you hold to avoid crystallising a loss. That's a choice to compound the loss instead.

Endowment effect: you own this stock. Ownership creates attachment. Owned things feel more valuable than identical unowned things. This is not rational. It's deeply psychological. If you didn't own BFF today, would you buy it at the current price given its governance risks and deteriorating trend? No. Of course not. But because you own it, you overweight the upside and underweight the downside. The thing you own feels safer than the thing you don't own.

Status quo bias: change requires action. Holding requires inaction. Your nervous system is biased toward inaction — it feels safer, it requires less energy, it doesn't require you to admit error. So you drift into holding not because of strong conviction, but because selling requires active choice and selling also requires acknowledgement that the thesis has changed. Much easier to call it a "reduction" or "monitor closely" or "wait for earnings" — anything but acknowledge that the position is invalidated.

[PAUSE]

These three biases don't operate separately. They compound. And they're operating right now in your portfolio — probably on more than one name.

## PART 2: KILL CRITERIA — THE ENGINEERING SOLUTION [~4 minutes]

So how do you fight back against a trio of cognitive biases that are built into your nervous system? You don't fight them with willpower. Willpower is unreliable, especially at the moment of pain when your position is down 20% and every fibre of your body is saying "hold on, it'll come back."

You use engineering instead.

Annie Duke's solution is called kill criteria. And here's the key: you define them before you buy, not at the point of pain.

At the moment you decide to build a position in a stock, you also decide: what conditions would invalidate this thesis? Not "it goes down" — that's not a condition, that's noise. But what specific, observable, measurable signals would tell you the thesis is broken?

For XVIVO, the kill criteria might have been: "If the CFO is assessed as bottom quartile for capability, exit. If basic execution errors persist beyond Q2, exit. If conversion rates in the US leg deteriorate, exit." You wrote all of this in your post-mortem. "Errors in one organ were first cockroach. Legacy moat, not execution moat. CFO assessed as bottom quartile — this alone should invalidate."

You knew this. You just didn't action it when you should have.

For BFF, the kill criteria were clearer in hindsight. Your journal is explicit: "ACH threshold breached = exit." Investor attention metrics turned negative. Governance credibility eroded — the Consob file on Max, the IR deterioration. Insider hiring and culture problems compounded. These were cockroaches, and the first cockroach meant more. Your own rule: "cockroaches never alone."

Again: you knew this. You didn't execute.

Duke's framework forces precision. Instead of vague concepts like "deterioration" or "conviction loss," you commit to specific measures. Here are Duke's categories.

**Quantitative kill criteria:** Numbers on a dashboard that trigger action automatically. A stock breaks below its two-hundred-day moving average in your portfolio, you start the sell process. EBITDA margins compress by more than X basis points, exit. Insider selling accelerates beyond a threshold, exit. These are mechanical.

**Qualitative kill criteria:** Specific observable conditions that require judgment but are defined in advance. "CFO leaves or is assessed as unfit," exit. "Fundamental thesis invalidated by new information," exit — though you define in advance what that information looks like. "Trend indicators flip negative," exit.

**Time-based kill criteria:** Your 30-day shot clock is one of these. You define it at entry: "I have thirty days to see confirmation of the thesis. If I don't see it, I reduce to a testing position."

The power of pre-commitment is this: you're making the hard decision while you're calm and rational, before the position is down 25% and your amygdala is screaming at you to hold on.

[PAUSE]

Mark Minervini's system works perfectly alongside Duke's framework. Minervini gives you the mechanical discipline: the Trend Template, Stage Analysis, the 7-8% maximum loss rule. These are kill criteria expressed as price behaviour. They are objective. They require no interpretation.

Combine Duke's kill criteria framework with Minervini's mechanical stop-loss, and you've engineered yourself out of the bias trap.

## PART 3: THE SELF-IMAGE LAYER — BECOMING A QUITTER [~3 minutes]

But here's the thing: engineering gets you 70% of the way there. The last 30% is identity.

Lanny Bassham, Olympic gold medallist, spent his career studying why some people execute under pressure and others don't. He found that performance and self-image are always equal. You will not, on any sustained basis, perform above your self-image.

Your self-image right now, as a disciplined quitter, is fragile. You see quitting as exception, as failure case. You see holding as the default, as conviction. So even with all the engineering — the kill criteria, the mechanical stops, the Minervini framework — your self-image will pull you back toward holding. Toward "giving it one more quarter." Toward "the thesis hasn't changed, the price action is just noise."

Bassham's system for shifting self-image is explicit mental rehearsal. But it's not wishful thinking. It's a six-step system that rewires your identity through repeated imprinting.

Here's what this looks like for you.

You define the identity first: "I am a disciplined position manager who honours my kill criteria. When the data shifts, I act. I view quitting a deteriorating position as a form of excellence, not failure. I execute exits cleanly and decisively."

You write it out. Five copies, handwritten, placed at Key Points — your monitor, your desk, your daily journal.

Every morning for twenty-one days, you read it. You visualise it. You feel yourself making the decision. You see yourself closing out a position because the Trend Template broke. You see yourself cold-calling the sell to your broker and executing it within two minutes, no regret, no hesitation. Because the thesis is invalidated. The data demands it. And you are a person who honours the data.

[PAUSE]

This is not motivational poster work. This is how Olympic athletes rewire themselves under pressure. Bassham won gold because his self-image as a champion was so deeply embedded that his nervous system executed the correct behavior under extreme pressure, even when his conscious mind was in doubt.

You're doing the same work, but on the portfolio.

## PART 4: YOUR THREE CASUALTIES — WHAT WOULD DUKE SAY? [~2.5 minutes]

Let me map Duke's framework onto your three documented failures. This isn't to make you wrong. It's to see the pattern so clearly that it becomes impossible to repeat.

**BFF.** You had all the data. Governance risk was clear. Consob file on Max. IR deteriorated. Insider hiring suggested management wasn't confident. Your gut told you something was weird in that January call. Duke's kill criteria framework would have said: "Governance red flag = reduce by 50% immediately. Further deterioration = exit entirely." Instead you held through hope, endowment effect, and sunk cost. The position did eventually go your way, but you held much longer than the data supported. And that holding cost you optionality — capital locked in a deteriorating position instead of deployed into better risk-reward.

**XVIVO.** This is pure sunk cost and endowment. You did deep research. You learned the company. You owned the thesis. When execution errors appeared — basic stuff, silly stuff, but first cockroach — you rationalized. "They'll fix it. The moat is there. I've done too much work to be wrong about this." Duke would say: that deep work is a sunk cost. It doesn't matter for the forward decision. The forward decision is: given what I know now, CFO bottom quartile, execution moat not real, is this where I want capital deployed? No. Exit. You held for months past where the data invalidated the thesis, because you couldn't bear to acknowledge that all that research was for a position that shouldn't exist.

**Goodwin.** You bought what you thought was a good business. Management couldn't deliver investor communication. Share price crashed 40%. You documented: "Poor investor support and lack of IR." This is a kill criterion you didn't define in advance. Going forward: "If management cannot execute basic IR discipline, exit. Small-cap businesses cannot afford IR fragility." Pre-committed. Mechanical. No ambiguity.

## THE TAKEAWAY [~1 minute]

Here's what you're going to do starting today.

For every position you own right now, you're going to define your kill criteria. Not vague. Specific. Quantitative where possible, qualitative where necessary, time-based where it makes sense.

You write them into your case file at the moment you size the position. Not at the point of pain.

You integrate Minervini's Trend Template as a mechanical pre-check. Stock breaks the two-hundred-day moving average, the kill criteria activate.

And you rewrite your self-image. Every morning for twenty-one days: "I am a disciplined position manager who honours my kill criteria. Quitting a deteriorating position is excellence, not failure."

This isn't about being tougher or more committed. It's about engineering yourself out of the biases that are hijacking your execution. It's about making the hard decision while you're calm, not at the point of pain.

Your pattern of holding losers too long is not a character flaw. It's a system problem. And you already know how to fix systems.

[PAUSE]

Now go execute.
