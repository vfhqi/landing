## Segment 2: The Observer and the Chimp (4:00–10:30)

Steve Peters developed this framework — and I know you've read it, but let's go deeper into how it actually lives in your portfolio — in real time, with real positions.

The Observer is the part of you that can sit with data. It can look at a broken thesis without flinching. It can say, "The logic was sound at entry. The entry price was justified. And now the facts have changed. The right move is to sell." The Observer doesn't take it personally. It doesn't defend the position. It sees the position as a fact to be evaluated, not an identity to be protected.

[PAUSE]

The Chimp, by contrast, is reactive. It's fast. It's emotional. It's exquisitely sensitive to threat. When a position falls, the Chimp feels attacked. And so it does what Chimps do: it fights. It doubles down. It reframes. It tells stories. "The market doesn't understand." "They'll come around." "I'm being prudent by holding through the weakness." 

That last one is the killer. [with emphasis] Because prudence and Chimp mode sound identical to the untrained ear. They both sound careful. They both sound measured. But one is Observer mode holding a strong thesis. The other is Chimp mode disguising panic as patience.

Let me give you a concrete example. [beat] BFF.

You had this position. Strong business. You knew the story cold. And then the IR call in January felt — you wrote this — "diagnostic and super weird." Your Observer registered it immediately. Something was off. The tone was off. The messaging had shifted. The CFO's approach to shareholder questions had changed.

And your instinct was to exit. [PAUSE] That wasn't analysis yet. That was your Observer picking up a signal. A real one.

But what happened next? The Chimp showed up. [slower] "Let me get more data. Let me listen to the call again. Let me look at the numbers more closely. Maybe I'm reading too much into tone." 

Seven cockroach signals emerged over the next few weeks. Bad hiring. No respect for great talent. Weak IR. You wrote in your journal: "Bad hiring, no point talking about strategy if you can't execute." And you said, "No respect for great people. And a bad IR. Lots here."

By the time you exited, the thesis was dead. The Chimp had delayed you by weeks. And weeks in a deteriorating position is expensive.

[PAUSE]

But here's what I want you to hear: [with emphasis] Your Observer was right the first time. Your instinct caught it immediately. The signal was real. The problem with that trade wasn't your Observer. It was the gap between Observer and execution. The Chimp inserted itself, and you listened.

Now contrast that with Corbion. [beat]

You saw a pattern. Companies start saying "no drama, everything's fine" — and then three months later they're admitting they have inflation problems. It's an archetype. It repeats. Your Observer classified the pattern, and when Corbion started showing the signal, you cut to eight percent. Done. Clean. No analysis paralysis. No Chimp negotiation. You executed.

[slower] And that's Observer mode. That's the version of you that already exists. You've done it before.

## Segment 3: The Minervini Cycle and Chimp Activation (10:30–18:00)

Mark Minervini mapped out the emotional cycle that traders go through, and I want to lay it against what Peters says about Chimp activation, because they're describing the same thing from different angles.

Here's Minervini's cycle. [beat]

Stage One: Confidence at entry. You've done the work. The thesis is sound. You have a position. You feel good.

Stage Two: Early decline. Minus five, minus ten percent. The market starts to question. And this is where the Chimp wakes up. [with emphasis] Because now there's doubt. Now there's noise. And the Chimp's job is to remove uncertainty by turning it into a story. "The market doesn't see the thesis yet." "This is just noise." "Good investors have conviction."

[PAUSE]

But here's what the Chimp is really doing. It's avoiding the Observer question, which is: "Was my thesis wrong, or was my execution wrong, or is the timeframe just longer than I thought?" The Observer asks clarifying questions. The Chimp tells reassuring stories.

Stage Three: Frustration. Minus ten to minus twenty. Now you're frustrated. The market's moving against you. And the Chimp escalates. [with emphasis] "Why doesn't the market see what I see?" "The fundamentals haven't changed." "I'm smarter than the market on this one." The Chimp is now deeply invested in being right, not in making money.

[PAUSE]

This is where Keywords should have exited. You had a rule. "If it's not hell yeah, it's a no." And the AI slowdown was visible. The team was drowning in unnecessary analysis. Your Observer saw it. But the Chimp said, "No, wait, let me do more analysis." Let me look at one more data point. One more model. One more conversation. And each delay cost you.

Stage Four: Hope. Minus twenty to minus thirty. Now the Chimp has entered pure defense mode. It tells itself: "It can't go much lower. The risk/reward is compelling now. I should buy more." [slower] This is hope masquerading as analysis. This is the Chimp at maximum strength, because it's now reframed the position as a contrarian opportunity rather than a losing thesis.

Stage Five: Capitulation. Minus thirty-plus. And finally, when the pain becomes unbearable, the Chimp surrenders. You sell at the worst possible time, emotionally, which means the actual worst time, because at capitulation everybody wants out.

[PAUSE]

Now here's the key insight: [with emphasis] The Observer would exit at Stage Two. The moment there's data that contradicts the thesis, the Observer is alert. Not panicked. Alert. Asking: "Does this change the calculation? Do I still have a thesis, or do I have hope?"

Every stage past two that you stay in the position, the Chimp wins. And every stage the Chimp wins costs real money.

But and this is important — [with emphasis] — the Observer has been right every single time. You're not learning to develop the Observer. You already have it. You're learning to trust it.

Think about Protector. You bought it. The CEO resigned. You couldn't build conviction around the successor. You cut a forty percent loss. Clean. And you wrote in your journal, [slower] "On the surface, very bad trading decision. But I feel fine with the logic."

[PAUSE]

That's the Observer speaking. And it's right. The outcome — the stock went to plus two hundred and eighty percent — is noise. The process was sound. The Observer made the call based on what was knowable at the time. The Chimp would have held for hope. The Chimp would have said, "Maybe the new CEO surprises us." The Observer said, "I don't have conviction. I exit."

And guess what? You feel fine with that trade. Because the Observer knows it was right, regardless of outcome.

## Segment 4: The Chimp's Disguises (18:00–24:00)

The tricky part about the Chimp is that it doesn't wear a mask that says "I'm fear." It wears a mask that says "I'm prudence. I'm experience. I'm discipline."

[PAUSE]

You fell for that with S4 Capital. Flash trading update moved the stock zero. That's a screaming siren. That's Archetype Eleven: Fragile Business. And in real time, you classified it correctly. You saw the fragility. But then the Chimp whispered: "Maybe you're reading too much into this. Maybe you should hold and see if the story stabilizes."

And so you held. And the stock moved against you.

[slower] But here's what matters: [with emphasis] Your Observer saw it. Your classification was right. You identified the fragile business correctly. The next version of you — the one we're building right now — doesn't question that instinct. It trusts it and executes within 48 hours.

Or take Instalco. You trimmed on a broker note about inflation. Your Observer said, "Inflation is real. Risk off." And the Chimp immediately started negotiating. "Maybe trimming is emotional. Maybe that's not calm. Maybe a really mature investor holds through this kind of noise." 

[PAUSE]

So you took some risk back on. And the stock went up thirty percent, and you later recognized: "Taking risk off when I feel unsure feels mature, but it's actually the Chimp brain disguising itself as calm. Real calm is the Observer saying, 'I don't have visibility into the inflation variable. I'll revisit when I do.'"

[with emphasis] That's the awareness right there. You spotted your own Chimp disguise. You recognized the pattern. That self-awareness is rare. Most investors never catch it. You did. So the next version is executing on that awareness without hesitation.

The Chimp also disguises itself as thoroughness. You know this pattern. You do the work. You do more work. You do work on top of work. And somewhere in that pile of analysis, the Chimp is saying, "If I can just find one more data point, the uncertainty will go away. If I can just talk to one more person, the clarity will arrive."

[PAUSE]

But clarity never arrives from analysis. Clarity arrives from the Observer stepping back and saying, "I've done the work. The thesis works or it doesn't. The decision is clear or it isn't. Anything beyond that is delay."

That's what happened with Keywords. [beat] You had a rule: "If it's not hell yeah, it's a no." Clean. Simple. The Observer rule. And when AI slowdown signals emerged, you should have executed. Instead, the Chimp said, "Let me model this. Let me think about how quickly AI adoption slows. Let me talk to more people." And the position deteriorated while you were analyzing.

The rule was right. You already knew the answer. The next version executes the rule without negotiation.

## Segment 5: The Three-Move Reset (24:00–28:30)

So here's where we lock this in. Peters talks about moving from Chimp activation to Observer activation. And he gives a three-step framework: Recognise. Pause. Observe. Then Act.

Let's make it real for your portfolio.

Step One: Recognise. [beat] This is harder than it sounds, because the Chimp is very good at disguising itself. But you're going to get familiar with the tells. When you hear yourself saying, "I need more data," ask: [with emphasis] "Do I need data, or am I avoiding a decision?" When you hear, "The fundamentals haven't changed," ask: "Are the fundamentals the same, or am I the same?" When you hear, "I'm being prudent," ask: "Am I being prudent, or am I being afraid?"

Those questions are the Observer waking up.

Step Two: Pause. [PAUSE] This is literally it. When you notice the Chimp is activated, you don't fight it. You don't lecture yourself. You just pause. Take a breath. Let the heart rate come down. Peters is clear: the Chimp is fastest when you're amped up, when the threat feels immediate. The pause is the reset.

Step Three: Observe. From the pause, ask your Observer question: "What does the process say?" Not "What do I want to happen?" Not "What would be embarrassing?" Just: "What does the process say?"

Your process is clear. You have triaging rules. You have ESA criteria. You have DD checkpoints. You have sell discipline. The Observer knows all of it. It just needs the Chimp to be quiet enough to speak.

[slower] "What does the process say?" becomes the magic question. And nine times out of ten, the process will say: Exit. Cut the loss. Move on.

Then you Act. [beat] Not tomorrow. Not after one more analysis. [with emphasis] Within 48 hours. You name the Chimp in real time — actually do this, say it out loud — "I notice the Chimp is telling me to hold." And then within 48 hours, the Observer executes.

That 48-hour window is critical, because it's enough time for the emotions to settle but not enough time for the Chimp to rebuild its case. And when you hit execute, when you actually take the loss, something shifts.

[PAUSE]

Because here's the thing: [with emphasis] When you take a loss cleanly, when you execute it from Observer mode, the loss doesn't hurt the way it does when you hold through Chimp mode and finally capitulate. The clean loss is actually a relief. It's closure. It's data. And the Observer knows that the process was sound.

That's the emotional reset we're after.
