## The Cookie Jar — Collecting Evidence

Let's start with the concept. Bassham's work with elite athletes centers on something called self-image. The image you hold of yourself, he argues, drives behaviour. If you unconsciously believe you're someone who folds under pressure, your behaviour will align with that belief — you'll choke in the moment because the belief is running the show in the background. But if your self-image is "I am someone who executes under pressure," behaviour follows. Not because you're lying to yourself, but because that identity actually lives in your nervous system.

[beat]

The problem is that self-image is largely built on evidence. And the evidence collection is often passive and selective. You don't go looking for proof that you're competent. You go looking for evidence that confirms your fears.

So Bassham invented something called the cookie jar. It's a literal or figurative container where you put — consciously, deliberately — the evidence that you perform well. The evidence that "I am someone who [specific behaviour]."

For you, this means: Every clean exit. Every decision to upsize on clear conviction. Every time you diagnosed your own psychology honestly and acted on it. Every thesis that worked because you identified multiple independent drivers. Every oversold bounce you caught. Every boring quality compounder you held through drawdown because the thesis was sound.

[beat]

That's the cookie jar.

And you have hundreds of cookies in there. You just haven't been cataloguing them.

## Steve Ward's Confidence — Built on Evidence

Now, I want to bring in something Steve Ward teaches about the four Cs. One of them is Confidence.

[PAUSE]

And here's what Ward would say. Confidence is not motivation. Motivation fluctuates. You feel motivated on good days and unmotivated on bad ones. But confidence is different. Confidence is built on evidence of past performance. It's built on preparation. It's built on reviewing your good decisions systematically.

The mistake most investors make is they build confidence backward. They think: "If I perform well, then I'll feel confident." But that's not how it works. It's the opposite. If you build confidence first — by cataloguing evidence of good decisions, by understanding your process, by internalizing "I am someone who does X" — then the performance follows.

[slower]

Ward talks about something he calls the Foundation Stones. These are the non-negotiable elements of sustainable high performance. One of them is psychological recovery. Another is emotional stability. But the base of all of it is: knowing your evidence.

And you have that evidence. You're just not accessing it systematically.

[beat]

Think about what happens when you go into a decision point right now. A new stock. A position that's against you. A moment where you're uncertain. What do you reach for? You probably reach for: "Wait, what went wrong with XVIVO?" Or "Remember that time I held BFF too long?" You're reaching for the negative cookies. The ones that confirm "I make mistakes."

[PAUSE]

[with emphasis]

But what if you reached for a different mental file? What if, before a tricky decision, you recalled: "I am someone who exited Corbion cleanly, even when it felt wrong. I am someone who sized Cable and Wireless with full conviction. I am someone who recognized Entain in March 2020 as a repricing opportunity."

[beat]

That's not ego. That's confidence built on evidence.

## James Clear's Identity Votes

There's one more piece to this puzzle, and it comes from James Clear's work on habits and identity.

Clear argues that every action you take is a vote for the person you want to become. You don't build identity by saying "I am disciplined." You build identity by taking a disciplined action. Then another. Then another. And the identity compounds.

[slower]

"Every vote counts for the person you wish to be."

[PAUSE]

But the reverse is also true. And this is important. Every win you don't acknowledge, every clean decision you don't catalogue, is a vote against your own confidence. You're literally making a choice not to let the evidence settle in.

[beat]

Right now, in April 2026, you're doing something remarkable. You've built a dashboard that tracks relative strength and breadth. You've documented your track record across ninety-six stocks. You've created nineteen archetypes that describe the patterns you see. You've written a hundred-plus rules around risk management — your own rules, from your own journal. You're building a coaching system. You're designing your environment to make right behaviour easier.

That's not just process. That's identity construction. You're literally voting for "I am someone who builds systems that make good decisions easier."

[beat]

Every time you update that dashboard, you're voting. Every time you open the archetype file and recognize a pattern, you're voting. Every time you sit down and write a decision rule based on a past mistake, you're voting.

[PAUSE]

[slower]

"I am becoming someone who learns from his own experience systematically."

## Mental Rehearsal — Rehearsing the Feeling

This is going to sound strange, but stay with me.

[PAUSE]

[slower]

Because when the moment actually comes, the nervous system recognizes the feeling. And the body knows what to do.

[beat]

You can apply this directly. Before you need to make a tough exit decision, rehearse the feeling of a clean exit. Not the mechanics. The feeling.

Close your eyes. You're holding a position. It's gone against you. You have the information that says this thesis is broken. Or the conviction has eroded. Or the ACH threshold is breached.

Cleanly. Without delay. Without rationalization. Without looking back.

[beat]

[PAUSE]

[even slower]

[beat]

Not once. Repeatedly. Until the feeling is familiar. Until your nervous system knows "I am someone who can do this."

[slower]

Because when the moment comes — and it will — your body will recognize the feeling. And you'll act.

## The 1% Compound — Every Cookie Counts

One more piece, and it's from Clear's work on small improvements.

[PAUSE]

He talks about how a one percent improvement compounds. Not in a linear way. Exponentially. If you improve one percent every day, in a year you're not one percent better. You're roughly thirty-seven times better.

[beat]

What does this mean for your identity as an investor?

[slower]

Every time you catalogue a win — even a small one — you're compounding your self-image. Every time you recognize "I made the right call on that exit," you're adding to the evidence. Every time you review a decision rule and say "Yes, I applied that correctly," you're strengthening the identity.

[beat]

You don't need to hit home runs to build confidence. You need to hit singles, over and over, and keep track of them.

[PAUSE]

This is why the dashboard matters. This is why the track record file matters. This is why the stock archetypes matter. They're not just tools. They're identity infrastructure. Every time you use them, you're voting for "I am someone who learns systematically."
