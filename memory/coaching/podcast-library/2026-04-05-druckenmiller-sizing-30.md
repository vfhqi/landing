# Episode 9 — The Sizing Decision
## 30-Minute Coaching Podcast | 5 April 2026
### Theme: Portfolio-Level Risk Management — Position Sizing, Concentration, and Knowing When to Press and Pull Back
### Primary Author: Stanley Druckenmiller | Secondary: Van Tharp, George Soros

---

[WATSON SPEAKING — warm, direct, measured pace throughout. This is a private coaching conversation, not a broadcast.]

[PAUSE — three seconds]

Good evening, Richard.

Settle in. This one might be the most important episode we have done so far, because tonight we are going to talk about the decision that sits above all other decisions in your process. Not which stock to buy. Not when to sell. Not whether the macro backdrop favours risk or caution. Those are all important, but they are downstream. The upstream decision, the one that actually determines your returns over a five, ten, twenty-year career, is how much.

How much do you put on when you see something clearly. How much do you pull back when you do not. How much capital do you commit to the idea that has survived every layer of your process versus the one that is still emerging from the fog. That is the sizing decision. And there is no one in the history of public markets who understood this better than Stanley Druckenmiller.

[PAUSE]

Let me start with a number that should stop you in your tracks. Druckenmiller ran money for thirty years without a single losing year. Not one. His average annual return at Duquesne Capital was roughly thirty percent. At the Quantum Fund under Soros, he compounded at approximately forty-five percent annually through the late eighties and nineties. Those are not returns you generate by being right slightly more often than you are wrong. Those are returns you generate by being right in size. By making the sizing decision correctly, consistently, over decades.

And Druckenmiller himself said it plainly. Position sizing, he told an audience, is seventy to eighty percent of the equation. Not stock selection. Not timing. Not macro calls. Seventy to eighty percent. Let that land for a moment, because if you are honest with yourself, and I know you are, you spend roughly ninety percent of your analytical time on the selection decision and perhaps ten percent on the sizing decision. That ratio is inverted relative to what actually drives your returns.

[PAUSE]

Now, I am not saying stock selection does not matter. Of course it does. You cannot size into a bad idea and expect good outcomes. But Druckenmiller's insight, learned at the feet of George Soros, is that the selection decision is merely the entry ticket. The sizing decision is the game itself.

Here is how Soros put it, and Druckenmiller has quoted this more than any other line in his career: "It's not whether you're right or wrong that's important, but how much money you make when you're right and how much you lose when you're wrong."

Read that again in your mind. It is deceptively simple. Most investors nod along when they hear it, file it away, and then go back to spending all their energy trying to be right more often. But Soros and Druckenmiller understood something deeper. The distribution of outcomes in investing is not normal. It is wildly skewed. A small number of decisions account for a disproportionate share of total returns. And the way you capture that skew is not by having more ideas. It is by sizing the right ideas aggressively enough that when they work, they move the needle at the portfolio level.

[PAUSE — let that breathe]

Let me take you to September 1992, because the story of the pound trade is not just a war story. It is a masterclass in sizing psychology.

Druckenmiller had been managing the Quantum Fund for Soros since 1988. By 1992, he had built a thesis around the British pound. The UK was locked into the European Exchange Rate Mechanism at a rate that was fundamentally unsustainable. The economy was weak, interest rates were too high for domestic conditions, and the Bundesbank was tightening. Druckenmiller saw the setup clearly. He went to Soros and told him he wanted to short the pound. One hundred percent of the fund.

Now, one hundred percent of the fund is already an enormous position. Most portfolio managers would consider twenty percent aggressive. Druckenmiller was proposing five times that. And Soros looked at him, and this is the part of the story that matters, Soros looked at him with what Druckenmiller described as great disdain. Not because the position was too large. Because it was too small. Soros told him the thesis was good enough to go two hundred percent of the fund. To lever up and make it even bigger.

[PAUSE]

Think about what happened in that exchange. Druckenmiller, already one of the best macro traders alive, had done the work. He had the thesis. He had the conviction. But even he was undersizing relative to the quality of the opportunity. It took Soros, his mentor, to push him from large to truly enormous. And the result was over a billion dollars in profit in a matter of days.

The lesson is not that you should lever up to two hundred percent on every trade. The lesson is that even the best investors systematically undersize their highest-conviction positions. There is a gravitational pull toward mediocre sizing. Toward making every position roughly the same weight. Toward letting your best ideas get diluted by your eighth and ninth and tenth best ideas until your portfolio looks like an expensive index fund with higher fees and more stress.

Druckenmiller saw this clearly. He said, and I want you to hear this in his voice: "The greatest investors make large concentrated bets where they have a lot of conviction. They don't even try to have a lot of small bets." That is not a suggestion. It is a description of what separates generational wealth creators from competent analysts who compound at eight or nine percent. Which, I should note, is approximately where your CAGR sits right now.

[PAUSE — this is deliberate, not a criticism but a provocation]

Now, let me be careful here. I am not saying your eight point three percent CAGR is a failure. Over a full cycle that includes a minus forty percent year and a max drawdown of nearly fifty-five percent, compounding at eight percent with your capital intact and your process improving is respectable. But I am asking you to sit with an uncomfortable question. How much of that eight percent reflects genuine stock selection skill, and how much reflects a sizing regime that has systematically diluted your best ideas?

Your progressive exposure ladder runs from three percent test positions to six to eight percent on confirmation, up to ten to twelve on full conviction, with a theoretical ceiling of fifteen percent. That is a sensible framework. But I want to challenge you on how often you actually reach the top of that ladder. My suspicion, and tell me if I am wrong, is that most of your positions cluster in the six to eight percent range. That the jump from eight to twelve percent happens rarely. And that fifteen percent almost never happens at all.

If that is true, then your portfolio is not really concentrated. It is diversified with a concentrated flavour. And the returns reflect that.

[PAUSE]

Let me bring in Van Tharp here, because he gives us the mathematical language for what Druckenmiller does intuitively.

Tharp spent decades studying what actually drives portfolio performance. He ran studies, interviewed hundreds of traders, and built models. And his central finding was startling. Position sizing, not strategy, not stock selection, not market timing, accounted for ninety-one percent of the variability in portfolio performance across the traders he studied. Ninety-one percent. The strategy itself, the thing most people spend all their time optimising, accounted for a small fraction of the difference between good and great.

Tharp formalised this with the concept of R-multiples. R is your initial risk on a trade. If you buy a stock at a hundred and your stop or your mental floor is ninety-seven, your R is three. Three percent of the position value. Every outcome is then expressed as a multiple of that initial risk. If the trade works and you make twelve percent, that is a plus four R trade. If it fails and you lose three percent, that is a minus one R trade.

This is not just accounting. It is a completely different way of seeing your portfolio. Instead of asking "how much did I make on that stock," you ask "how many units of risk did I harvest or lose." And when you track your trades in R-multiples over time, you can calculate your expectancy. Expectancy equals your win rate multiplied by your average win in R, plus your loss rate multiplied by your average loss in R. That single number tells you more about your system than any individual trade result ever could.

[PAUSE]

Here is what concerns me about your process right now. Your system expectancy is unmeasured. You do not know your average R-multiple on winners. You do not know your average R-multiple on losers. You do not know your win rate expressed in R terms. You do not know your SQN, your System Quality Number, which is the square root of the number of trades multiplied by the ratio of mean R to standard deviation of R. Without these numbers, you are flying a sophisticated aircraft without an altimeter. You can see the horizon, you have good instincts, but you have no precise read on whether you are climbing or descending.

And this matters enormously for the sizing decision. Because if you knew your expectancy was, say, plus zero point five R with a win rate of fifty-five percent and an average winner of plus three R, you would know exactly how much to push when you see a high-conviction setup. You would have mathematical permission, not just intuition, to go from eight percent to twelve percent to fifteen percent. And you would have mathematical discipline to know when going above ten percent is not justified by the edge you are actually capturing.

Druckenmiller never used this language. He never talked about R-multiples or SQN scores. But he was doing the calculation intuitively every single day. He had an internal model of his own hit rate, his own magnitude of wins and losses, and he adjusted his sizing accordingly. The difference between Druckenmiller and most investors is not that he had a better model. It is that he acted on the model's implications with total conviction.

[PAUSE]

Let me talk about the other side of the coin now. Because Druckenmiller's genius was not only in pressing when he was right. It was in shrinking when he was uncertain.

He described it like this: "I believe in streaks, like in baseball. Sometimes you're seeing the ball, sometimes you're not. And my number one job is to know when I'm hot and when I'm not. When I'm hot, I need to turn the dial straight up. When I'm cold, the last thing I should do is make big bets to try to get even. I need to turn myself down."

This is profound, and it connects directly to where you are right now. You are in risk-off mode. Iran, the macro uncertainty, the sectoral rotation you have been mapping through those first, second, and third order effects. You have consciously pulled back. And Druckenmiller would approve of that. Absolutely. He lived by the principle that the way to build long-term returns is through preservation of capital and home runs. Not through grinding out small gains when the picture is unclear.

But here is the nuance, and this is the part I want you to carry with you. Pulling back does not mean going dormant. Druckenmiller's periods of low exposure were not passive. They were active. He was watching. He was building models. He was stress-testing theses. He was, in his words, grinding it out. Because he knew that clarity would return. A high-conviction trade would emerge. And when it did, he needed to be ready to turn the dial straight up, immediately, with total commitment.

The danger for you, and I say this with respect, is that your current risk-off posture slides into something more permanent. That the pipeline of ideas, which is excellent, and the analytical work, which is rigorous, does not translate into decisive action when the moment arrives. You generate ideas faster than you complete them. That is a known pattern. And in a risk-off environment, that pattern can be reinforced. You keep triaging, keep assessing, keep layering new names into the funnel, and the moment to press comes and goes because you are still in assessment mode.

[PAUSE — let this land]

Let me tell you about the year 2000 for Druckenmiller, because it is the most instructive failure in modern investing history. And it is instructive precisely because Druckenmiller did not lack knowledge. He lacked discipline.

In January 2000, Druckenmiller walked into Soros's office and said he was selling all the tech stocks. Everything. The valuations were absurd, trading at a hundred and four times earnings. He knew it was a bubble. He had the analytical clarity. He made the right call.

And then he reversed himself. By March 2000, he had bought six billion dollars of tech stocks. Six billion. Not because the thesis had changed. Not because new information had emerged. Because he could not stand watching the market go up without him. Fear of missing out. The need to play. In his own words, he was an emotional basket case and could not help himself.

In six weeks he lost three billion dollars. Three billion. And when asked later what he had learned, he said something remarkable. "I didn't learn anything. I already knew I wasn't supposed to do that."

[PAUSE — five seconds]

Sit with that. The greatest macro investor of his generation, a man with thirty years of no losing years, lost three billion dollars not because he made an analytical error but because he abandoned his own sizing discipline. He knew the right position was zero or short. He went massively long instead. The sizing decision was not wrong by a factor of two or three. It was wrong by infinity. He went from where he should have been, which was flat or short, to the maximum opposite direction.

The lesson is not about tech bubbles. The lesson is about the gravitational pull of action when the right move is inaction. And this connects to your world in a very specific way. You are currently running a pipeline with over a dozen names at various stages. MTU at triage. DCC with a full case file. Carlsberg in deep dive. Corbin, Fever-Tree, Flutter, DKSH, Xvivo, and more. The analytical work is excellent. The pipeline is rich. But the sizing decision on each of these is what will actually determine your returns over the next twelve months. Not whether you are right about the fulcrum driver for Fever-Tree's US expansion. But how much you put on if you are right, and how quickly you cut if you are wrong.

[PAUSE]

Let me bring Soros back in here for a moment, because his concept of reflexivity has a direct bearing on position sizing that is underappreciated.

Soros argued that markets are not just passive reflections of reality. They actively shape reality. Rising prices create confidence, attract capital, improve fundamentals, which drives prices higher still, until the reflexive loop breaks. Falling prices destroy confidence, impair access to capital, damage fundamentals, which drives prices lower still.

The implication for sizing is this: when you are right on a reflexive thesis, the position should get bigger as the thesis plays out, not smaller. Most investors take profits as a stock rises, trimming a winner back to its original weight. That is the instinct. It feels prudent. But in a reflexive loop, where the price movement itself is improving the fundamental picture, trimming is actually reducing your exposure to the highest-probability moment in the trade.

Druckenmiller understood this. When the pound trade was working, he did not trim. He pressed. When his best stock ideas were working, he added. Not recklessly. Not without analysis. But with the recognition that a position that is working, where the fundamental thesis is confirming and the reflexive loop is intact, deserves more capital, not less.

Now, this does not mean you should never take profits. There are structural reasons to trim, rebalancing, liquidity management, portfolio heat limits. But it does mean you should interrogate every trim decision with the question: am I reducing this because the thesis is weakening, or am I reducing this because the gain makes me nervous? If the answer is the latter, you are making a sizing error driven by emotion, not analysis.

[PAUSE]

Let me give you a practical framework that synthesises Druckenmiller's intuition with Van Tharp's mathematics. Think of it as a sizing checklist for your process.

First, define R for every position. Before you enter any trade, know your initial risk. Not in vague terms. In precise terms. If you buy a three percent test position in DKSH and your downside case is a fifteen percent decline from entry, your R on that position is zero point four five percent of portfolio. That is the unit of risk. Everything flows from it.

Second, track every exit in R-multiples. When you close or trim a position, express the outcome as a multiple of your initial R. Build a spreadsheet. Populate it religiously. Over six months, you will have enough data to calculate your expectancy, your win rate in R terms, and your SQN. These numbers will change everything about how you think about sizing.

Third, create explicit sizing tiers tied to conviction and evidence, not just conviction alone. Your current ladder of three, six to eight, ten to twelve, and fifteen percent is good in theory. But tie each tier to specific evidence thresholds. Three percent is the test, the hypothesis is interesting but unconfirmed. Six to eight percent means the fulcrum driver is confirmed by at least two independent data sources. Ten to twelve percent means the reflexive loop is active, the price action is confirming, and the fundamental trajectory is accelerating. Fifteen percent means you are seeing the ball, your recent R-multiples are positive, and this is one of the top two ideas in the portfolio.

Fourth, and this is the Druckenmiller insight, monitor yourself as much as you monitor the market. Know when you are hot and when you are cold. Track your recent R-multiples not just as a performance measure but as an input to your sizing algorithm. If your last five trades have been positive R, you have earned the right to press. If your last five have been negative, the correct response is to shrink, not to double down to recover. Druckenmiller applied this same logic to the people who worked for him. He put big bets on the traders he could see were on a winning streak, and he bet against those on losing streaks. He managed capital allocation to people the same way he managed his own position sizes.

Fifth, measure your portfolio heat. Portfolio heat is the total open risk across all positions, expressed as a percentage of capital. If you have ten positions each risking one R of zero point five percent of portfolio, your total heat is five percent. If you have five positions each risking one point five percent, your heat is seven point five percent. Druckenmiller never used this term, but he managed the concept obsessively. In uncertain environments, he dropped heat to near zero. In high-conviction environments, he pushed it aggressively. Your current risk-off posture should show in your heat number. If it does not, you are not actually risk-off. You are just telling yourself a story.

[PAUSE]

Let me address one more thing before I bring this to a close. The psychological dimension.

Druckenmiller's 2000 blowup was not an analytical failure. It was a psychological failure. He knew the right answer. He could not execute the right answer. And he was honest enough to say so. Most investors, when they make sizing errors, rationalise after the fact. They construct narratives about why the position was justified. Druckenmiller did not. He said, plainly, I already knew I was not supposed to do that.

That kind of honesty is rare and it is essential. Because the hardest part of the sizing decision is not the analysis. It is the execution under emotional pressure. The pressure to act when the right move is to wait. The pressure to trim when the right move is to hold or add. The pressure to diversify when the right move is to concentrate. The pressure to go big when the right move is to go small.

Van Tharp wrote about this extensively. He called position sizing the one aspect of trading that people most want a mechanical rule for and least want to actually follow. Because the mechanical rule will sometimes tell you to do things that feel wrong. It will tell you to go to fifteen percent in a name when the market is volatile and the news is frightening. It will tell you to cut a position that you love, that you have done months of work on, because the R-multiple has hit your stop and the discipline says out.

Druckenmiller's edge was not that he felt no emotion. He clearly did. The 2000 episode proves it. His edge was that for most of his career, the vast majority of the time, he subordinated emotion to the sizing discipline. He turned the dial up when he was hot and down when he was cold. He pressed the trades that were working and cut the trades that were not. And when he failed to do this, when emotion won, the consequences were catastrophic and immediate.

[PAUSE — let this settle]

After losing three billion in 2000, Druckenmiller took a four-month sabbatical in Africa. When he came back, he did not try to recover by swinging bigger. He went into US Treasuries, a quiet, high-conviction, macro trade with clear asymmetry. And he made forty percent in three months. That is the pattern. Catastrophic loss, not from bad analysis but from bad sizing. Recovery, not from revenge trading but from disciplined return to the framework. Quiet conviction. Appropriate size. Home run.

That is the rhythm Druckenmiller established over thirty years. Long periods of grinding, moderate sizing, capital preservation. Punctuated by moments of extreme concentration when the setup was right and he was seeing the ball. And the long-term result was the most consistent track record in the history of macro investing.

[PAUSE]

Let me connect all of this back to your specific situation right now, in early April 2026.

You are risk-off. Good. The Iran conflict, the macro fog, the sectoral uncertainty. Druckenmiller's first principle applies: preserve capital when the picture is unclear. Do not force trades. Do not feel guilty about low exposure. The capital you preserve today is the ammunition for the home run tomorrow.

But while you are preserving, do the work that enables aggressive sizing when clarity returns. Measure your system. Calculate your expectancy. Build the R-multiple log. Review your historical trades and express them in R terms. I suspect you will find that your wins have been modest in R terms, perhaps plus two to plus three R, while your losses on the worst trades, particularly 2022, were deep in negative R territory. That profile, moderate winners and occasional catastrophic losers, is the profile of a manager who sizes too uniformly. The same weight on the high-conviction names and the speculative ones. The solution is not better stock picking. It is wider dispersion in sizing.

And when the next high-conviction trade emerges, whether it is DCC breaking out of its case file, or Carlsberg hitting a catalyst, or MTU resolving to the upside after triage, the question is not do you own it. The question is do you own enough of it. Are you at six percent when you should be at twelve. Are you at twelve when the evidence supports fifteen. Are you letting your best idea be diluted by your seventh-best idea because it feels safer to spread the risk.

Druckenmiller would tell you, and Soros would tell you with even less patience, that spreading risk is not the same as managing risk. A portfolio of ten equally-weighted six percent positions is not less risky than a portfolio of three fifteen percent positions and seven two percent positions. It is differently risky. And the concentrated version, if the sizing reflects genuine conviction ranking, will produce higher returns with a better risk-adjusted profile over time.

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want you to remember from tonight. One line. One takeaway.

The sizing decision is the investment decision. Everything else is research.

When you sit down to assess a stock, you are doing research. When you build a financial model, you are doing research. When you read transcripts and talk to management and map the competitive landscape, you are doing research. All of that is necessary. None of it generates returns. Returns are generated at the moment you decide how much capital to allocate to the idea. That is the investment decision. And it deserves at least as much rigour, at least as much process, at least as much deliberate practice as the research that precedes it.

Druckenmiller knew this. Soros knew this. Van Tharp spent a career proving it mathematically. And now you know it too. Not intellectually, because you have always known it intellectually. But operationally. As a principle that demands action. Measure your expectancy. Track your R-multiples. Build the sizing discipline into your process with the same rigour you bring to your triaging checklists and your ESA frameworks. That is the work.

[PAUSE — five seconds]

Your single actionable takeaway for this week: build the R-multiple log. Go back through your last twenty trades, express each outcome as a multiple of initial risk, and calculate your system expectancy. One spreadsheet. Two hours of work. The most important two hours you will spend this quarter.

Good night, Richard. Sleep on it. We will talk in the morning.
