## Part One: Why We Do Not Quit

Let me start with the central argument from Duke's book *Quit: The Power of Knowing When to Walk Away*. She argues that human beings are systematically, structurally, biologically biased toward persistence and against quitting. Society celebrates grit. We tell stories about the entrepreneur who was rejected a hundred times before succeeding. We admire the marathon runner who finishes on a broken ankle. We say things like "winners never quit and quitters never win."

But Duke points out something that should be obvious once you hear it: for every story of someone who persisted and succeeded, there are a hundred stories of people who persisted and failed — catastrophically — because the same psychological forces that drove them through genuine difficulty also drove them through deteriorating situations where the rational choice was to stop. We just do not hear those stories, because nobody writes a bestselling memoir called "I Quit at the Right Time and Avoided Ruin."

[beat]

The skill is not grit or quit. It is knowing which is which. And that distinction, Duke argues, is the single hardest cognitive task in any domain where outcomes are uncertain and feedback is delayed. Which is to say: investing.

Here is the part that matters for you. Duke says the opposite of a great quitter is not a gritty person. The opposite of a great quitter is someone who will not make hard decisions when the data demands it. Your self-image — decisive, dynamic, action-oriented, the Finn Russell of stock pickers — should include quitting as a form of decisiveness. Not as a failure of conviction. Not as weakness. As action.

[with emphasis] Quitting a deteriorating position is action. Holding a deteriorating position is passivity disguised as conviction.

## Part Two: Kill Criteria — The Core Framework

Duke's most powerful concept for you is kill criteria. These are specific, observable, measurable conditions defined at the point of commitment — not at the point of pain — that specify when you will exit an investment. Every kill criterion combines two elements: a state, which is an objective, measurable condition like "eighteen-month forward consensus EPS falls five percent from peak" or "stock enters Stage 3 on the Trend Template," and a date, which is a deadline like "within thirty days of the trigger" or "before the next earnings report."

Now, why must they be set at the point of commitment? [slower] This is Duke's most critical insight and I want you to sit with it.

At the point of investment, you are rational. You are optimistic but clear-headed. You have done the analysis. You understand the four pillars. You have run the triaging checklist. You can define, calmly and precisely, what conditions would invalidate the thesis. You can imagine the bear case without flinching.

Six months later, when the stock is down twenty-five percent and your ego is attached, you cannot think clearly about exit conditions. The sunk cost of all your analytical work is dragging on you. The endowment effect is making you overvalue the position because it is yours. The status quo bias is making inaction feel safe. And the fear of future regret — what if I sell and it bounces — is paralysing your decision-making. In that state, you will rationalise. You will delay. You will hope. And hope, as Minervini says, is not a strategy.

The kill criteria bypass this impairment because they were set when your cognition was unimpaired.

Duke frames this as a Ulysses contract. Ulysses had his sailors tie him to the mast so he could not steer the ship toward the Sirens. The ropes were not there because Ulysses was weak. They were there because he was wise enough to know that his future self, hearing the Sirens' song, would be irrational. [PAUSE] The kill criteria are the ropes. They do not prevent you from hearing the biases. They prevent you from acting on them.

Duke's research shows that pre-commitment dramatically improves quit timing. When people define exit conditions in advance, they quit an average of forty to sixty percent earlier than when they make exit decisions in real time. Not because they quit too early — but because real-time decisions are systematically delayed by the bias triad.

## Part Three: The Bias Triad and FOFR

Let me now lay out the full psychological architecture of why you do not exit when you should. Duke identifies three biases that compound into what she calls the triad, and then a fourth force — FOFR — that sits on top and makes the whole thing nearly inescapable without pre-commitment.

First, sunk cost. The tendency to continue investing because of what has already been invested — time, money, emotional energy, analytical effort — rather than evaluating future prospects on their own merits. Your specific exposure here is enormous. You invest weeks of research into each position. Multiple Claude reports, AlphaSense deep research runs, IR calls, management meetings, detailed case files. That analytical investment creates a massive sunk cost that makes exiting feel like wasting all that work. Your own words on XVIVO capture it perfectly: "Learning all that stuff was trees not wood." The learning itself became a sunk cost that kept you invested.

Second, endowment effect. We value things more simply because we own them. Once you own a position, you psychologically overvalue it relative to alternatives — not because it is objectively better, but because it is yours. Duke extends this beyond physical objects to beliefs, ideas, and decisions. Your Love portfolio test — "every stock is loved here and now today, such that I would be happy if it was twenty-five percent of AUM" — is designed to combat endowment effect. But Duke would note the test itself is contaminated: you are asking whether you love something you already own, which triggers the endowment bias. The more rigorous version is the inversion: "If I did not own this stock today, would I buy it at the current price with the current information?" That inversion strips out the endowment premium.

Third, status quo bias. The preference for the current state of affairs. Exiting a position requires active effort and feels like introducing new risk. Holding requires no action and feels safe. Your concentrated portfolio of sixteen stocks, each deeply researched, creates a very strong status quo. Each position is a world of knowledge — case files, monitoring data, IR relationships, mental models. Exiting means abandoning all of that context. The temptation is always to give it one more quarter rather than face the cognitive cost of closing the file.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the critical point. These three biases do not just add. They multiply. You have spent six months analysing XVIVO — sunk cost. You own it — endowment. It is in the portfolio with a full case file and monitoring schedule and IR relationship in place — status quo. Now the CFO turns out to be bottom quartile and execution is faltering. Rationally, you should exit. But sunk cost says do not waste six months of work. Endowment says this stock is special, I understand it better than anyone. Status quo says changing the portfolio is risky, better to hold and monitor. The combined force is why your thirty-day shot clock defaults to exit, not reassess. The default must be action because the entire weight of the triad pushes toward inaction.

And then, on top of the triad, comes the force Duke considers the single most powerful psychological barrier to exits: Fear of Future Regret. FOFR. Minervini captures it in his own words: "The real fear that keeps people from selling is that you're going to sell it and it's going to turn around and go back up. Being wrong twice is a powerful psychological force."

Why is FOFR so powerful? Because the asymmetry of regret is brutal. Counterfactual regret — selling and watching the stock recover — is vivid and specific. You can picture it. You can feel the humiliation. Experienced regret — holding and watching the stock continue to decline — is diffuse and gradual. A little more each week. A slow burn. And slow burns are easier to tolerate than sharp pains, even when the cumulative damage is far greater. On top of that, acts of commission generate more regret than acts of omission. Selling is an act. Holding is passive. If the stock recovers after you sell, you blame yourself for the act of selling. If it continues to decline while you hold, you do not blame yourself as intensely because you did not do anything — the market did it to you.

Duke's antidote: pre-commitment strips FOFR of its power because the decision was already made. If your kill criterion says exit when consensus EPS falls five percent and it falls six percent, you do not have to decide to sell. The decision was made months ago, when you were calm and rational. There is no FOFR because there is no decision point where FOFR can operate. The criterion was met. The process executed. Just as you would not feel regret about stopping at a red light.

The playbook is evaluated across the distribution of outcomes, not on any single instance. Over twenty positions using the same process, what is the average outcome? That is the meaningful metric. But the key point for today's episode is this: the kill criteria protect you in both directions. When the criteria are not breached, you have structural permission to hold. You do not need to second-guess. You do not need to panic when the stock dips five percent on noise. The criteria say hold, so you hold. That is trusting the process, in Rotella's language. The analysis was done at entry. The kill criteria were set when you were rational. Now you are in execution mode. Trust the pre-commitment. Trust the ropes.

## Part Nine: Escalation of Commitment — The Ratchet

Duke identifies escalation of commitment as the mechanism by which small, rational-seeming decisions compound into large irrational outcomes. Each incremental decision to hold a little longer or give it one more quarter feels reasonable in isolation. But the cumulative effect is a position held far past any rational exit point.

The mechanism is rooted in prospect theory. At each decision point, you face a choice between exiting now and locking in a known loss, or holding and hoping for a recovery. Kahneman and Tversky showed that people are risk-seeking in the domain of losses — they prefer an uncertain chance of recovery over a certain loss, even when the expected value of holding is negative. This preference for uncertainty over certain loss means you systematically choose to hold, quarter after quarter, accumulating an ever-larger loss that becomes ever-harder to exit.

Duke adds a devastating observation: clearly defined goals, paradoxically, make escalation worse. If your goal is twenty-five percent IRR and a losing position threatens that goal, you are more likely to hold — because exiting would lock in the failure to meet the goal, while holding preserves the increasingly remote possibility of meeting it. The goal itself becomes a trap.

Kill criteria are the circuit breaker. They interrupt the escalation at the first step. Before the sunk cost accumulates. Before the endowment effect deepens. Before FOFR arrives. Before the ratchet locks.

## Part Eleven: Your Thirty-Day Shot Clock Through Duke's Lens

Your existing shot clock framework is strong. Duke's contribution is to make it earlier, more specific, and less discretionary.

Three enhancements. First: the shot clock currently starts when deterioration is flagged, which requires a judgement call about whether deterioration has occurred. That judgement call is precisely where endowment and sunk cost corrupt the process. Duke's kill criteria are defined at entry. The question is not "has this stock deteriorated enough to start the clock?" The question is "has this stock breached the kill criterion I defined before I owned it?"

Second: the current framework allows disproval. "Disprove the deterioration with specific, quantifiable evidence." Duke would say: a motivated reasoner can always find specific, quantifiable evidence. When you own a position and it is down twenty percent, your brain is extremely creative at finding reasons the thesis is intact. Kill criteria remove this discretion. The criterion is met or it is not met. There is no disprove option. There is only exit or criterion not yet met.

Third: immediate exit signals for certain triggers. Stage 4 — no shot clock, immediate exit. Cockroach sighting in a position already on the shot clock — immediate exit. CFO or CEO assessed bottom quartile — immediate exit. These are the signals where thirty days is too generous.

Duke would approve of the shot clock as a pre-commitment contract. She would push you to make the criteria more specific, set them at entry, make the default exit not reassess, and reduce the discretion. Because discretion is where the biases live.
