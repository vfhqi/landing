Let me ask you a question that sounds simple but isn't.

When you hold a position, what are you actually monitoring?

[PAUSE]

Most investors, if they answer honestly, would say: the fundamentals. Management execution. Estimate revisions. News flow. The narrative. And these are not wrong things to monitor. But Stan Weinstein spent the better part of forty years arguing — quietly, methodically, with overwhelming empirical evidence — that you are monitoring the wrong things first. Or at least: in the wrong order.

Weinstein's central claim is not that fundamentals don't matter. It is that price is a leading indicator of fundamentals, not a lagging one. Price leads by three to six months on average. Which means: by the time the fundamental deterioration is visible in the numbers you are monitoring, the stock is already in a different stage. The stage you should have exited three to six months earlier.

[beat]

Today is not about the four stages as an entry framework. You have covered that. Episodes twenty-three and twenty-four gave you the mechanics of Stage 1 and the 1→2 transition. Today is about something more advanced and more uncomfortable: using Weinstein's stage model as a full lifecycle management framework — from the moment you enter a Stage 2 position to the moment you should be well clear of it. And specifically: what the stage model tells you that your fundamental process cannot tell you until it is too late.

This is where Weinstein gets genuinely hard to apply. Not because the theory is complex. Because the theory is in direct conflict with what deeply analytical investors feel is legitimate data.

**Part One: The information hierarchy problem**

[normal pace]

Weinstein was a practitioner, not an academic. He ran a newsletter called the Professional Tape Reader from 1971 to 2011 — forty years of weekly market analysis, serving institutional and professional individual investors. He was not a momentum trader in the modern sense. He was a disciplined technician who believed that the price and volume action of a stock was the aggregate of all information possessed by all market participants — including information that was not yet public, not yet visible in filings, and not yet captured in analyst models.

This is not a mystical claim. It is an information processing claim. Institutional investors with better information, faster data, and superior analytical resources act before the information becomes public. Their buying and selling is visible in price and volume before the news is visible in earnings reports. Weinstein's stage model is essentially a translation tool: it converts the aggregate behaviour of better-informed market participants into an observable signal that any investor can use.

[with emphasis]

Here is the implication that most fundamental investors resist: in Weinstein's framework, the fundamental analysis you are most proud of is the last to know. Not because it is wrong — often it is right. But because the market has already priced the fundamental thesis before your analysis can confirm it. When the stock breaks above its 30-week moving average with expanding volume, Weinstein says: someone with better information than you is buying aggressively. You don't need to know what they know. You need to recognise what the price is telling you and act accordingly.

Stanley Druckenmiller, who produced one of the great track records of the twentieth century, articulates the same principle from the macro side. His most famous claim: "Earnings don't move markets. Earnings expectations, and changes to earnings expectations, move markets. It's the shift in the momentum of earnings expectations, not the earnings themselves." Which means: by the time earnings confirm your thesis, the easy money has already been made by whoever was positioned for the shift in expectations.

[beat]

For you specifically, this creates a particular tension. Your strategy is GARP plus event-driven special situations. The "event" — the catalyst that unlocks the value — is by definition a future event that is not yet in the price. That is the intellectual thesis. The question Weinstein would ask is: is the stock behaving as if the event is beginning to be priced? Is the Stage 2 structure intact? Is volume confirming accumulation rather than distribution?

When the answer is yes — when both the fundamental thesis and the technical structure are aligned — you are in the best of all worlds. The thesis gives you conviction. The stage confirms institutional agreement. Those positions tend to work.

When the answer is no — when the fundamental thesis looks intact but the technical structure is deteriorating — you have an information hierarchy problem. Something in the market's aggregate knowledge base is inconsistent with your thesis. Weinstein would say: trust the price, not the thesis. Because the market is almost always ahead of you.

BFF. In Stage 4. Fundamental thesis "still intact." You know how this ends.

**Part Two: The distribution stage — the most dangerous period you will ever hold a stock**

[slightly slower]

Let me spend real time on Stage 3. Not the mechanics. The psychology of being in a Stage 3 stock and not being able to see it clearly.

Stage 3 is Weinstein's distribution phase. The stock's advance has stalled. The 30-week moving average has gone from rising to flat. Volume is high on down days and low on up days — institutions selling into rallies, retail buying the dip. The stock makes lower highs. The base formations become irregular. The RS starts to drift.

None of this is catastrophic-looking in isolation. Each individual observation is ambiguous. The stock is "just consolidating." The "fundamentals are strong." The selling "could be tax-loss." The volume could be a one-off. There is always an explanation for why this particular price action does not mean what Weinstein says it means.

[PAUSE]

This is the stage where the analytical mind becomes the enemy of the portfolio.

Here is what is happening neurologically. Kahneman's confirmation bias is operating at full power. You are in a Stage 3 stock with a thesis you believe in. Every piece of information that is consistent with the thesis is registered and weighted. Every piece of information that is inconsistent — including the stage itself, which is perhaps the most information-dense signal available — is interpreted charitably, is bracketed as temporary, is explained away.

Howard Marks wrote about this directly in his "Race to the Bottom" memo — not about stage analysis, but about the same psychological phenomenon. He observed that investors at the late stages of a bull market are not ignorant of the risks. They are choosing to weight them differently because the recent experience of being rewarded for taking risk has restructured their probability assessments. The Stage 3 stock investor is doing the same thing at the position level: recent experience of the stock performing well has created a bias toward interpreting ambiguous signals as benign.

Marks's antidote is second-level thinking — asking not "is the thesis intact?" but "what does everyone who is still holding believe, and what would cause them to change their mind?" In Stage 3, the answer to the second question is often: nothing within the analytical framework can cause them to change their mind. Only price action can. Which is exactly what Weinstein said.

[with emphasis]

Let me be concrete about what Stage 3 looks like in your specific track record, because I want you to see the pattern not as an abstraction but as a recurring film.

BFF. The 30-week moving average flattened months before the fundamental deterioration was confirmed. Volume on down days exceeded volume on up days for an extended period. The RS drift began before the cockroaches became visible. The technical stage was signalling distribution while your analytical process was still accumulating evidence about whether the thesis remained intact. Those processes were operating in opposite directions, and the technical one was right.

Kainos. The signal failure archetype in full flower. Your own internal indicator reversed — a deliberate rule you had constructed to track the thesis. And what did you do? You re-analysed. You went back to the fundamentals to check whether the indicator might be wrong. You treated the technical signal as something to be evaluated by the analytical process, rather than the analytical process as something to be evaluated by the stage.

This is the inversion Weinstein spent his career fighting. Most fundamental investors treat price as data to be explained by analysis. Weinstein insists that analysis is a story to be corroborated by price. When the two diverge, price wins. Not always in the immediate term. But over a six-to-twelve-month horizon, the divergences almost always resolve in price's direction.

**Part Three: The 30-week MA as a portfolio circuit breaker — advanced application**

[normal pace]

You know the 30-week moving average. You know that a declining 30-week MA with the stock below it is Weinstein's definition of Stage 4. You know the rule: do not own Stage 4 stocks.

But here is the advanced application that episode twenty-three did not cover: using the 30-week MA as a systematic circuit breaker across the entire portfolio, not just at the individual stock level.

Weinstein argued — and this is in his chapter on managing portfolios that most summaries skip — that when more than half of your positions are below their 30-week moving averages and those averages are declining, you are in a portfolio-level environment that demands a fundamentally different stance. Not just stock-by-stock discipline. Portfolio-wide risk reduction.

[beat]

This has direct relevance to your experience in the first quarter of 2022. You have documented what happened in extraordinary detail. The portfolio entered 2022 at sixty-seven percent concentration in growth names — XVIVO, Kainos, S4, Medartis, Instalco, Fasadgruppen. Within eight weeks, the portfolio was down dramatically. You described it as being "snookered with liquidity" — illiquid small-caps in a risk-off environment where selling created its own adverse price action.

Weinstein would have read the portfolio-level stage signal in November 2021, if not earlier. The growth indices had already begun their rolling bear market. The macro backdrop — rising rates, reversing liquidity — was the mirror image of 2020's conditions. The 30-week MAs on your European growth names were either flattening or beginning to decline. Not all of them. Not catastrophically. But the portfolio-level signal was deteriorating for months before the P&L damage arrived.

Your documented lesson from 2022 was correct: "The whole portfolio needs to be reviewed regularly as a portfolio. Not just position by position." Weinstein would have added: and the review needs to use price and stage as primary data, not just fundamental analysis of each position in isolation.

Van Tharp's portfolio-level R-multiple framework makes the same point in a different register. The expected value of the portfolio is the aggregate of each position's R-multiple expectancy. But the R-multiple for a Stage 3 or Stage 4 position is negative by definition — you are holding a position where the probability-weighted outcome is a loss. Even if the fundamental thesis is intact, the stage reality means the market is distributing, not accumulating. Your edge has temporarily inverted.

[slower]

Here is the advanced Weinstein insight that almost nobody articulates: stage analysis is not primarily about entry timing. It is about information sequencing. The stage tells you who has the information advantage right now. In Stage 1, you have the information advantage — the stock is basing while you are doing the fundamental work that will precede the institutional discovery. In Stage 2, you and the institutions are aligned — the thesis is being confirmed in both the fundamental data and the price action. In Stage 3, the institutions have regained the information advantage — they are distributing based on something you do not yet know. In Stage 4, the information disadvantage is severe and the distribution is complete.

The question for any holding is: which information environment are you in? And the only honest way to answer that question is through price and volume. Fundamentals tell you what you know. Price tells you what someone else knows.

George Soros makes this point through his reflexivity framework, though coming at it from the opposite direction. His observation is that in financial markets, the act of believing something affects the thing believed in — share prices affect management decisions, analyst coverage, access to capital, employee morale, customer confidence. In Stage 4, the falling price itself creates the fundamental deterioration that eventually confirms the technical signal. The price was "right" before the fundamentals showed it because the falling price was part of the causal chain that produced the fundamental deterioration.

For BFF specifically: the falling share price reduced management's incentive alignment, increased institutional attention on governance, reduced the quality of management candidates who would join, and began to attract short-seller scrutiny. The Stage 4 reality created the conditions that made the Stage 4 technical signal self-fulfilling. Weinstein would have been unsurprised. This is how distribution phases typically resolve.

**Part Four: The hardest exit — selling a declining winner you still believe in**

[slightly slower]

Let me go somewhere harder. Not Stage 4 exits — those are hard psychologically but straightforward mechanically. Let me talk about selling a position that is clearly in Stage 3, where the stock was a significant winner for you, the fundamental thesis feels intact, and the current price still represents a meaningful gain from your entry.

This is the most costly mistake in Weinstein's framework — not holding Stage 4 losers (everyone knows that's wrong) but holding Stage 3 former-winners through the distribution phase. Because the emotional dynamics are completely different. With a Stage 4 loser, you are fighting loss aversion and the reluctance to crystallise a loss. With a Stage 3 former-winner, you are fighting the satisfaction of being right, the temptation to hold for even more gain, and the narrative that you understand this company better than the market does.

[PAUSE]

Avanza is the relevant case from your track record. Seventy-two months. 170 basis points. Your best position by a significant margin. And you are currently — or were recently — in an environment where European fintech and neo-banking names face real headwinds: rate normalisation reducing net interest margins, competitive intensity increasing, valuation compression.

I am not making a call on Avanza. But I want you to think about how you would know if Avanza were in Stage 3. What would that look like? What would you observe? And if you observed it — if the 30-week MA flattened, if the RS started to drift, if volume on down days started to exceed volume on up days — what would you do?

The question matters because your most successful positions are the ones where Weinstein's framework is hardest to apply. You have enormous identity investment in Avanza as a Connoisseur hold — a quality compounder that you held through multiple tests of conviction. Selling it because of technical deterioration, while the fundamental thesis feels intact, would feel like betraying the entire investment philosophy that produced the position in the first place.

[with emphasis]

Weinstein would say: you have a quality compounder archetype tendency that creates systematic exit blindness at Stage 3. The very reason you are good at identifying and holding quality compounders is the reason you will be systematically late to exit them. The intellectual capability that lets you see through short-term noise — the patience and analytical rigour that turns a three-percent position into a twenty-percent position over seventy-two months — is exactly the capability that will rationalise holding a Stage 3 former-compounder through the distribution phase.

This is not fixable by better analysis. It is fixable by adding a non-discretionary technical tripwire to the exit process for your highest-conviction positions. Not to replace the fundamental view. To compete with it on equal terms.

The tripwire does not need to be complex. Weinstein's original formulation: if the 30-week MA turns down after a sustained advance, reduce by fifty percent regardless of fundamental view. If the stock breaks below the declining 30-week MA with volume, exit the remaining position and rebuild only on a Stage 1 base resolution.

William O'Neil translates this into his own language: the exit rules for your best stocks are the most important, not the least important. Because your attachment to them is highest and your objectivity is lowest.

**Part Five: Sector rotation and the context you cannot ignore**

[normal pace]

One Weinstein dimension that gets relatively little attention in most summaries is his insistence on sector analysis before individual stock analysis. Not as an afterthought. As a prerequisite.

Weinstein's hierarchy: first, determine the overall market stage. Then, identify which sectors are in Stage 2. Then, within those sectors, find the individual Stage 2 stocks. The point is that a Stage 2 stock in a Stage 4 sector is fighting a very powerful headwind — institutional rotation out of the sector will drag even the best individual names.

[beat]

This is directly relevant to your current portfolio context. Your current risk-off positioning — recurring non-cyclical revenues, domestic EU footprint, banks, insurance, cables, healthcare — is Weinstein-compatible sector rotation in practice, even if you have not used his framework explicitly to derive it. You are avoiding the sectors (European chems, consumer discretionary, beverages) that the Iran conflict and rate environment are putting into Stage 3 or Stage 4 at the sector level.

But here is the advanced application: the sector rotation analysis should be continuous and forward-looking, not reactive. Weinstein would argue that you can often see the sector-level stage deterioration three to six months before the macro thesis that explains it becomes obvious. The European rate-sensitive growth sectors began their Stage 3 formation in late 2021, months before the inflation narrative became consensus. The sector-level technical signal was available to anyone looking at price and volume, before the fundamental narrative hardened.

Your RS Dashboard — the one you built with 1,392 stocks, sixteen industries, seventy-five sectors — is the Weinstein sector analysis tool made operational. The question is how systematically you are using it at the sector level rather than only at the individual stock level. If a sector's RS is declining on a twelve-week view and the sector's 30-week MA is rolling over, that is a Stage 3 sector signal that should affect how you size every position within it.

[slowly]

The most powerful application: use the sector RS analysis to set a prior before you start individual stock analysis. If the sector is in Stage 3, every individual stock within it is carrying an adverse sector headwind. This does not make the individual stock uninvestable — there are always sector leaders that outperform relative to a declining sector. But it should affect your sizing. A Stage 2 stock in a Stage 3 sector gets 50-60% of normal conviction sizing. A Stage 2 stock in a Stage 2 sector, in a Stage 2 market, gets full conviction sizing.

This is Van Tharp's concept of "market type" applied at the sector level. The expected R-multiple of any position is partially a function of the market and sector environment, not only of the individual stock's characteristics. Position sizing that ignores the sector environment is leaving risk on the table in the wrong direction — not being compensated for the sector headwind you are absorbing.

**Part Six: The discipline of the 30-week MA as a psychological anchor**

[slower]

I want to end with something Weinstein says toward the end of his book, in a passage that rarely gets quoted but that I think is the most personally relevant part of his entire framework for you.

He writes: the most important function of the 30-week moving average is not as an entry or exit signal. It is as an anchor against the natural human tendency to substitute story for data. The story is always more compelling than the moving average. The story is vivid, specific, emotionally resonant. The moving average is abstract, mechanical, unemotional. Under cognitive load and emotional pressure, the story wins every time — unless you have pre-committed to a rule that makes the moving average non-negotiable.

[PAUSE]

That is the real value of Weinstein's framework. Not that it is always right. It is not. There are false Stage 3 signals. There are stocks that look like they are in Stage 3 and then resume Stage 2 advances. Weinstein acknowledges this. But the rule — follow the MA, not the story — is not intended to be right every time. It is intended to ensure that when you are wrong, you are wrong small. And when you are right, you are right with maximum capital deployed.

Which is exactly Minervini's formulation: the system is designed so that your mistakes cost small and your wins pay big. No single mistake is catastrophic. The aggregate of correct process decisions compounds over time.

For you: the 30-week MA on your key positions is a fact, available every week. The story about your key positions is a narrative you construct, updated selectively, filtered through your analytical lens and your emotional state. In a direct competition between fact and narrative — in a calm, analytical moment — the fact wins easily. Under pressure, with a significant position in distress, the narrative wins almost automatically.

The discipline is to make the competition structural rather than volitional. Not: "I will pay attention to the 30-week MA when I'm feeling clear-headed." But: every Thursday, the 30-week MA status of every position above five percent is reviewed in writing, and any position in Stage 3 or Stage 4 has its kill criteria formally evaluated. Full stop.

That is not extra work. It is eighteen months of position management reduced to twenty minutes per week. And it is the single process change most likely to prevent the next BFF.

**The single actionable**

[slowly, deliberately]

For every position you currently hold above five percent of the portfolio: look up the 30-week moving average status today. Not the price versus a target. Not the current fundamental picture. Just: is the 30-week MA rising, flat, or declining? And is the current price above or below it?

Make a simple table. Four positions in Stage 2 — rising MA, price above it. Two in ambiguous territory. One in Stage 3.

Then for the one in Stage 3: what is your current plan? Not the fundamental thesis. What is the specific, pre-defined, written plan for when to exit, based on stage progression and not on fundamental disappointment?

[beat]

If you do not have that plan written down, write it today.

The market will tell you what it knows before your analysis can confirm it. The question is whether you are listening.

**Part Seven: The information embedded in volume — Weinstein's second instrument**

[normal pace]

Most discussions of Weinstein focus on the moving averages. Understandably — the 30-week MA is his most elegant and most useful tool. But Weinstein is equally insistent on volume as a second, independent instrument that confirms or challenges the stage diagnosis.

His volume rules are simple but demanding. In a healthy Stage 2 advance, volume should expand on up weeks and contract on down weeks. This pattern reflects the underlying institutional behaviour: accumulation during advances, reduced selling during pullbacks. When the volume pattern reverses — when volume expands on down weeks and contracts on up weeks — the institutional behaviour has shifted from accumulation to distribution. Stage 3 has begun, even if the price is still nominally near its high.

[beat]

This volume inversion is typically visible three to six weeks before the price confirms the Stage 3 diagnosis through a declining 30-week MA. That three-to-six-week window is your advance warning. And it is almost invisible to an investor who is monitoring the fundamentals and interpreting price action only through the lens of "is the thesis intact?"

Soros's reflexivity framework adds a dimension here. In a distribution phase, the volume pattern that Weinstein identifies is itself part of the reflexive loop: institutional selling creates price softness, which creates uncertainty among retail holders, which reduces buying pressure, which reduces volume on up days, which confirms the pattern Weinstein describes. The pattern is self-reinforcing because human behaviour is self-reinforcing. The volume signal is not predicting what will happen. It is describing a process that is already underway.

[with emphasis]

For your portfolio monitoring: a weekly volume scan across all positions above five percent is a small additional check that dramatically improves your stage diagnosis accuracy. You do not need precise figures. You need the directional check: are the biggest volume weeks the up weeks or the down weeks? Over the last eight weeks, which side is winning?

O'Neil integrates this into his CAN SLIM system as a variant of the same principle. "Up volume should exceed down volume by a ratio of at least two-to-one in a healthy advance." His observation was that the ratio inversion — more volume on down days than up days — preceded earnings misses and guidance cuts by an average of six to eight weeks in his dataset. The institutions are selling ahead of news that has not yet been made public. Volume is the earliest available signal of that shift.

Your current risk-off posture — and the sector analysis that drives it — should include the volume dimension for each current holding. Not as a definitive signal. As a confirming or disconfirming data point alongside the moving average stage diagnosis. When both the MA and the volume pattern agree — when the 30-week MA is flattening and volume is expanding on down weeks — the Stage 3 signal is significantly stronger than either indicator alone.

**Part Seven coda: The 30-week MA as emotional anchor — the advanced psychological contract**

[slower]

Let me end with the most advanced psychological application of Weinstein's framework. Not the mechanics. The contract.

The 30-week MA is most useful not as a trading signal but as a pre-commitment device. Before you enter any new position, you write down what the 30-week MA will need to show for you to consider the stage intact. You define, in advance, what a stage deterioration signal looks like for this specific position. You make a written commitment: if the 30-week MA turns down from a declining 150-day MA and the stock is below it for two consecutive weeks with expanding down-volume, I will reduce by at least fifty percent.

This is not a stop-loss. Stop-losses create their own perverse incentives — they can be moved, rationalised, overridden by the fundamental view. This is a stage-based pre-commitment that is grounded in the question "what is the market telling me about who has the information advantage?" rather than "how much am I willing to lose?" The psychological contract is different. You are not accepting a loss. You are acknowledging an information asymmetry.

[PAUSE]

The investors in Jack Schwager's Market Wizards who reference stage-type analysis consistently — and there are many, including Tudor Jones, Druckenmiller, and O'Neil — share one characteristic in how they describe their relationship with price action. They do not fight the tape. Not as a rule imposed on them. As a deeply held belief that price carries information they do not have, and that fighting price is equivalent to arguing with the aggregate of a thousand better-informed adversaries.

Weinstein's framework is the simplest operationalisation of that belief. The 30-week MA is not a number. It is a statement about the current balance of information between you and the market. When the stock is above a rising 30-week MA, the market is agreeing with your thesis. When the stock is below a declining 30-week MA, the market is disagreeing with your thesis — and the market is usually disagreeing with better information than you have.

The wise response to that disagreement is not to argue harder. It is to listen more carefully.
