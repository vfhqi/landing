## The Monkey and the Pedestal (6 min)

Duke has a framework she calls "the monkey and the pedestal." It's deceptively simple.

Before you build anything—a house, a company, an investment thesis—there's a hard part and an easy part. The hard part is the part that, if you can't solve it, the whole thing falls apart. The easy part is the part you can keep polishing forever.

In carpentry, the hard part is the foundations and load-bearing walls. The easy part is the paint and the mouldings. You can spend weeks choosing the perfect paint colour. Doesn't matter if the foundations are weak. The house collapses.

In investing, the hard part is the exit. The easy part is the research.

Think about that for a second. [PAUSE] You can research a stock until you're absolutely certain about the intrinsic value. You can build a beautiful model. You can cross-reference every claim against three different databases. And none of that matters if you can't answer one simple question: when would I sell?

That's the monkey. That's the load-bearing problem. And if you haven't solved it before you commit capital, all the beautiful research is just polishing the pedestal. You're making the easy thing prettier while the hard thing remains unsolved.

Let me give you a concrete example from your own book.

You've held XVIVO for years. The research is extraordinary. You understand the ACH reimbursement regime. You know the customer base. You have opinions on competitive dynamics. But when XVIVO was added to your portfolio, did you write down: "If X happens, I will sell, and here's why"?

I'm guessing not. I'm guessing you had a sense of the company. You had conviction. You had a model. But you didn't have kill criteria. And because you didn't have kill criteria—because you hadn't solved the monkey—every time the stock moved, every time there was news, you had to decide again. You had to re-evaluate. You had to use willpower.

That's not investing. That's wheel-spinning. That's emotional labour you could have eliminated at entry.

Here's what Duke would tell you: the moment you identify an investment idea, your job is not to research harder. It's to ask: What is the hard part here? What would invalidate this thesis? And then write that down.

If the hard part is CFO execution, your kill criterion might be: "If the CFO misses guidance by more than 10% two quarters in a row, I exit."

If the hard part is the ACH reimbursement regime changing, your kill criterion might be: "If CMS guidance suggests a material reduction in reimbursement rates, I sell within 30 days."

If the hard part is market structure, your kill criterion might be: "If a competitor enters the space with a materially superior product, I exit within 60 days."

See? You're identifying the monkey—the load-bearing problem—and you're writing down exactly what signal would tell you the monkey is broken. You're not polishing the pedestal. You're solving the hard thing.

And once you solve it, you have freedom. [PAUSE] Because the decision is no longer in your hands. It's in the market. The market will tell you if your kill criterion has been met. And when it is, you execute. No deliberation. No second-guessing. No emotional tax.

That's the principle. And it applies to every position in your book.

## Kill Criteria: The Death of Ambiguity (7 min)

Let me define kill criteria precisely, because it matters.

A kill criterion is a specific, observable, pre-committed condition that, if met, triggers an exit. It has three components. First, it's specific. Not "things seem bad." Not "I feel worried." Something observable. Something measurable. Something you could verify in five seconds if you looked at the data.

Second, it's pre-committed. You write it down before you enter. Not after six months when you're already emotionally invested. Before. When you're thinking clearly. When your System 2—your analytical mind—is in control.

Third, it's actionable. It's something the market can reveal. A miss in guidance. A regulatory change. A competitive product launch. Not something like "if my conviction declines," because that's internal and squishy. Something external. Something verifiable.

Pre-commitment is the magic ingredient here. Daniel Kahneman wrote about this. You have two systems of thought. System 1 is fast, intuitive, emotional. It feels. System 2 is slow, analytical, deliberative. It thinks. In the moment of a stock crisis, System 1 takes over. You feel fear. You feel loss aversion—you feel losses roughly twice as intensely as equivalent gains. Your judgment becomes irrational.

But if you've already decided, in advance, when you'd sell—back when you were calm and thinking clearly with System 2—then System 1 doesn't matter. The decision is already made. You just execute.

You already understand this principle in one context. You invented it. "Hell yeah or no." That's a kill criterion. It's pre-committed. If a new idea doesn't trigger a hell-yeah feeling, it's a no. You don't add it to the watchlist. You don't deliberate. The decision is already made. You just execute.

Keywords is the example. You have kill criteria for those kinds of ideas. They're crystal clear. You've pre-committed to what you're looking for. So when something doesn't meet that standard, you don't agonise. You move on.

The version 2.0 is applying the same discipline to positions you've already entered.

Duke talks about a concept called "resulting." Resulting is evaluating a decision based on its outcome rather than its process. This is where most investors go wrong. A decision is good or bad based on whether it was the right call given the information at the time. Not whether it worked out.

Imagine you sell a position because your pre-written kill criterion says you should. The stock recovers a month later and doubles. Was your exit a bad decision? No. Your exit was correct. You executed on a pre-committed criterion. You followed a good process. The fact that the outcome worked against you is irrelevant. That's luck, not judgment.

Conversely, imagine you hold a position because you've got a gut feeling. No kill criteria. Just hope. The stock happens to work out. Was your hold a good decision? No. You got lucky. Your process was sloppy. You had no framework. You were emotionally reactant.

This is critical because it means you can't evaluate your investing by looking at P&L. You have to evaluate it by looking at decisions. Did you follow your system? Did you execute your pre-written criteria? Or did you wing it?

If you're winging it, you're not investing. You're gambling with a research department.

Here's a concrete frame for writing kill criteria. Use this template.

"If [specific observable signal], I will sell my position in [stock] within [timeframe]."

Let's work through a few real examples.

Written down. Pre-committed. So it's not dependent on your emotional state in the moment. It's dependent on a framework you've already decided on.

## Quitting is Not Failure—It's Information Processing (5 min)

Here's a belief that's probably costing you millions: quitting is failure.

That's wrong. Duke has spent two decades studying decision science, and she'll tell you: quitting is not failure. Quitting is information processing. Quitting is seeing new data and updating your thesis. Quitting is recognizing that the conditions that made an investment idea attractive have changed.

The alternative—holding on to positions because you've already invested time and money and mental energy—that's the sunk cost fallacy. And it's not virtue. It's error. It's letting past decisions constrain future choices. It's letting what you've already paid dictate what you should do next.

Duke has a great thought experiment. Imagine you're walking down the street and you find a ten-pound note on the ground. Do you pick it up? Of course. Now imagine you bought a stock two years ago and you've lost 30% of your money. Would you buy it today at this price? Probably not. If the answer is no, you should sell. Because the only relevant question—the ONLY relevant question—is: given what I know now, would I start this? Not: did I start this? Not: how much have I already paid? Now. Given current information. Would I start?

If the answer is no, you sell.

This is where pre-written kill criteria become so powerful. Because they short-circuit the sunk cost thinking. They say: I already decided. I'm not re-evaluating based on my emotional attachment. I'm executing on a pre-written system.

You know the pain of sunk costs. You've written about it. BFF cost you years because you kept hoping that the next quarter would clarify things. You'd already invested so much thought that you couldn't let go. KAINOS was the same. The indicator flashed red, but you'd already spent so much time on the thesis that quitting felt like waste.

But here's the reframing: quitting would have freed you. Quitting would have freed capital. It would have freed attention. It would have freed emotional energy. Those freed resources could have gone to better ideas.

The pre-mortem is Duke's tool for pre-committing to this frame. Before you enter a position, imagine it's 18 months from now. The investment has failed. The stock is down 40%. Why? Write it down. Write down five to seven reasons why this could fail.

Those reasons are your kill criteria. Not all of them. But some of them will become your triggers. "If management changes, I exit." "If the regulatory environment shifts, I exit." "If a competitor emerges with superior technology, I exit." These are the things that would make you wrong. If they happen, you exit. You don't hold and hope. You execute and move on.

## System 2 Thinking: The Leverage Point (4 min)

Kahneman talks about two systems. System 1 is automatic. It's intuitive. It feels. System 2 is deliberative. It requires effort. It thinks.

In the moment of a stock crisis, System 1 dominates. You feel fear. You feel regret. You feel loss aversion. Your judgment is clouded. You can't think clearly. That's not a character flaw. That's neurology. That's how humans are built.

But here's the leverage point: you can use System 2 before the crisis. You can think clearly when there's no emotional pressure. You can write down exactly when you'd sell. And then when the crisis comes, System 1 can scream all it wants, but System 2 has already decided. You just execute.

This is why pre-commitment is so powerful. It's not about willpower. It's about decision architecture. You're designing a system that doesn't rely on willpower in the moment. You're designing a system where the decision is already made.

Think about Minervini for a moment. His entire system is built on this principle. Seven to eight percent stop-loss. Mechanical. No deliberation. The stop fires, and you move on. Is that a constraint? No. It's liberation. Because the moment you enter the position, you know exactly what will happen if it goes wrong. You don't have to decide under emotional pressure. You just execute.

You're not as mechanical as Minervini. That's fine. But you can still borrow the principle. You can still pre-commit. You can still use System 2 thinking in advance to override System 1 thinking in the moment.

Here's the key insight: every stock in your portfolio right now contains an implicit System 1 decision. You bought it because you felt conviction. You hold it because you don't feel strong enough discomfort to sell. That's System 1 running the show. And System 1 is not reliable under pressure.

The move to System 2 is simple: write it down. "Here's my kill criterion for this position. If it's met, I sell." Once you've done that, you're operating from a framework. You're not relying on willpower. You're relying on architecture.

## The Reframing: Freedom, Not Constraint (4 min)

Here's the core reframe that Duke makes, and it matters.

Kill criteria feel like constraints. More rules. More restrictions. One more thing you have to follow. One more way to be wrong.

That's backwards. Kill criteria are freedom.

[PAUSE] Think about what you're doing right now. You hold positions. You don't have pre-written exit criteria. So every time the stock moves, every time there's news, every time the market context shifts, you have to re-decide. You have to re-evaluate. You have to deliberate under emotional pressure. That's not freedom. That's servitude. You're a slave to the news cycle. You're a slave to your own uncertainty.

Kill criteria liberate you from that. They say: I've already thought about this. I've already decided. Here's what would make me wrong. Here's when I'll exit. And then you can actually relax. You can stop deliberating. You can stop second-guessing. You can stop paying emotional rent on positions that no longer serve you.

That's freedom.

In fact, here's a deeper frame: the positions without clear kill criteria are the positions running your life. They're the ones you're constantly re-evaluating. They're the ones that keep you up at night. They're the ones that consume your attention. BFF consumed years of your attention because you never pre-committed to an exit. You just kept hoping the next quarter would clarify things.

The positions with clear criteria—the ones where you've pre-committed—those are actually the most relaxing positions. Because you know exactly when you'd sell. And until that criterion is met, you can actually think about something else.

That's not weakness. That's leverage. That's using system design to reduce the emotional tax of decision-making.

Duke puts it simply: the hard part is not knowing when to quit. The hard part is knowing that you can quit, and trusting that quitting is the right call. Kill criteria solve both problems. They tell you when to quit. And they remove the emotional tax from quitting, because you've already decided.

## The Monkeys and Pedestals Redux: A Checklist (4 min)

Let me give you a systematic way to think about this for your whole portfolio.

For every position, ask: Have I solved the monkey?

The monkey is the load-bearing assumption. The thing that, if it breaks, the thesis breaks. Here are some common monkeys in your portfolio.

For CARLB: Is it the margin accretion? Is it the acquisition target universe? Is it the current valuation relative to peers? Identify the monkey. Then ask: What signal from the market would tell me the monkey is broken? Write that down. That's your kill criterion.

For CRBN: Is the monkey energy demand? Is it regulatory capture? Is it execution risk? Identify it. Then ask: What would the market reveal that would tell me I'm wrong?

For DKSH: You just flagged this for triage. Before you even do deep work on DKSH, identify the monkey. What's the load-bearing assumption? Once you know that, you can write the kill criterion in advance. And then as you do your research, you're explicitly testing that hypothesis. You're not building a beautiful case study that avoids the hard part.

Here's the pattern. For each position in your book, ask three questions.

One: What is the hard part here? What assumption is load-bearing?

Two: What specific market signal would tell me that assumption is broken?

Three: If I see that signal, will I commit right now to selling within [timeframe]?

If you can answer those three questions for every position, you've solved the problem. You have kill criteria. You have pre-commitment. You have freedom.

If you can't answer those three questions, you're polishing the pedestal. You've got beautiful research and no exit plan.

## The Deep Frame: This is About Virtue (2 min)

Let me end on something bigger.

Kill criteria sound like a technical tool. Write down when you'll exit. Mechanical. Systematic. And it is that. But it's also something else. It's about virtue. It's about integrity. It's about being someone who keeps their word—even to themselves.

When you write down that you'll exit if [criterion], you're making a promise. A promise to yourself. A promise that you're not going to let emotions override analysis. A promise that you're not going to let sunk costs trap you. A promise that you're going to value your time and capital more than your ego.

That's hard. It's actually harder than making the money in the first place. Because making money is about being clever. Exiting is about being honest.

But here's the payoff: once you're someone who keeps their word to themselves, you're someone who can be trusted. You're someone with integrity. And integrity, in investing, is rare. Most investors rationalize. Most investors hold losers too long. Most investors let ego drive decisions. You won't be that person.

You'll be someone who says, "I have kill criteria. I set them in advance. I execute them when they're met. That's not ambivalence. That's discipline."

And discipline is how you survive. It's how you win. [PAUSE] Not the dramatic wins. The quiet, consistent wins that come from a clean process.
