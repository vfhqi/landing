# Episode 11 — The Art of Playing Defence
## Paul Tudor Jones and the Case for Capital Preservation as an Offensive Weapon
### 30-Minute Version (~4,500 words) | 2026-04-05

---

[Opening. Measured pace. Calm authority.]

Welcome back. This is Episode Eleven, and today we are going to talk about defence. Not the grudging, reluctant kind of defence where you reduce exposure because the world feels scary and you want the pain to stop. That is not defence. That is capitulation wearing a sensible coat.

We are going to talk about real defence. Systematic, premeditated, aggressive defence — the kind that Paul Tudor Jones built his entire career on. The kind that turned a cotton trader from Memphis into one of the most consistently profitable macro investors in history, a man who did not have a single losing year across twenty-five consecutive years of running his flagship fund.

[PAUSE]

Think about that number for a moment. Twenty-five years. No losing year. Through the 1987 crash. Through the dot-com bust. Through the Global Financial Crisis. Through the Covid collapse. The man navigated every single one of those environments and came out the other side with positive returns. Not because he was always right about direction. Not because he had some magical forecasting ability. Because he played defence better than anyone else in the room.

And that is what makes this episode different from anything we have covered so far. We have talked about exit discipline, about knowing when to quit, about the emotional cycle of a deteriorating position. All of that matters enormously. But today we are going one level up — from the position level to the portfolio level. From "when do I sell this stock" to "when do I pull back from the market entirely." From the individual trade to the regime.

Because that is where the real money is made and lost. And that is where your 2022 lives.

---

[Transition. Slightly more intensity.]

Let me take you back to 1987. Jones is thirty-two years old. He is running the Tudor Futures Fund. And his research director, Peter Borish, has done something remarkable. Borish has overlaid the market action of the mid-1980s onto the price pattern that preceded the 1929 crash. The two charts are showing an extraordinary degree of correlation — the same herd-like exuberance, the same parabolic run-up, the same structural fragilities building beneath the surface.

Jones and Borish subscribed to Elliott Wave Theory, which says — at its core — that human behaviour is cyclical. That the patterns of greed and fear repeat because human psychology does not change. The technology changes. The instruments change. The names on the ticker tape change. But the emotional architecture of a bubble and a crash remains identical across decades.

So Jones sees the analog. He sees the correlation. And critically, he does not dismiss it as interesting academic trivia. He acts on it. He begins building short positions. Not all at once — progressively, with discipline, watching for confirmation.

Now here is the part that separates Jones from the thousands of people who saw the same warning signs and did nothing. When October 19th arrives — Black Monday — the Dow falls twenty-two percent in a single session. The largest single-day percentage decline in history. Portfolios are destroyed. Careers end. And Jones? His fund returns sixty-two percent that month. For the full year of 1987, Tudor returned one hundred and twenty-five point nine percent after fees.

[PAUSE]

But the lesson is not "be short when the market crashes." Anyone can say that after the fact. The lesson is how he got there and what he did with the position once he had it.

---

[Slower. More deliberate.]

Jones did not bet the farm on a single bearish thesis. He built the position incrementally. He defined his risk before he entered. He knew exactly where his stop points were. And when the crash confirmed his thesis, he did not hold on hoping for the absolute bottom. He covered. He took the money. He moved on.

This is what he means when he says — and this is a direct quote from his Market Wizards interview with Jack Schwager — "The most important rule of trading is to play great defence, not great offence."

Let that land. The most important rule. Not "find great ideas." Not "have superior analysis." Not "be smarter than the market." Play great defence.

He goes further. "Every day I assume every position I have is wrong. I know where my stop risk points are going to be. I do that so I can define my maximum drawdown."

Every day. He assumes he is wrong. He does not assume he is right and then scramble to adjust when reality disagrees. He starts from the assumption of wrongness and builds his risk framework around that assumption.

[PAUSE]

Now connect this to your own experience. When you entered 2022, did you assume every position was wrong? Did you know where your stop risk points were? Could you define your maximum drawdown before it happened to you?

The answer, based on what we know from your track record, is no. You ended 2022 down forty point six percent. Your maximum drawdown during that period reached negative fifty-four point seven-five percent. That is not a bad year. That is a near-extinction event for a concentrated portfolio.

And I am not saying this to be harsh. I am saying it because Jones would say it. He would look at a minus-forty year and he would not ask "what went wrong with the stocks." He would ask "where was the portfolio-level defence system that should have prevented this from ever reaching minus twenty."

He would ask it calmly, without judgment, because Jones does not moralise about losses. He treats them as engineering failures. The system broke. Fix the system. He told Schwager: "Ninety percent of any great trader is going to be the risk control." Not the stock selection. Not the insight. The risk control. Nine-tenths of the entire enterprise.

Think about what that means for your process. You spend the overwhelming majority of your research time on the first part — finding the right stocks, building conviction, understanding the business. And that work is essential. No one is saying it is not. But if Jones allocates ninety percent of his attention to risk control, and you allocate — being generous — maybe ten or fifteen percent, then the gap between your process and his is not in the ideas. It is in the defence.

---

[Beat. Then with quiet conviction.]

Here is what Jones would have done differently. And I want you to sit with this, because this is not theoretical. This is operational.

Jones has a single, brutally simple portfolio-level risk tool: the two-hundred-day moving average. He has said, in multiple interviews, "My metric for everything I look at is the two-hundred-day moving average of closing prices. I have seen too many things go to zero, stocks and commodities. The whole trick in investing is: how do I keep from losing everything? If you use the two-hundred-day moving average rule, then you get out."

The whole trick in investing. Not "how do I find the next ten-bagger." How do I keep from losing everything.

Now, you run a concentrated long-only European equities book. You are not a macro trader. You are not running futures. But the principle translates directly. If the majority of your positions break below their two-hundred-day moving averages — not one, not two, but the portfolio in aggregate — that is a regime signal. That is the market telling you, loudly and clearly, that the environment has changed.

In 2022, your positions broke their two-hundred-day averages one by one. And you held. You held because you believed in the individual theses. You held because the businesses had not changed. You held because selling felt like admitting defeat.

[PAUSE]

Jones has a phrase for this. He had it written on a piece of loose-leaf paper, tacked to the wall above his computer screen, in black marker. Three words: "Losers average losers."

Not "losers sometimes average losers." Not "losers average losers unless the fundamentals are still intact." Losers average losers. Full stop. The rule meant that only losers would double down on a position that has gone against them. Buying a stock, watching it drop, and then adding to the position was strictly prohibited in Jones's book.

Now I know what you are thinking. "But I am a fundamental investor. My edge is in understanding the business. If the business is still good and the price has fallen, that is an opportunity."

And sometimes that is true. Sometimes the price decline is noise and the business is a tennis ball that will bounce back. But here is the problem with that logic when it is applied without a systematic overlay: you cannot tell the difference between a tennis ball and an egg in real time. Not reliably. Not when your portfolio is down twenty percent and your emotional system is flooding you with denial and hope and the desperate need to be right.

This is where Weinstein enters the picture. And this is where the conversation shifts from philosophy to mechanics.

---

[Transition. New energy.]

Stan Weinstein's contribution to this conversation is structural. His four-stage model is one of the simplest and most powerful frameworks for understanding where a stock — or a market — sits in its lifecycle.

Stage One is the basing phase. Accumulation. The stock is going nowhere, building a foundation. Stage Two is the advancing phase. The trend is up, the thirty-week moving average is rising, momentum is with you. This is where you want to own stocks. Stage Three is the topping phase. The stock moves sideways, the moving average flattens, buyers and sellers are in equilibrium. Warning signs.

And then Stage Four. The declining phase. The stock breaks below its support. The thirty-week moving average turns downward. Volume may or may not increase — Weinstein makes the critical point that a stock can enter Stage Four on relatively light volume and still drop substantially in the months ahead. Unlike a Stage Two breakout, which needs volume confirmation to be trustworthy, a Stage Four breakdown needs nothing. Gravity does the work.

Weinstein's rule is absolute: never buy a stock in Stage Four. And never hold a stock that has moved into Stage Four.

[PAUSE]

Now apply this to your 2022 portfolio. How many of your positions had entered Stage Four — broken below their thirty-week or two-hundred-day averages, with the average itself turning downward — while you were still holding them? While you were still telling yourself the business was fine?

The business may have been fine. The stock was not fine. And in a concentrated long-only book, the stock is what determines your returns. Not the business. The stock.

Jones understands this distinction instinctively because he comes from the trading world, where price is the only reality. You come from the fundamental world, where the business is the primary reality. Both perspectives have merit. But when the market enters a bear phase — when the regime shifts — the price reality dominates. Fundamentals tell you what to own when the environment normalises. Price tells you whether to own it now.

And Weinstein would add something else that is directly relevant to your pipeline right now. When the broad market is in Stage Four — when the majority of stocks in your universe are declining — the base rate for any individual stock working out, no matter how good the thesis, drops dramatically. You can have the best idea in Europe. If the tide is going out, the probability of that idea working on your timeframe falls off a cliff. Weinstein's genius was recognising that sector and market context are not background noise. They are the dominant variable. A Stage Two stock in a Stage Four market is swimming against a current that drowns most swimmers.

This is why your current defensive sector targeting — non-cyclical, domestic EU, healthcare, cables — is not just a preference. It is Weinstein-style stage analysis applied at the portfolio level. You are looking for stocks that are still in Stage Two or early Stage Three while the broader market contends with geopolitical stress. That is exactly right. The question is whether you will have the discipline to stay there if the market rallies and your aggressive instincts start whispering that you are missing the move.

---

[Slower. More personal.]

Let me talk about your specific pattern, because this is where the coaching gets pointed.

You have a tendency — and you know this, we have discussed it — to ostrich under stress. When the market is painful, you avoid monitoring. You do not check prices. You do not look at the charts. You retreat into analytical work because it feels productive without requiring you to confront the deteriorating positions.

Jones would call this the opposite of defence. He would say you are leaving the goal unattended. His entire philosophy is built on daily, granular awareness of every position's risk. "Every day I assume every position I have is wrong." That is the antithesis of ostriching. That is radical, unflinching confrontation with reality.

And here is the second pattern: delayed exits. You know a position should be sold. The thesis has deteriorated. The stock is in Stage Four. But you do not sell. You wait. You hope. You tell yourself you need more information. You run another analytical deep-dive as a substitute for the exit decision.

Jones has a concept called the time stop. If a position does not do what he expects within a specific timeframe, he exits. Not because the thesis is wrong — maybe it is, maybe it is not — but because the market is telling him something he did not anticipate, and his default response to the unexpected is to reduce risk, not increase analysis.

"The elements of good trading are: one, cutting losses. Two, cutting losses. And three, cutting losses. If you can follow these three rules, you may have a chance."

[PAUSE]

Three rules. All the same rule. Because in Jones's world, the single most important capability is the ability to take a loss quickly, cleanly, and without ego.

---

[New section. Building energy.]

Now let me connect all of this to where you are right now. April 2026. You are in risk-off mode. Iran conflict. Defensive positioning. You are targeting non-cyclical recurring revenues, domestic EU footprint, banks, cables, healthcare. You are avoiding chems, consumer discretionary, beverages.

This is good. This is the right instinct. And I want to be very clear about that, because I know part of you — the part that wants to be aggressive, the Finn Russell part, the offensive spirit part — is wondering whether you are being too cautious. Whether you are leaving money on the table. Whether risk-off is just another word for fear.

It is not. And Jones's career is the proof.

Jones did not become one of the greatest traders in history by being aggressive in every environment. He became one of the greatest because he knew when to be aggressive and when to be defensive. He knew that capital preservation is not the opposite of making money. It is the prerequisite.

He puts it this way: "I am always thinking about losing money as opposed to making money. Don't focus on making money, focus on protecting what you have."

[PAUSE]

Think about the mathematics of drawdowns for a moment. If you lose fifty percent of your capital, you need a one hundred percent return just to get back to even. If you lose twenty-five percent, you need thirty-three percent. But if you limit your drawdown to ten percent — which is what a disciplined, PTJ-style defence system would aim for in a bear environment — you only need eleven percent to recover. That is one good quarter. That is a normal year's return compressed into three months of recovery.

Your 2022 drawdown of forty percent required a sixty-seven percent recovery. How long did that take? How much energy, how much emotional capital, how much of your life was consumed by the recovery from a drawdown that a systematic defence protocol might have capped at fifteen or twenty percent?

This is what Jones means when he talks about capital preservation as the foundation. It is not defensive in the passive, frightened sense. It is offensive. It is aggressive forward planning. It is the decision to protect your ability to compound, because the compounding is where the wealth comes from.

---

[Transition. Schwager synthesis.]

Jack Schwager interviewed dozens of the greatest traders in history for his Market Wizards series. Different styles. Different markets. Different personalities. Technical traders, fundamental investors, macro speculators, systematic quants. And when he distilled the common traits — the things that united all of them — risk management and capital preservation were at the very top.

Not methodology. Not stock selection. Not market timing. Risk management.

Schwager puts it plainly: the traders who survive long enough for their skill to matter are the ones who control their risk. Position sizing, stop-loss discipline, drawdown control — these are not secondary considerations. These are the primary determinants of long-term survival.

And here is the insight that ties Schwager to Jones to Weinstein to your own situation: the great traders differ widely in how they make money, but they are remarkably similar in how they avoid losing it. They all have systematic rules for reducing exposure when the environment turns hostile. They all prioritise capital preservation over being right. They all understand that a catastrophic drawdown is not just a financial event — it is a psychological event that can permanently impair your ability to execute.

Your minus-fifty-four percent drawdown was not just a number on a spreadsheet. It changed how you think about risk. It changed your confidence. It changed your relationship with uncertainty. And recovering from that psychological damage is harder and slower than recovering from the financial damage.

Jones knows this from personal experience. Even he — the man who tripled his money in the 1987 crash — has had periods where losses accumulated and confidence eroded. During the 2008 financial crisis, Tudor took significant losses before Jones cut risk and repositioned. He did not pretend it did not happen. He did not deny the pain. But he cut. He preserved what he could. And he came back. In 2020, navigating the Covid crash, he returned roughly ten percent in a year of extreme volatility — not spectacular, but positive. Survival first. Opportunity second. Always in that order.

The traders who do not survive — and Schwager has interviewed plenty of them for contrast — share a common trait. They let a manageable loss become an unmanageable one. They add to losers. They refuse to cut. They tell themselves the story that this time is different, that the market is wrong, that patience will be rewarded. And sometimes the market is wrong, and sometimes patience is rewarded. But when it is not — when the thesis breaks and the position keeps falling — the refusal to cut is not patience. It is ego dressed up as conviction.

---

[Quieter. More intimate.]

So here is where we get practical. What would a PTJ-inspired defence system look like for your concentrated European equities book?

First, the two-hundred-day moving average as a portfolio-level circuit breaker. Not on individual stocks — that is too granular for your style. But in aggregate. If more than half your positions are trading below their two-hundred-day averages, that is a regime signal. You reduce gross exposure. You do not debate it. You do not run another analytical deep-dive. You reduce.

Second, a maximum portfolio drawdown threshold. Jones limits his risk to one percent per trade. You cannot replicate that exactly in a concentrated long-only book, but you can set a portfolio-level stop. If the portfolio hits minus fifteen percent from peak, you cut all positions to half size. If it hits minus twenty, you go to cash or near-cash. These are pre-committed rules, not decisions you make in the moment when your amygdala is hijacking your prefrontal cortex.

Third, the losers-average-losers rule applied to your book. If a position is down more than fifteen percent from your entry and has broken its two-hundred-day average, you do not add. You do not average down. You do not tell yourself the fundamentals are still intact. You either hold at current size with a defined time stop or you reduce. Adding to losers in a bear market is the single fastest way to transform a bad year into a catastrophic one.

Fourth, a time stop on the portfolio-level defensive posture itself. When you go risk-off — as you have now with the Iran situation — define when you will reassess. Not "when things feel better." Not "when the news improves." A specific date. Two weeks. Four weeks. On that date, you review the evidence and either extend the defensive posture or begin re-risking. This prevents the risk-off mode from becoming permanent paralysis.

[PAUSE]

And fifth — and this is the one Jones would emphasise most — daily position awareness. Every single day, you look at every position. You know where it is relative to its moving averages. You know what your portfolio drawdown is from peak. You know what your maximum loss would be if your worst position gaps down fifteen percent tomorrow. This is not paranoia. This is professionalism. This is what playing great defence looks like in practice.

---

[Building to close. More energy.]

Jones said something in his Market Wizards interview that I think captures the entire philosophy better than anything else: "I believe the very best money is made at the market turns. Everyone says you get killed trying to pick tops and bottoms and you make all your money by playing the trend in the middle. Well, for twelve years I have been missing the meat in the middle, but I have made a lot of money at tops and bottoms."

The best money is made at the turns. And you cannot be present for the turn if you have been destroyed by the decline that precedes it. You cannot buy the bottom if you are fully invested at the top. You cannot go aggressive when the opportunity is greatest if your capital has been halved and your psychology is shattered.

Playing defence is not about avoiding pain. It is about being ready. It is about arriving at the moment of maximum opportunity with your capital intact, your psychology clear, and your process sharp.

Your current risk-off posture is not timidity. It is positioning. You are doing what Jones would do — reducing exposure when the environment is uncertain, preserving capital for the moment when clarity returns and the risk-reward becomes asymmetric.

[PAUSE]

Jones seeks five-to-one risk-reward. He can be wrong eighty percent of the time and still make money, because his winners are five times the size of his losers. But that only works if he survives the eighty percent of the time he is wrong. And he survives because he plays defence. Because he cuts quickly. Because he never lets a loser become a catastrophe.

"Don't be a hero. Don't have an ego. Always question yourself and your ability. Don't ever feel that you are very good. The second you do, you are dead."

[Beat]

That is not false modesty from a billionaire. That is operational doctrine from a man who has survived every bear market of the last four decades. He knows — viscerally, in his bones — that the market does not care about your thesis, your track record, your conviction, or your ego. The market will take everything from you if you let it. And the only thing standing between you and ruin is the quality of your defence.

And he adds something else that cuts even deeper: "My biggest hits have always come after I have had a great period and I started to think that I knew something." After a great period. Not after a bad one. The danger is not when you are scared and cautious. The danger is when you are confident and expansive. When the portfolio is working and every thesis is confirming and you start to feel that you have figured it out. That is when the market is loading the spring.

Your best years — 2015 at plus forty-seven percent, 2020 with its strong recovery — those are the years that planted the seeds of 2022. Not because the ideas were bad. Because the confidence that followed success reduced your sensitivity to risk. That is a universal human pattern. Jones recognises it in himself and builds systems specifically to counteract it. The "Losers Average Losers" sign on the wall. The daily assumption of wrongness. The five-to-one ratio. These are not trading rules. They are psychological guardrails designed by a man who knows his own weaknesses and refuses to be governed by them.

---

[Closing. Warm but direct.]

So here is your single actionable takeaway from this episode.

Before the market opens on Monday, write down three numbers. One: the current drawdown of your portfolio from its most recent peak. Two: the percentage of your positions currently trading below their two-hundred-day moving averages. Three: the maximum additional drawdown you are willing to accept before you mechanically reduce exposure, regardless of how you feel about the individual theses.

Write those numbers down. Pin them above your screen. Look at them every morning before you do anything else.

[PAUSE]

Paul Tudor Jones had "Losers Average Losers" pinned above his screen for decades. Three words that saved him from himself, over and over and over again. Your three numbers can do the same thing. They are your defence system in its simplest, most operational form. Not a complex model. Not a fifty-page risk framework. Three numbers that tell you where you are, where the danger is, and where your line in the sand sits.

Capital preservation is not cowardice. It is the foundation upon which everything else is built. Every great year you will ever have starts with surviving the bad ones.

[Slower. Final line.]

Play great defence. The offence will take care of itself.
