## [3:00–8:30] Bassham's Core Architecture: Self-Image Drives Performance

Let me set the stage with Bassham's core insight. He built a framework called Mental Management, and it has three layers. Think of them as concentric circles, each one feeding into the next.

The outermost circle is **Conscious**. This is what you think about deliberately. Your analysis. The criteria you write down. The rules you've created for yourself. The kill conditions. This is the analytical layer. You're very good here.

The next circle inward is **Subconscious**. This is the layer that actually runs the show when you're not thinking about it. It's your habits, your instincts, your reflexes. It's the part of you that acts without deliberation.

And at the very centre—the core—is **Self-Image**. This is the deepest layer. It's your fundamental belief about who you are as an investor. It's not a rule. It's not a goal. It's an identity.

[PAUSE]

Now here's where the power is. These three layers have to be in alignment. If they're not, you'll fail. You'll have a conscious rule that says "I exit when the thesis breaks." But your subconscious is still running the old pattern: "Wait and see. Maybe it'll turn around." And underneath that, your self-image is whispering: "I'm someone who holds positions. That's my style. That's like me."

The conscious rule loses every time. The subconscious and self-image are stronger.

So if you want to change your behaviour—really change it, not just white-knuckle your way through—you have to change the self-image. Because Bassham proved this over and over. Your performance will always equal your self-image. You can't sustain behaviour that contradicts who you believe you are.

[PAUSE]

The good news: you already know how to change your self-image. You've done it before. Every time you say "that's like me," you're reinforcing the identity. Every time you picture yourself doing something well, you're writing it into the self-image. Bassham called this mental rehearsal. And it works.

So the question isn't: "Can I learn to take losses?" You already can. The question is: "Can I build a self-image that says this is who I am?"

The answer is yes. And we're going to wire that today.

## [8:30–16:00] The Seven Principles: From Theory to Action

Bassham's Mental Management system rests on seven principles. I'm going to walk through them because each one is a lever you can pull.

**First principle: Take control of what you picture.** This sounds obvious, but it's not. Most people let their environment, their emotions, their past experiences control what they picture. When you think about cutting a position, what do you see? A loss crystallized. A mistake admitted. If that's what you picture, your subconscious will resist. It'll find reasons to hold, to wait, to hope. Instead, you need to deliberately picture what you want to happen: the deterioration signal arrives, you check your kill criteria, the criteria are breached, you execute the exit calmly, and you feel relief. Not regret. Relief. That's what you picture. Over and over. Until it's what your subconscious expects.

**Second principle: Give commands positively.** Don't think "I need to stop holding losing positions." Think "I am a disciplined investor who exits when the thesis breaks." Don't say "I shouldn't average down on red flags." Say "I add only to positions with strengthening conviction." The subconscious responds to what you're moving toward, not what you're avoiding. So your affirmations have to be directive, present tense, and framed as identity, not as aspiration.

**Third principle: Trust your subconscious.** Once you've done the mental rehearsal, once you've built the new picture into your self-image, your subconscious will execute it. You don't have to think about it. You don't have to white-knuckle. You'll see the deterioration signal, and your body will know what to do. Your subconscious will check the criteria. It'll count the breaches. And when the condition is met, you'll act. Not because you forced yourself. Because it's who you are.

**Fourth principle: Performance is what you consciously picture.** This is critical. You can't outperform your mental image. If you picture yourself holding too long, you'll hold too long. If you picture yourself exiting too early because of impatience, you'll exit too early. But if you picture yourself executing clean exits—executing the criteria, honouring the thesis breaks—that's what you'll do. Consistently. Without effort.

**Fifth principle: Performance always equals self-image.** This is the lynchpin. You already know this is true. Look at your exits. Look at your entries. Look at your hold discipline. Your performance is a perfect mirror of your self-image. It always is. So if you want to change your performance, you have to change the mirror.

**Sixth principle: You are responsible for changing your self-image.** Not luck. Not the market. Not a better methodology. You. This is the one that matters most, because it means you have control. No one can do this for you. But once you accept that you're responsible, you realize you have power. You can rewire yourself. You can build the identity you want.

**Seventh principle: Think, talk, and write about what you wish to happen.** Don't rehearse failures. Don't tell yourself stories about your losses that reinforce the old self-image. Instead, when you journal, when you debrief, focus on the exits you nailed. The Corbion exit. The Protector exit. The times you honoured your kill criteria and felt clean about it. Build your mental cookie jar of good exits. That's the raw material of the new self-image.

[PAUSE]

These aren't abstract principles. They're levers. And you can pull every single one of them.

## [24:30–27:30] The Mental Rehearsal Practice: 60 Seconds Daily

Here's the practice. It's simple. It takes 60 seconds.

Every morning, before you check the markets, before you read the news, you do one mental rehearsal. You don't need all of them. Pick one recent exit or imagine one future exit. Picture it in vivid detail.

The deterioration signal arrives. Maybe it's a missed earnings guidance. Maybe it's a margin compression that wasn't in your model. Maybe it's a competitive threat that's crystallizing. Whatever it is, the signal is there. You see it. Your mind is clear.

[Slower]

You open your kill criteria document. The one you wrote when you built your thesis. You check each criterion. One by one. Is this criterion breached? Yes. And this one? Yes. And this one? The tally is clear. The thesis condition is met. Deterioration has occurred. The exit condition is active.

You execute the exit. You don't hesitate. You don't hope. You don't average down. You exit. Calmly. You feel relief. Not regret. Not loss. Relief. The thesis is broken. The position is closed. You're clean.

And as you picture this—really vividly, really slowly—you say internally: "That's like me. I take losses cleanly. I honour my criteria. That's who I am."

[PAUSE]

Sixty seconds. Every morning. Do that for two weeks and you'll feel the shift. Your subconscious will start to expect the exit as a normal, natural move. It'll stop resisting. It'll start coordinating with the conscious rule. And your self-image will start to embed: "I am a disciplined investor who exits when the thesis breaks."

Do it for a month and it becomes automatic.
