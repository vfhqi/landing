## PART 2: RESULTING — THE MASTER BIAS [~4 minutes]

Resulting is Duke's term for the cognitive error of judging a decision by its outcome rather than by the quality of the decision process at the time you made it. It's the single most corrosive bias in decision-making, and it's endemic to investing.

Duke's poker illustration makes it concrete. You hold pocket aces — best possible hand. You go all-in. Your opponent calls with seven-deuce offsuit — worst possible hand. The flop comes seven-seven-deuce. You lose. Was your decision to go all-in bad? No. It was mathematically optimal. The outcome was unlucky. But the feeling — the visceral experience of losing with the best hand — makes your brain want to code the decision as wrong. That's resulting.

[PAUSE]

The investing parallel is exact. Your BFF thesis was sound. Four pillars checked out. The governance risk wasn't foreseeable from public information. But the outcome was bad. Minus 30%. Your brain now codes the decision as wrong. Not as "good process, bad luck," but as "I made a mistake." And when you code it that way, you start trying to fix the thing that isn't broken — your analytical process. You become more cautious. You second-guess your four-pillar framework. You add more due diligence, more confirmatory bias hunting, more skepticism toward your own conviction.

Meanwhile, the actual problem — your exit discipline — gets ignored because you just got punished for being wrong, and you're not prepared to be wrong again, so you tighten up your entry standards instead of tightening up your exit standards.

Duke's diagnosis is brutal: your brain conflates good outcomes with good process and bad outcomes with bad process. So your learning loop is corrupted. You learn the wrong lessons from every experience.

Here's a concrete example. Your telecom trade — you're proud of it. "Plus thirty-four percent versus plus four percent and could've been another ten to twenty percent easily." You code this as success. The playbook worked. You executed. And in a sense, you did — the outcome was positive. But Duke would ask: was the process that produced plus thirty-four percent genuinely different from the process that produced BFF's minus thirty percent? If both were sound research followed by size based on conviction, then the difference is luck, not skill. The telecom trade was lucky. The BFF trade was unlucky. Both may have been well-researched. Coding the telecom as "the playbook works" and BFF as "I made an error" is resulting in both directions.

[PAUSE]

Why this matters: if you keep learning from outcomes instead of processes, you corrupt your feedback loop. You end up reinforcing the wrong behaviors and undermining the right ones. You become gun-shy on entry when the real problem is on exit. You tighten analytical standards when you should be loosening time constraints.

Duke's prescription is deceptively simple. Evaluate every decision on process quality alone. Not outcome. Process.

Ask: Given what I knew at the time. Given my process at that moment. Given the information available to me before I invested. Was this a reasonable decision?

If yes, the outcome is irrelevant to the quality assessment. You made a good decision. You just got unlucky.

If no, the outcome is also irrelevant. You made a bad decision. You just got lucky this time, and eventually, bad process catches up.

[PAUSE]

This is the philosophical foundation. You need it to even begin to think clearly about exits.

## PART 3: THE 2×2 MATRIX — DECISION QUALITY VS OUTCOME QUALITY [~3 minutes]

Duke presents a framework that looks simple but cuts to the heart of why you're struggling.

Think of every decision as a cell in a two-by-two matrix.

On one axis: outcome. Good outcome, bad outcome.

On the other axis: decision quality. Good decision process, bad decision process.

Four cells.

**Good decision, good outcome.** That's deserved success. Telecom trade, if your process was sound. You should feel good about this. You executed well and got lucky to be right.

**Good decision, bad outcome.** That's bad luck. BFF, if your process was sound at entry but you got blindsided by governance risk. Your process was right. Your outcome was wrong. This is the cell where learning matters most — because you need to protect the good process from being corrupted by bad luck.

**Bad decision, good outcome.** This is the dangerous cell. You made a poorly researched, emotionally driven bet. And it worked. You made money. Your brain codes it as skill. You do it again. And again. And eventually, bad process catches up with you at scale.

**Bad decision, bad outcome.** That's deserved failure. You made a poor decision. You got unlucky. This is the cell where you do learn — but it's also where loss aversion creates the most dangerous behavior, which is holding on hoping for recovery instead of accepting the loss and moving on.

[PAUSE]

Now here's the insight that matters to you. Your documented pattern of holding losers too long — BFF, XVIVO, Goodwin — suggests you're living in the bad decision, bad outcome cell while simultaneously trying to deny you're there.

You made a decision (the research was thorough, so it feels good). You own the position (endowment effect). The outcome is now bad (down twenty, thirty percent). Your brain is torn. You can't admit the decision was bad, because the research was good. So you rationalize. "The thesis hasn't changed. The macro is noisy. Give it one more quarter." You're trying to rewrite yourself out of the bad decision, bad outcome cell by pretending the outcome isn't as bad as it looks.

But Duke's framework forces clarity. If the outcome is bad, you're in the bottom half of the matrix. Period. The question is: which half?

If the research process was good, the thesis was sound, the time horizon hasn't elapsed, but something fundamental just broke (Consob file on Max, CFO revealed as bottom quartile, trend completely inverted), then you're in good decision, bad outcome. Accept the loss. Move to the next idea. Your process was right. This was just luck.

If the research was thin, you were overconfident, the fundamentals weren't as solid as you told yourself, and now it's down thirty percent, then you're in bad decision, bad outcome. This is harder to accept. But the correct response is the same: accept the loss. Move on. Don't compound the error by holding hoping for recovery.

But if you can't see which cell you're in — if the outcome is bad but you can't let go of the process — then you're stuck in limbo. And limbo is where capital gets destroyed.

[PAUSE]

## PART 4: KILL CRITERIA — THE ENGINEERING FRAMEWORK [~5 minutes]

So how do you fight back? Not with willpower. Willpower is unreliable, especially when the position is down twenty percent and your amygdala is flooding your system with loss aversion signals.

You use engineering.

Annie Duke's central answer is kill criteria. Specific, observable, measurable conditions defined before you invest that specify when you will exit. Not at the point of pain. Before the pain exists.

Think about how Minervini operates. Before he places a single share, he knows exactly where he's getting out if he's wrong. The dollar amount. The percentage. He's already accepted the loss. He's written it down. It becomes a non-negotiable rule.

Duke extends this logic across your entire thesis. Not just price stops. But fundamental invalidation signals. Operational red flags. Time-based cutoffs. All defined at entry.

There are three categories.

**Quantitative kill criteria** are the clearest. Numbers on a dashboard. A stock breaks below its two-hundred-day moving average — you're underweight by fifty percent within twenty-four hours. EBITDA margins compress by more than one hundred basis points — immediate review to exit. Insider selling accelerates to more than ten percent quarterly — exit the full position. These are mechanical. No judgment required.

Your RS and Breadth Dashboard already tracks many of these measures. The Minervini Trend Template is a quantitative kill criteria framework expressed as price behavior. You already have the infrastructure. The problem is that infrastructure gets overridden by conviction.

**Qualitative kill criteria** are more nuanced but still specific. Not vague feelings. Specific observable conditions. For XVIVO, the kill criteria might have been: "CFO assessed as bottom quartile or below median for capability — exit immediately." Not "if management quality deteriorates." That's vague. "Bottom quartile on CFO capability — measurable, specific, exit."

For BFF, it might have been: "ACH threshold breaks above three days, reduce by fifty percent. Five days, exit full position." Or: "Governance red flag from regulatory body — reduce immediately." You wrote these lessons after the fact. Pre-commitment means writing them before.

**Time-based kill criteria** are your shot clocks. Your 30-day shot clock for new investments is one of these. You define it at entry: "I have thirty days to see earnings confirmation or thesis validation. If I don't see it, I reduce to testing position." This is brilliant. It forces periodic re-evaluation instead of drift.

[PAUSE]

The power of pre-commitment is this: you're making the hard decision while you're calm and rational, before the position is down twenty-five percent. Your conscious mind is clear. Your self-image hasn't been threatened yet. The emotional fog hasn't descended. This is when you can think with clarity.

Then, when the stock is down and every fiber of your being is screaming "hold on," you don't have to make a decision. You just have to follow the rule you already made.

Here's the concrete process.

When you enter a position, you write a one-page case memo. Thesis. Time horizon. Four-pillar checklist. And then: kill criteria.

"I will exit if any of the following become true:

1. Two-hundred-day moving average breaks.
2. CFO leaves or is assessed as below median capability.
3. Revenue growth decelerates below X percent for two consecutive quarters.
4. Insider buying reverses to insider selling.
5. Thirty days pass without confirmatory fundamental data.

Any of the above, and I initiate a full exit process within two business days."

You write it down. You put it in the case file. And then you don't have to be strong. You just have to follow the rule.

[PAUSE]

Mark Minervini's system is perfectly aligned with this framework. The Trend Template is a quantitative kill criteria screen. The Stage Analysis gives you the structure for when to hold (Stage 2) and when to exit (Stage 3 distribution, before Stage 4 decline). The seven-to-eight percent maximum loss rule is a price-based kill criteria.

Combine Duke's kill criteria framework with Minervini's mechanical rules, and you've engineered a system where quitting happens automatically because the data demands it, not because you had to summon willpower.

## PART 5: THE THREE BIASES THAT SABOTAGE EXITS [~3 minutes]

Duke identifies a triad of overlapping biases that specifically sabotage exit decisions. I want to name them explicitly because they're operating in your portfolio right now, on multiple positions.

**Sunk cost fallacy.** You've invested capital. You've invested time. You've invested emotional energy in building the thesis. Your brain tries to be helpful and says: if I sell now, all of that becomes a crystallized loss. So it's better to hold and hope it comes back, so the sunk cost doesn't feel real.

But here's the truth: the sunk cost is already real. It doesn't matter whether you hold or sell. The capital is gone. The time is gone. The only thing that matters is: given what I know now, where should the next unit of capital go? If the answer is "not into this stock," then holding the position isn't recovering the sunk cost. It's compounding it.

**Endowment effect.** Owned things feel more valuable than identical unowned things. This is not rational. It's deeply psychological. If you didn't own BFF today, would you buy it at current prices given the governance risks and deteriorating trend? No. Obviously not. But because you own it, you overweight the upside, underweight the downside. The thing you own feels safer than the thing you don't own.

This operates at a subconscious level. You're not consciously telling yourself "I'm going to weight this higher because I own it." It just feels different. And that feeling is the endowment bias at work.

**Status quo bias.** Change requires action. Holding requires no action. Your nervous system is biased toward inaction. It feels safer. It requires less energy. And psychologically, it doesn't require acknowledgment of error. Much easier to say "I'm monitoring closely" than to admit "the thesis is invalidated and I was wrong."

[PAUSE]

These three biases don't operate independently. They compound. They create a reinforcing loop of inaction. And they're why you've written the lessons but not implemented them.

Duke's kill criteria framework addresses the bias at the structural level. You're not relying on emotional strength or willpower or the ability to override your biases in the moment of pain. You're engineering the system so the biases never get activated in the first place. The decision is made before the pain arrives.

## PART 6: MARK MINERVINI'S MECHANICAL SYSTEM [~4 minutes]

Mark Minervini is a trader and systems designer who won the U.S. Investing Championship in 1997, then came back twenty-four years later and won it again in 2021. He's built a mechanical system for position management that is complementary to Duke's kill criteria framework — instead of adding another layer of decision-making, it automates the execution.

Here's how Minervini thinks about exits. Every stock is in one of four stages at any moment.

**Stage 1 is basing.** The stock moves sideways around its two-hundred-day moving average. No clear trend. This is where you do research. You do not deploy capital. Research and capital wait for permission from the technical setup.

**Stage 2 is advancing.** The stock breaks above the moving averages and trends higher. Higher highs, higher lows. Volume expands on advances. This is the only stage to own stocks. All superperformance moves occur in Stage 2. Minervini's entire system is designed to buy early in Stage 2 and exit before Stage 3 completes.

**Stage 3 is distribution.** The advance stalls. Volatility increases. The fifty-day breaks below the one-fifty. Smart money is distributing to retail. This is where you must sell — or at minimum, reduce aggressively. Duke would call this a kill criterion: Stage 3 breakdown = immediate exit process.

**Stage 4 is decline.** Below all major moving averages. Each rally fails at a lower level. Never hold through Stage 4.

[PAUSE]

Here's where it gets personal. Stage 3 is where most fundamental investors rationalize holding. The thesis hasn't changed, right? The numbers still look okay. The conference call was solid. Sell-side hasn't downgraded. But the price is telling you something the thesis hasn't caught up with yet.

This is exactly where BFF was when you should have sold. You saw deterioration in trend indicators. You documented it: "Two-hundred-day moving average — so powerful as a sell discipline." But you didn't use it as a sell discipline. You used it as a consideration to be weighed against conviction. And conviction won. Until it didn't.

Minervini's genius is that he doesn't allow conviction to overrule the technical signal. The Trend Template becomes a mandatory pre-buy check. If a stock fails the template, it goes on the watchlist, not into the portfolio. Capital waits. Research continues. But risk is not deployed until the technical setup permits.

And on the exit side, Stage 3 breakdown is automatic. Minervini says: I don't care how good the fundamental thesis is. Stage 3 is distribution. Smart money is leaving. So I leave. That position goes from full size to fifty percent within twenty-four hours. Stage 4? Exit the whole thing.

No debate. No "let me just see if it bounces." No "the thesis hasn't changed." The price action changed. The stage changed. That's the signal.

[PAUSE]

Combine this with Duke's kill criteria. You define your quantitative signals (Minervini's Trend Template, Stage Analysis), your qualitative signals (fundamental invalidation points), and your time signals (30-day shot clock). And then you execute mechanically. No willpower required.

## PART 7: SELF-IMAGE — BECOMING A QUITTER [~4 minutes]

But here's the limitation of engineering: it gets you seventy percent of the way there. The remaining thirty percent is identity.

Lanny Bassham is an Olympic gold medallist. Rifle shooting, 1976. He won because he reverse-engineered mental performance under pressure. And he discovered something that changed how I think about your coaching: performance and self-image are always equal. You will not, on any sustained basis, perform above your self-image.

Your self-image currently, as a disciplined quitter, is fragile. Quitting feels like exception. Like failure. Like weakness. Holding feels like the default. Like conviction. Like strength.

So even with all the engineering — the kill criteria, the mechanical stops, the Minervini framework — your self-image will pull you back toward holding. Toward "giving it one more quarter." Toward "the thesis hasn't changed." Your nervous system will find reasons to delay. Your conscious mind will rationalise. And Bassham would say: you're not weak. Your self-image as a quitter hasn't been imprinted yet.

Bassham's system for shifting self-image is called directive affirmation. It's not positive thinking. It's not mantras. It's a deliberate, multi-sensory, six-step process for rewiring your identity at the neurological level.

Step one: define the goal specifically. Not "be a better investor." But "I am a disciplined position manager who honors my kill criteria and exits deteriorating positions within two business days of signal breach."

Step two: write it out in first person, present tense, as if already true. "I am a disciplined position manager. When my kill criteria are breached, I act decisively. When the Trend Template breaks, I exit immediately. I view quitting a deteriorating position as excellence, not weakness."

Step three: place copies at Key Points. Your computer monitor. Your desk. Your notebook. Your journal. Anywhere you look multiple times daily.

Step four: daily ritual. Every time you see it — morning, lunch, afternoon — you read it. You visualize it. You feel yourself making the decision. You see yourself closing out XVIVO because the CFO is bottom quartile. You see yourself reducing BFF by fifty percent because the ACH break triggered. You feel the cleanness of the execution. No regret. No hesitation. Because the thesis is invalidated. The data demands it. And you are a person who honours the data.

Step five: hold it for twenty-one days. Three weeks. This is how long neurological imprinting takes. Then nine days off for integration. This isn't magic. This is how Olympic athletes rewire themselves under pressure.

Step six: measure and reinforce. After twenty-one days, you ask: when I got the signal, did I act? Did I honour the criteria? If yes, that imprinting sticks. If no, you run the protocol again.

[PAUSE]

Bassham won gold because his self-image as a champion was so deeply embedded that his nervous system executed the correct behavior under extreme pressure, even when his conscious mind was in doubt. Doubt is normal. But the self-image overrides it.

You're doing the same work. You're not trying to become more gritty or more committed. You're rewiring your identity so that quitting becomes who you are, not what you do reluctantly.
