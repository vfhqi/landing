## Part One: Van Tharp's Core Insight — Risk Is What You Define It To Be

Van Tharp spent decades studying what separates elite traders from average ones. He interviewed hundreds of them. He ran workshops. He built entire systems of psychological and technical analysis. And the thing he kept coming back to — the insight that everything else rested on — was deceptively simple.

Risk is not what happens to you. Risk is what you define before you enter.

[slower] Risk is what you define before you enter.

Most investors — most professional investors, not just retail ones — think of risk as something imposed on them by markets. The stock goes down. That's risk. The earnings miss. That's risk. The macro turns. That's risk. They experience risk as something that happens after the decision.

Tharp said: that's not risk management. That's consequences management. And it's the wrong frame entirely.

His framework starts with a single concept: the Initial Risk, or R. R is the distance between your entry price and the price at which you've definitively decided you are wrong. It's defined before you enter. It is a commitment made in advance, in clear-headed, System 2 thinking, before the Chimp gets involved, before loss aversion kicks in, before the position appears on your screen in red.

Once you define R, everything else is expressed as multiples of R. A 1R loss means you lost exactly what you pre-agreed to risk. A 2R gain means you made twice what you risked. A 5R gain is a brilliant trade. A 0.5R loss is a trade where you exited slightly early relative to your stop but followed your rules.

What makes this powerful — what makes this genuinely transformative rather than just intellectually interesting — is what it does to the psychology of loss-taking.

When BFF started deteriorating in January 2026, and you knew the IR call felt weird, and you knew the cockroach pattern was triggering, and you still held — what was happening psychologically? You were experiencing the loss as an event happening to you. The stock going down was an injury. You hadn't pre-defined the loss. You hadn't decided in advance what "wrong" looked like. So when wrong arrived, it arrived as a shock, and shocks produce avoidance, and avoidance produced the seven-lesson post-mortem you spent days writing.

[pause]

Now contrast that with Corbion in January 2022. What made that different? You had a position. You had a thesis. The thesis showed clear signs of invalidation. And you cut it — cleanly, at a loss, without drama. That wasn't willpower. That wasn't you having a particularly disciplined day. That was the Corbion decision being structured differently from the BFF decision. You knew, roughly, what "wrong" looked like on Corbion. You executed against it.

Tharp would say: Corbion was a 1R loss executed correctly. BFF was an undefined-R loss that became a much larger loss because the boundary was never set.

## Part Two: Expectancy — The Metric That Actually Matters

Here's what most investors optimise for: hit rate. Win percentage. They want to be right more often than they're wrong.

Tharp says this is a category error.

The metric that actually matters is expectancy. And expectancy is defined as:

[with emphasis] The average R-multiple across all your trades.

Which means: your win rate alone tells you almost nothing about whether your system is profitable. What matters is the interaction between your win rate and the size of your wins relative to your losses.

Consider two systems.

System A: wins 60% of the time, average win is 1R, average loss is 1R. Expectancy = positive. Fine.

System B: wins 40% of the time, average win is 3R, average loss is 1R. Expectancy = 0.4 × 3R - 0.6 × 1R = 1.2R - 0.6R = 0.6R. Also positive — and more positive than System A, despite being wrong more often.

Now consider a third system. Richard Black's actual system.

You win positive months 57% of the time. But your best year is +47.80% and your worst is -40.61%. That asymmetry tells a story. When things go right, they go really right. When they go wrong — particularly in 2022 — they went very, very wrong. The max drawdown of 54.75% from January 2022 to October 2023 is, in Tharp's language, a series of negative-R outcomes that compounded into something catastrophic.

Why? Not because your stock picks were bad. Because your R was undefined on the losers. The XVIVO position — 18.67% of the portfolio, held 60 months, -55% from peak — that was never a defined-R position. You were in it before you had established exactly what "wrong" looked like. And when wrong arrived, it arrived in stages, which is the worst way for wrong to arrive, because each stage gave you something to analyse rather than something to act on.

> "Too much detail, no simple ACH invalidation guide. Learning all that stuff was trees not wood."

You wrote that about XVIVO. And you're right. But here's what Tharp would add: the problem wasn't the detail. The problem was that all that detail was being gathered *instead of* defining R. Analytical depth is how you delay the moment of committing to a loss boundary. It feels like work. It produces documents. It creates the sensation of progress. And it is, in a certain light, a sophisticated form of avoidance.

[pause]

The antidote is not less analysis. The antidote is always knowing — before you size up — exactly what would cause you to exit. One sentence. One number. One condition. If X happens, I'm out.

## Part Four: Minervini's 7-8% Rule as Applied R-Discipline

Mark Minervini didn't read Van Tharp before he built his system. But he arrived at the same place from a different direction.

Minervini's 7-8% rule — the automatic exit trigger on any new position — is the mechanical implementation of pre-defined R. You buy at the breakout. You define your stop at 7-8% below entry. If the stock closes below that level, you're out. Not because you've decided the thesis is broken. Not because you've done more analysis. Because the price told you something your analysis didn't know yet.

The reason this works isn't technical. It's psychological.

The 7-8% rule converts the exit from a decision made under stress — a decision where loss aversion, sunk cost, and FOFR are all screaming — into a decision made in advance, in clarity, when none of those forces are present. The trade is already closed before it needs to be closed emotionally.

Tharp would call this a pre-committed 1R loss. Minervini calls it his single most important rule. They're the same insight wearing different clothes.

## Part Five: Duke's Kill Criteria as the Other Half of R

Annie Duke would say that defining R at entry is only half the system. The other half is knowing, in advance, what would cause you to abandon the position before your stop is hit.

She calls these kill criteria. And they're different from stops.

A stop is price-based. Kill criteria are thesis-based. The stop says: if this stock falls 8% from my entry, I'm out regardless. Kill criteria say: if management's narrative falls apart, if the organic growth numbers reveal themselves as M&A-driven, if the macro deteriorates beyond a certain threshold — I'm out, even if the stock hasn't fallen to my stop yet.

The most expensive positions in your track record — BFF, XVIVO, Kainos, Keywords — were all positions where thesis kill criteria existed in theory but weren't written down. The exit conditions were implicit, floating in your head, where they could be rationalised, reinterpreted, delayed.

> "Bad hiring, no respect for great people, bad IR. Lots here."

You knew the BFF kill criteria had been triggered in January 2026. The IR call felt weird. The cockroach was visible. The kill criterion was there — not written down, but there. And the absence of the written commitment meant the Chimp could whisper: maybe it's a one-off, maybe the new hire will fix it, maybe the IR is just having a bad quarter. Every maybe kept you in.

Duke's framework says: write kill criteria at entry, not under stress. One sentence, per condition. Three conditions maximum. If any one fires, exit within 48 hours — not at end of week, not after the next results statement, within 48 hours.

For DCC, if you were to enter post-May 19th, what would your kill criteria be?

Three candidates:

One: If the full-year FY26 organic growth ex-FLAGA is revealed to be negative at the next reporting period, exit.

Two: If the 200D MA is decisively broken on two consecutive weeks after entry, exit.

Three: If SSEM shifts from Orange back to Red following any management commentary, exit.

Write those down before you enter. Not after. Before. That's the kill criteria serving their function as pre-committed insurance against the Chimp.
