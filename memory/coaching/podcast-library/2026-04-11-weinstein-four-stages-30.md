## Segment 1: Who Stan Weinstein Was, and Why He Still Matters [0:00-3:00]

Let me start with a name you may or may not know: Stan Weinstein. Published a book in 1988 called "Secrets for Profiting in Bull and Bear Markets." If you were trading equities in the late eighties, nineties, or early two-thousands, you probably read it. If you didn't read it, you almost certainly read someone who did. It's one of those books that shaped an entire generation of market participants.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable about Weinstein: he believed in simplicity. Not oversimplification, but genuine clarity. He thought that most investors and traders made themselves crazy by adding layer upon layer of unnecessary complexity. Buy this moving average, sell that oscillator, watch the RSI divergence, monitor the MACD histogram — and somewhere along the way, you lose the forest for the trees. You're so focused on the technical minutiae that you miss the one thing that actually matters: what stage is the stock in right now?

[beat]

That's the core insight. Stage analysis. And Weinstein's genius was in making it so simple that a disciplined investor could apply it mechanically across an entire portfolio. One moving average. Four distinct stages that every single stock lives through. Volume as confirmation. That's it. That's the system.

Now, you're a fundamental investor. You do business analysis. You read transcripts. You build models. You understand what a business is worth. That's your core competence, and it should be. But here's what Weinstein discovered — and here's what Minervini confirmed decades later, and what O'Neil's best students have validated: price moves before fundamentals. [with emphasis] Price leads fundamentals by three to six months. Sometimes longer.

[PAUSE]

What that means is this: the stage analysis is not a replacement for your fundamental process. It's an overlay. It's the discipline that tells you when to deploy your fundamental research and when to step back. It tells you when the market is saying something that your spreadsheet hasn't caught yet. And in a concentrated portfolio like yours, where you're holding five to fifteen positions and you're trying to maximize returns per unit of risk, that discipline is worth its weight in gold.

## Segment 2: The Four Stages — The Architecture [3:00-12:00]

So let's walk through the four stages. This is the entire system. Understand these four stages, and you understand how stocks move.

[beat]

**Stage 1 is accumulation. It's the boring stage.** The stock trades sideways. It's flat. Volume is low. The stock is moving around its thirty-week moving average — which is roughly equivalent to the one-hundred-fifty-day MA you already track. There's no clear uptrend, no downtrend. Just sideways price action. Flat. Dormant. Boring.

But here's the secret: Stage 1 is where smart money is working. Institutional investors are quietly accumulating shares. They know something. They're not rushing. They're patient. They're building positions while nobody's paying attention and the price hasn't moved. Volume is thin because retail investors have already given up on the stock. Nobody cares.

[PAUSE]

This is where *you* should do your research. This is the time to pull the financial statements, to read the transcripts, to understand the business, to do your ESA, to dig into the fundamentals. But [with emphasis] do not deploy capital yet. Do not try to catch the bottom. Weinstein was adamant about this: don't buy the bottom of Stage 1. You'll just buy it again on the way back down. Wait for confirmation. The confirmation is Stage 2.

**Stage 2 is advance, or markup.** This is the only stage where you should own stocks. Period. The stock breaks above the thirty-week moving average. Volume expands. The moving average itself turns upward. Higher highs, higher lows. The trend is established. And this is where the big money is made. All the significant moves, the big advances, they happen in Stage 2. This is where you're supposed to be making your money.

[beat]

Think about the mechanics for a second. In Stage 1, while you were doing your research, smart money was accumulating silently. Volume was low. Price was flat. Then something shifts. Maybe earnings beat. Maybe a broker initiates coverage. Maybe sector sentiment improves. Maybe the board announces a restructuring. Something gives. And when it does, volume surges. That's the signal. The stock breaks the 30-week MA on expanding volume. The moving average tips upward. That's Weinstein's green light. That's the entry signal.

And here's the beauty of it: you've already done your homework in Stage 1. You know the business. You know the risks. You know the upside case. So when that Stage 2 breakout happens, you're ready to buy with conviction.

[PAUSE]

**Stage 3 is distribution, or topping.** This is the dangerous stage. The advance stalls. Volatility increases. The moving average flattens out — it stops going up. The stock still looks okay on the surface. It's still above the moving average, technically. But the character of the move changes. Volume surges on down days. Volume dries up on up days. That's the X-ray of what's happening: smart money is distributing. They're taking profits. They're getting out. And they're using any bounce to offload shares.

Here's where your fundamental investing impulse will betray you. The thesis hasn't changed. The business is still good. Management is still competent. The numbers are still solid. So you rationalize holding. You tell yourself this is just a correction within the advance. The moving average hasn't broken yet, so technically we're still in Stage 2, right?

[beat]

Wrong. The price is telling you something that your spreadsheet hasn't caught yet. The price is three to six months ahead of the fundamentals. [with emphasis] By the time management admits there's a problem, by the time earnings start to miss, by the time analysts downgrade, the stock will have already fallen twenty, thirty, forty percent. The price was the signal. You just ignored it.

**Stage 4 is decline, or markdown.** The stock is below all major moving averages. Each rally fails at progressively lower levels. Volume expands on down days. This is where you lose the most money if you're still holding. This is where you absolutely cannot own stocks. And this is where you definitely cannot try to catch a bargain. A stock in Stage 4 that looks cheap is not cheap. It's expensive. Because it's going to get cheaper.

[PAUSE]

Weinstein's rule: never buy in Stage 4. Never hold through Stage 4. I know that sounds blunt. I know your instinct as a fundamental investor is to say: well, if the business is solid and the stock has fallen fifty percent, isn't that a good entry? The answer is no. Not yet. The bottom of Stage 4 isn't the place where value emerges. The bottom of Stage 4 is the place where the most recent cohort of bagholders finally capitulates. You wait for Stage 1. You do your research in Stage 1. You wait for Stage 2. That's when you buy.

## Segment 3: The 30-Week Moving Average as Your Guiding Light [12:00-16:30]

So how do you know which stage a stock is in? One tool. One moving average. The thirty-week moving average.

[beat]

Weinstein's genius was in the simplicity. You don't need forty indicators. You don't need the MACD and the Stochastic and the ADX and the Williams %R. You need one line. One thirty-week moving average. That's it. Everything else is noise.

Stock above a rising thirty-week moving average? That's Stage 2. That's where you belong. Stock below a falling thirty-week moving average? That's Stage 4. That's where you absolutely do not belong. Stock bouncing around the moving average, sometimes above, sometimes below? That's Stage 1 or Stage 3, the transition zones.

Now, here's the thing: you already have this infrastructure in place. You already track the two-hundred-day moving average for your positions. The thirty-week MA is roughly equivalent to the one-hundred-fifty-day MA. You've got the data. You've got the charts. What Weinstein gives you is the discipline to make it non-negotiable.

[PAUSE]

Because right now, here's what happens in real life: the stock breaks below the 150-day MA. You look at it, and you think: well, the business is still good, the thesis is intact, this is just a pullback. I'll hold. And you hold. And it breaks below the hundred-day. You hold. And it breaks below the fifty-day. You hold. And six months later, it's down fifty percent and you're wondering how you let that happen.

Weinstein's answer is stark: you didn't follow the signal. The thirty-week MA is the signal. When the stock closes below the thirty-week MA on rising volume, [with emphasis] the stage has changed. The thesis debate is over. The price has moved. Now you deal with reality.

[beat]

This doesn't mean you never get fooled. Short-term whipsaws happen. A stock can dip below the MA and bounce back. Fine. That's why you have rules. The rule is: if a stock in Stage 2 pulls back to the MA, that's often a buying opportunity, not a selling signal. But if the MA itself starts rolling over, if the slope flattens, if the stock closes below it on high volume, the character has changed. You need to acknowledge that.

## Segment 4: Volume — The X-Ray of Institutional Intent [16:30-20:00]

Now, moving averages tell you direction. But direction without confirmation is just noise. And this is where volume comes in.

[beat]

Weinstein treated volume as the X-ray. Price is what people claim they're doing. Volume is what smart money is actually doing.

In Stage 1, volume is light. Nobody's interested. The stock's flat. Nobody's buying, nobody's selling. Just sideways chop on low volume.

The entry into Stage 2 requires volume expansion. You don't want to see the stock break above the 30-week MA on low volume. That's a dead cat bounce. That's a trap. You want to see the stock move higher, the volume surge, the smart money flooding in. High volume into the breakout means conviction.

[PAUSE]

In Stage 3, the volume signature changes. The rally stalls. Volume comes in on down days — that's distribution, smart money selling. Volume dries up on up days — retail is refusing to follow the bounce. That's the tell. That's what says: this trend is exhausted.

In Stage 4, volume surges on the down days. The stock's falling apart. Everybody's trying to get out at once. That volume tells you there's capitulation. There's fear. There's panic. And panic is where the bottom forms. But the bottom in Stage 4 is not where you buy. The bottom in Stage 4 is where the last holder finally gives up.

[beat]

Here's what this means for you: you're already tracking price. Add volume confirmation to your decision matrix. When a Stage 2 breakout happens, is volume with it? Or is the stock rising on a whisper? When a stock is rolling over from Stage 2 to Stage 3, is volume on the down days? That's your early warning. That's your signal that the stage is changing before the damage is done.

## Segment 5: Sector Analysis — Your Hidden Weapon [20:00-23:30]

Weinstein emphasized something that gets overlooked: buy the strongest stocks in the strongest sectors. Don't fight sector weakness.

[beat]

This is where your RS and breadth dashboard gives you a massive edge. You've built a tool that Weinstein would have absolutely loved. Thirteen hundred ninety-two stocks across sixteen industries and seventy-five sectors. You can see which sectors are strong, which are weak. You can see which stocks are outperforming within their sector. You can apply stage analysis at the sector level, not just at the individual stock level.

[PAUSE]

Here's the logic: if the semiconductor sector is in Stage 4 — it's broken down, the moving average is falling, volume's on the downside — then even the best-of-breed semiconductor company, the one with the best fundamentals, the highest quality, it's going to struggle. It's fighting gravity. The sector wind is against you. Weinstein's rule is simple: don't do it. Don't buy the best stock in a dying sector. Wait for the sector to shift.

Your risk-off posture right now, in April 2026, with the Iran crisis creating sector rotation pressure — that's Weinstein thinking. You're not fighting every sector. You're targeting sectors that are in relative strength: recurring non-cyclical revenue, banks and insurance, cable, healthcare. You're avoiding sectors in relative weakness: European chemicals, consumer discretionary, beverages. That's exactly what Weinstein would do.

[beat]

So the insight for you is this: your stage analysis is not just about individual stocks. It's about sectors. It's about the market itself. When the market is in Stage 4, when the broad indexes are rolling over and breaking their moving averages, even the best individual stock in the best sector will face headwinds. This is where your risk management instinct — stepping back, reducing exposure, respecting the market environment — that's not pessimism. That's Weinstein discipline.

## Segment 6: The Most Dangerous Insight — Price Leads Fundamentals [23:30-28:00]

Let me bring this back to the core insight, because this is where Weinstein's system becomes truly valuable for you.

[PAUSE]

You do fundamental research. You're deep in the numbers. You understand business quality, competitive advantages, capital allocation, growth drivers. That's your edge. That's what you're best at. And you should keep doing it. But here's what your edge is not: predicting when the market will finally agree with your fundamental view.

[with emphasis] Price leads fundamentals by three to six months.

Think about how this works in practice. You identify a business. Let's say it's a software company with recurring revenue, high margins, great unit economics. The fundamentals are rock-solid. The business is compounding. But the stock is in Stage 1 or early Stage 2. It hasn't moved much. Most of the market hasn't woken up yet. So you do your research. You validate the thesis. You wait for the stage to confirm.

Then something shifts. Maybe an analyst initiates coverage. Maybe there's a capital raise at a higher valuation. Maybe the quarterly results are just slightly better than expected. Something gives. The stock breaks above the 30-week MA on expanding volume. That's your entry signal. Stage 2 confirmed. Now you buy with conviction.

[beat]

The thing is: you're not predicting the move. You're confirming it's already happening. By the time you see the Stage 2 breakout, smart money has already accumulated for months. The move is starting. You're buying into a trend that's already in motion, with the fundamental tailwind at your back.

Now flip it. You own a position. It's been in Stage 2 for two years. It's up fifty percent. The business is still good. The thesis is still intact. But then Stage 3 starts. Volume on the down days. The moving average flattens. The character of the move changes. You look at the latest earnings call. Management says everything is fine. Same guidance. Same narrative. And you think: well, management says it's okay, so I'll hold.

[PAUSE]

But here's what's about to happen: in the next quarter or the quarter after that, the earnings will be just slightly lower than expected. Or management will guide down. Or a broker will initiate coverage and they'll be more skeptical than the incumbents. Or the sector momentum will shift against them. And suddenly the thesis that looked so solid starts to crack. By the time the fundamentals confirm, the stock will be down thirty percent from where you should have exited.

Weinstein's insight is stark: the price knows. The price told you in Stage 3 that something was wrong. But you rationalised. You told yourself the thesis was intact. You waited for fundamental confirmation. And the price kept falling until the fundamentals finally caught up.

[beat]

So here's the discipline Weinstein gives you: [with emphasis] when the 30-week moving average rolls over, the stage has changed. When volume shifts, the character has changed. When a stock enters Stage 3 or Stage 4, the clock is ticking. It doesn't matter that your thesis is unchanged. It doesn't matter that you think the business is still good. The stock has just told you that the smart money is moving. And smart money moves first.

Stage analysis doesn't make you right all the time. But it gives you a framework to distinguish between noise and signal. It gives you a way to say: is this a Stage 2 pullback that I should own, or is this the beginning of a Stage 3 transition that I should exit?
