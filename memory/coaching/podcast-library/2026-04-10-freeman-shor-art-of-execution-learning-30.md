# Learning From Others: Lee Freeman-Shor — The Art of Execution
## 30-Minute Podcast Script (~4,500 words)
### Episode 17 | Theme: Position Management Over Stock Selection | Primary Author: Lee Freeman-Shor
### Personalisation Level: Medium-Heavy | Derived from: Learning From Others [C] @ 10-Apr-26

[Opening — warm, direct, slightly provocative]

Here's a number that should change how you think about investing. Forty-nine percent.

[PAUSE]

That's the hit rate. The percentage of investments that made money when Lee Freeman-Shor gave over a billion dollars to forty-five of the world's top investors and told them to invest only in their ten best ideas. Their absolute highest-conviction bets. Not a diversified portfolio. Not a hedged book. Just ten names — the stocks these world-class managers believed in more than anything else. And still — forty-nine percent. Essentially a coin flip.

[beat]

Now here's the thing that makes this extraordinary. Many of those managers — the ones who were wrong more than half the time on individual stock picks — still generated excellent overall returns. Some generated great returns. And others, who were right just as often, lost money. Significant money.

Same hit rate. Same universe. Same time period. Radically different outcomes. The difference was not what they bought. It was what they did after they bought it.

[slower]

That, in a single sentence, is the thesis of The Art of Execution. And tonight, I want to take you through what Freeman-Shor found — the five tribes, the data, the psychology — and more importantly, I want to map it onto your own track record. Because this isn't abstract. This is your Avanza. Your BFF. Your Instalco. Your Fasadgruppen. Every position you've ever held fits into one of these five behavioural patterns. And understanding which tribe you're in, position by position, might be the single most useful diagnostic tool in your entire coaching architecture.

[Lesson 1 — The Five Tribes Framework]

Freeman-Shor spent seven years watching these forty-five elite managers make one thousand eight hundred and sixty-six investments and over thirty thousand trades. Not hypothetical paper portfolios — real money, real decisions, real consequences. And from that data, he identified five distinct tribes — five behavioural archetypes that explain how investors handle their positions after purchase.

Three tribes deal with losing positions. Two deal with winning positions. And the pattern is devastatingly clear.

[PAUSE]

On the losing side, you have the Rabbits, the Assassins, and the Hunters.

The Rabbits — and this was by far the largest group — did nothing. When a position declined, they held. When it declined further, they held. When it became a catastrophic loss, they held. They added no capital. They had no exit plan. They were, as Freeman-Shor describes them, more interested in being right than in making money. The psychological mechanism is paralysis — loss aversion so severe that the act of selling crystallises a loss that feels unbearable, so the brain simply refuses to act.

[with emphasis]

And the numbers are brutal. He documented cases where Rabbit behaviour led to losses of ninety-six percent and ninety-nine percent on individual positions. Not hypothetical positions — real money managed by world-class investors. One manager bought shares at two pounds ten. Two and a half years later, sold at two pence. A ninety-nine percent loss. And this was someone entrusted with tens of millions of dollars. The stock hadn't suddenly collapsed overnight — it had deteriorated week after week, month after month, quarter after quarter. And at every single point, the manager chose inaction.

Freeman-Shor estimates that Rabbit behaviour alone destroyed more capital across his fund than any other single factor. Not bad stock picks. Not bad timing. Not bad markets. Paralysis. The refusal to act when the evidence was screaming at them to act.

And here's the insidious thing about Rabbit behaviour — it's self-reinforcing. Once a position has declined by thirty or forty percent, the loss feels too large to crystallise. So you hold. Then it declines to fifty percent. Now the original loss looks even more catastrophic, and the brain whispers "it can't go much lower from here." And you hold. At sixty, seventy, eighty percent down, you've essentially written the position off psychologically — but you still haven't sold, because the act of selling at a ninety percent loss is so emotionally devastating that the brain prefers to pretend the position doesn't exist. Freeman-Shor calls this the "ostriching" effect. And you've identified it in your own behaviour, Richard — it's in your coaching notes.

[beat]

The Assassins were the opposite. They killed every losing position at a pre-determined threshold — typically twenty to thirty-three percent. No negotiation. No re-analysis. No "just one more quarter." They placed their stop-losses at the time of purchase, before the emotional brain had any input. And it worked. By capping losses small, they only needed modest winners to generate positive returns. The Assassin's edge wasn't in stock selection — it was in damage control. They accepted that they would be wrong regularly, and they built a system that made being wrong affordable.

The Hunters were the rarest tribe. When a high-conviction position declined, they didn't just hold — they bought more. Significantly more. Not token amounts to make themselves feel better, but material capital deployments that meaningfully lowered their average cost. The key distinction from Rabbits: Hunters had explicit kill points. They knew in advance what would cause them to abandon the thesis entirely. And they had position-sizing discipline — they only hunted when they had the capital reserves to deploy and the conviction to justify the additional risk.

[slower]

Now, here's the diagnostic that matters for you specifically. Both Rabbits and Hunters hold losing positions. From the outside, the behaviour looks identical. But the difference is action versus paralysis. The Hunter has a plan. The Rabbit has a hope. The Hunter has pre-determined the thesis invalidation point. The Rabbit has constructed an elaborate narrative for why the thesis will eventually be proven right.

[PAUSE]

And Freeman-Shor offers a devastating test: if you cannot articulate what specific new information would cause you to exit the position entirely — not a vague "if things get worse" but a concrete, observable event — you are not a Hunter. You are a Rabbit pretending to be a Hunter. And that self-deception is the most expensive cognitive error in investing.

[Lesson 2 — The Winning Side]

On the winning side, you have the Raiders and the Connoisseurs. And this is where the data gets really uncomfortable for most investors.

The Raiders sold winners too early. And the scale of this behaviour is extraordinary. Of the nine hundred and twenty winning investments across all forty-five managers, sixty-six percent were sold for a profit of less than twenty percent. Let that land. Two-thirds of all winners — identified by some of the world's best stock pickers — were banked for a gain that barely covers two years of inflation. And of those early sales, sixty-one percent continued to rise after being sold — many going on to become multi-baggers.

[with emphasis]

Think about that for a moment. The majority of these elite investors sold the majority of their winners before the real money was made. And the majority of those stocks kept going up. The Raider's tragedy isn't that they're wrong about the stock. They're right. They're right about the direction. Right about the thesis. And they still leave the vast majority of the money on the table, because the anxiety of holding a gain triggers the same loss-aversion circuit that freezes the Rabbit — except in reverse. The Rabbit is afraid to crystallise a loss. The Raider is afraid to lose an unrealised gain.

The Novo Nordisk case study is the most painful illustration. An investor sold after a twenty-seven percent gain — a perfectly respectable return, the kind of number that feels like a win when you see it on the screen. The stock then rose another one hundred and seventy-five percent after the sale. The investor captured barely a quarter of the available return. And felt good about it at the time.

[beat]

Freeman-Shor found this pattern repeated across hundreds of positions. One investor sold Apple for a twenty percent gain. Another sold Amazon after it doubled — which sounds great until you realise it went on to rise by more than a thousand percent. The Raider keeps harvesting the flowers and watering the weeds. And the tragic irony is that Raiders often feel like disciplined investors. They're "taking profits." They're "managing risk." They're "being prudent." All of which sounds responsible — until you calculate what those premature sales cost them in foregone returns. Freeman-Shor's data shows that the aggregate opportunity cost of Raider behaviour across his fund was staggering. The profits banked were real. The profits left on the table were multiples larger.

[PAUSE]

The Connoisseurs were the antidote. They let their winners run. And here's the paradox that makes this framework so powerful — Connoisseurs typically had a lower raw success rate than Raiders. They made money on only four out of every ten investments. That's worse than the average. But their winners were so large — a hundred percent, two hundred percent, sometimes a thousand percent — that the profits from the few winners overwhelmed all the losses combined. The mathematics of asymmetry working in their favour.

And the key habit that distinguished Connoisseurs from everyone else? No arbitrary price targets. They didn't set a number where they would sell. They asked a different question entirely. Not "should I lock in this profit?" but "is this still a great business?"

[slower]

That question is everything. "Should I lock in this profit?" is a question about the share price — about your profit and loss statement — and the answer will almost always be yes, because the brain wants to secure gains. The endowment effect kicks in. The unrealised gain starts to feel like your money, and the prospect of giving it back becomes unbearable. "Is this still a great business?" is a question about fundamentals — about the actual enterprise you own a piece of. And for truly excellent businesses, the answer stays yes through temporary price weakness, through multiple compression, through short-term earnings misses, through sector rotations. The Connoisseur's discipline is to keep asking the right question.

[Lesson 3 — Your Track Record Through Freeman-Shor's Lens]

Now let's get personal. Because every position in your ninety-six-stock track record maps to one of these five tribes. And the mapping is illuminating.

[PAUSE]

Your Avanza — held seventy-two months, a hundred and seventy basis points of contribution, peaking at nearly twenty-one percent of the portfolio. That is textbook Connoisseur behaviour at its finest. You identified a quality compounder, you sized it meaningfully, and you held it through everything the market threw at you. Through drawdowns, through noise, through the constant temptation to take the profit. That position contributed more to your returns than dozens of other positions combined. And it only happened because you resisted the Raider impulse at every stage.

Your Telecoms and Greggs wins show the same pattern. GVC at a hundred and forty basis points. Entain. When you're at your best, you're a natural Connoisseur. You identify quality, you hold quality, and you let time and compounding do the work. That's your edge. That's your performance niche, in Steenbarger's language.

[beat]

But then there's BFF. And this is where Freeman-Shor's framework cuts deep.

BFF is your crown jewel Rabbit case. You knew the thesis was impaired. You documented it in your journal. You identified endowment bias and commitment bias operating in real time — you could see the cognitive distortions as they were happening. And you still held too long. Freeman-Shor would diagnose this in seconds — you were a Rabbit who happened to be exceptionally good at constructing narratives for why the stock should recover. The analytical depth didn't protect you. It made it worse. Every layer of research added another layer of emotional investment that made the sell decision harder.

[PAUSE]

And XVIVO. Same pattern, different stock. Your own journal note says it — "too much detail, no simple ACH invalidation guide." That's the Rabbit's signature move. Using analytical depth as a substitute for a simple exit decision. The deeper the analysis, the harder it becomes to sell — because every hour of research creates sunk cost bias, and every piece of confirming evidence gets filed away while disconfirming evidence gets explained away.

S4 Capital. Kainos. Keywords. Fasadgruppen at minus seventy basis points held thirty-four months. That last one is particularly instructive because it's the involuntary Rabbit — trapped by illiquidity. You couldn't be an Assassin even if you'd wanted to, because the position was too illiquid to exit cleanly. Freeman-Shor lists liquidity as his fifth winning habit for exactly this reason. Illiquid positions eliminate your ability to manage them. They take away your agency.

[with emphasis]

Now here's the really uncomfortable one. The Instalco trim in July 2021. That was pure Raider. You had a quality compounder working beautifully. The thesis was intact. And you trimmed — not because anything fundamental had changed, but because anxiety peaked. You felt the position was too large, the market was too frothy, the gain was too good to be true. Panic selling disguised as prudent risk management. That's what Freeman-Shor calls Raiding. And his data says sixty-one percent of the time, the stock keeps going up after you sell.

[Lesson 4 — The Asymmetry Principle]

Beneath all five tribes lies a single unifying idea, and it's the most important concept in the book.

[slower]

Asymmetric returns.

Successful investing is about ensuring that the upside from your winners dramatically exceeds the downside from your losers. That's it. That's the entire game. You achieve this through two mechanisms working simultaneously: cap losses small on the losing side, and let gains run large on the winning side. Both sides of the equation matter. Do one without the other and you still lose.

[PAUSE]

Freeman-Shor's data proves this is not theory. Only eleven percent of winning stocks in his study gained more than fifty percent. Only one percent returned more than a hundred percent. That extreme concentration of returns means that a tiny number of big winners generate the vast majority of portfolio-level alpha. The distribution is not normal — it's massively right-skewed. And the entire game of investing is about staying in position to capture those rare, enormous winners while minimising the damage from the frequent, smaller losses.

Every time you sell a winner early — every time you act as a Raider — you are cutting off the right tail of your return distribution. The tail that generates all the alpha. And every time you hold a loser passively — every time you act as a Rabbit — you are fattening the left tail. The tail that destroys capital.

[with emphasis]

Your own returns confirm this perfectly. Avanza at a hundred and seventy basis points. Greggs. Telecoms. GVC at a hundred and forty. Entain. These are your right-tail positions. And they contributed more than enough to cover all the Fasadgruppens, the XVIVOs, the S4 Capitals, and the Kainoses. But only because you held them. If you'd Raided them — if you'd sold Avanza after a thirty percent gain, or trimmed GVC at the first sign of regulatory noise — the entire portfolio arithmetic would have collapsed. The right tail would have been amputated, and the left tail would have dominated your returns.

Your January 2022 concentration — sixty-seven percent in growth names — into the drawdown of minus fifty-four point seven-five percent by October 2023. That's the left tail at work. Rabbit behaviour at the portfolio level, not just the position level. Not one position held too long — an entire cluster of positions, all deteriorating simultaneously, all held past the point where the evidence demanded action. The drawdown wasn't caused by one bad stock. It was caused by the same tribal behaviour — Rabbit paralysis — applied across multiple positions at once. And it took your right-tail Connoisseur wins to recover from it. The Avanzas and the GVCs bailed out the BFFs and the Fasadgruppens. But the margin of safety was thinner than it needed to be.

[Lesson 5 — The Knowing-Doing Gap]

Here's the most uncomfortable finding in the entire book, and it applies directly to you.

[PAUSE]

Every Rabbit in Freeman-Shor's study knew — intellectually — that holding a declining position was suboptimal. Every Raider knew that selling winners early truncated returns. The knowledge was there. The execution was not. These were not ignorant amateurs. These were world-class professionals managing billions, with decades of experience, with research teams and risk management systems. And they still fell into the same tribal patterns that destroy returns.

This is the knowing-doing gap. And we've talked about this before — it was the entire subject of episode two — but Freeman-Shor provides the hardest empirical evidence for it. Not anecdotes, not psychology experiments, not self-help platitudes. Actual performance data from actual professional investors managing actual billions. The most dangerous gap in investing, because it creates the illusion of competence. You think you've solved the problem because you understand the problem. You read the book. You nod along. You underline the key passages. And then you go back to your desk and do exactly the same thing you were doing before, because intellectual understanding and behavioural change are separated by a chasm that knowledge alone cannot bridge.

And your journal proves this gap exists in your own process. March 2025, your own words — "The only two profitable position management strategies is adding all the way down, pyramiding, or the opposite — cutting hard and fast. The middle is death. A bit of either is death, too."

[slower]

That is Freeman-Shor's entire thesis, stated perfectly, in your own journal. Months before you read the book. You know this. You've articulated it with precision. And yet — BFF. XVIVO. Instalco July 2021. Fasadgruppen. S4 Capital. The middle — the death zone of partial conviction and delayed action — keeps pulling you back.

[beat]

The implication is humbling and it's the most important lesson in the book: knowing the right thing to do is insufficient. You must build systems, rules, and habits that force the right behaviour even when your emotions are screaming for the wrong one.

This is why the Assassin's mechanical stop-loss outperforms the Rabbit's sophisticated analysis. The stop-loss is a system. The analysis is a process that can be hijacked by emotion. Your thirty-day shot clock, your ACH threshold, your pre-mortem protocol, your disprove-or-exit framework — these are not bureaucratic overhead. They are the machinery that keeps you in the Connoisseur-Assassin quadrant when the emotional brain is trying to drag you into Rabbit or Raider territory.

[Lesson 6 — The Productive Tension]

Now, I want to sit with a tension that Freeman-Shor's framework creates for your specific approach. Because it's not a clean fit, and pretending it is would be dishonest coaching.

[PAUSE]

Your GARP framework naturally generates fair value estimates. That's part of how you identify opportunities — you model a range of earnings outcomes, assign multiples, and arrive at a target price. But Freeman-Shor's Connoisseurs explicitly avoided price targets. They argued that any fair value estimate is a moving target for a compounding business — and that selling because a stock has reached "fair value" is Raider behaviour wearing fundamental clothing. The valuation discipline that protects you from overpaying on the way in becomes an unconscious exit trigger that causes you to sell your best compounders too early on the way out.

[beat]

So what do you do with that? You can't abandon valuation discipline — it's a core part of your edge and it protects you from the kind of overpaying that leads to permanent capital loss. But you also can't let valuation become the reason you sell an Avanza-quality compounder at fair value when the business itself is getting more valuable every year.

[slower]

One resolution: treat fair value as a trimming trigger, not an exit trigger. When a quality compounder reaches your estimated fair value, take ten or fifteen percent off. Not the whole position. Not even half. Just enough to satisfy the anxiety. And let the core position run. The trim acknowledges the valuation signal. The hold acknowledges the compounding reality.

Another resolution: distinguish between fair value for a normal business and fair value for a compounder. An End-Game Logic Winner — your Archetype seventeen — has a gravitational pull upward over time. Static fair value estimates are structurally wrong for these businesses because the business itself is getting more valuable every year. The fair value you calculated six months ago is already stale. The fair value you calculate today will be stale in six months. For genuine compounders, the right question isn't "has it reached fair value?" — it's "is the compounding engine still intact?"

[PAUSE]

The second tension is about risk-off mode. You're in risk-off right now, driven by Iran. And in risk-off, the temptation is to become an aggressive Raider — sell anything showing a profit to reduce exposure, to get smaller, to feel safer. Freeman-Shor would say: adjust at the portfolio level, not the position level. If you need to reduce risk, cut your weakest positions — the ones where the thesis is most uncertain, where the business is most exposed to the macro shock. Don't sell your best compounders because the macro is scary. The question isn't "am I scared?" — it's "is this business impaired by the macro?"

If Avanza's competitive position isn't affected by Iran, selling it to reduce risk is Raider behaviour — sacrificing your best position because of generalised anxiety. Sell the position where the thesis actually depends on the macro improving.

[The Actionable Takeaway]

[slower, with emphasis]

Here's your one thing.

[PAUSE]

Starting this week, on every position in your portfolio, answer one question. Not which stock to buy or sell. Not what the market is doing. Not what the macro outlook is. Just one diagnostic question:

Which tribe am I in on this position right now? Rabbit, Assassin, Hunter, Raider, or Connoisseur?

And then — is that the right tribe for this position?

[beat]

If you're holding Avanza and acting as a Connoisseur — good. That's the right tribe for a quality compounder. Stay there.

If you're holding a position where the thesis has deteriorated and you're acting as a Rabbit — that's the wrong tribe. Switch to Assassin. Set the shot clock. Define the kill criteria. Execute.

If you're sitting on a winner and feeling the itch to take profits — ask yourself: am I a Raider right now, or is there a genuine fundamental reason to sell? Is this "prudent risk management" or is it anxiety dressed up in analytical clothing?

If you're averaging down on a loser, ask: am I a Hunter with a clear invalidation point and the capital to back it? Or am I a Rabbit who's buying more to avoid admitting the first purchase was wrong?

Write it down. Position by position. Tribe assignment plus one sentence of justification. Do it this week. And if the exercise reveals that more than two positions are in the wrong tribe — that you're Rabbiting when you should be Assassinating, or Raiding when you should be Connoisseur-ing — then the system is telling you something. And the system's voice matters more than your emotional state in the moment.

[PAUSE]

Freeman-Shor proved, across thirty thousand trades by the world's best investors, that the difference between excellent and mediocre returns is not what you buy. It's what you do after you buy it. And what you do is a choice — a choice you can make deliberately, with a system, rather than leaving it to the emotional default that your brain will impose if you let it.

[slower]

The formula is simple. Cap your losses small. Let your gains run large. Accept that you'll be wrong half the time — even the best in the world are. And build the systems that keep you executing the right tribal behaviour — position by position, day by day — regardless of what the market or your emotions are telling you in the moment.

[PAUSE]

That's the art of execution. Not the art of selection. The art of what comes after.

[beat]

Good night, Richard.
