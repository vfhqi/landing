Tonight we're going to talk about something that sounds boring but turns out to be one of the highest-leverage behaviours in your entire system. Not entry. Not exit. Not position sizing. Monitoring.

[PAUSE]

Because here's the problem. You've done extraordinary amounts of research to get into a position. You understand the business. You've written the thesis. You've named the fulcrum driver. You've set the kill criteria. And then, once you're in, something subtle happens. The process that was so rigorous at entry becomes intermittent at best. The same analytical discipline that got you to "buy" becomes sporadic, distracted, and — in your worst cases — deliberately avoided. Ostriching. You know the word. You've named the pattern. Naming it is not the same as beating it.

Brett Steenbarger calls this the performance review problem. Elite traders and investors, he says, make the mistake of reviewing performance only when they're forced to. When the stock moves. When the earnings come out. When someone sends them a note. Reactive monitoring. Which is exactly the wrong mode.

The alternative is what Steenbarger calls proactive performance discipline. You build a structured, scheduled, deliberate review cadence. Not a daily panic check on price. Not a weekly scroll through Bloomberg headlines. A proper review that asks: *what has changed in the underlying reality of this business, and what does that mean for the thesis?*

Let's start with Kainos.

You held Kainos for 44 months. Peak position of nearly 16%. For context, that is one of the larger sizing decisions in your 11-year track record. And what you documented afterwards — in your own words — is one of the clearest case studies in monitoring system failure I've ever seen in an investor's self-review.

Here's what happened. Your team built a hiring indicator. This was the signal: positive hiring comments from the CFO, positive external hiring data, revenue inflection follows. You invested enormous analytical energy in designing that indicator. It was excellent work. And then the indicator started giving you uncomfortable information.

The CFO made muted comments on hiring. Your team's response was to assume the CFO was being conservative. "Low-balling." And for a while, the external hiring data backed that up. Then the indicator reversed. It went negative. At that point, there was a moment. A single moment where the thesis needed updating. And instead of updating the thesis, you blamed the indicator.

[slower]

"Indicator doesn't work."

[PAUSE]

Steenbarger has a name for this. He calls it the "theory-protecting reflex." When new data contradicts your model, the automatic response is to protect the model and question the data. It's not stupidity — it's neuroscience. Daniel Kahneman showed that System 1 has already decided what it believes, and when disconfirming data arrives, System 1 finds an explanation that neutralises the threat. System 2 — the deliberate analytical brain — needs to be explicitly invited to override that reflex. And it won't override automatically. You have to build a structure that forces the invitation.

Your structure, with Kainos, had a gap. The indicator was a brilliant piece of design, but the review protocol around it — "what do we do when the indicator reverses?" — was underspecified. What Steenbarger would call a missing procedure. What you called, after the fact, signal escalation failure.

[PAUSE]

Now look at S4 Capital. Different failure mode, same root.

Peak size of 17% — one of the largest positions in your entire track record. And in January 2022, a flash trading update was issued. Price didn't move. Not a single basis point.

In your own notes: *"Flash trading update didn't move price — major warning sign."*

You identified the signal. You named it a warning. And then? The position was held. Down 35% from peak by end of that January. Down 16% in that month alone.

What happened between "major warning sign" and "held"? This is the monitoring gap. Because identifying a warning sign is not the same as having a pre-built response procedure. In Steenbarger's language, the flashcard was read — you saw the signal — but the performance review didn't produce an action. Because there was no pre-written rule for "market indifference to positive news equals distribution signal equals exit trigger." The rule needed to exist before the event, not as a post-mortem.

[PAUSE]

This is the knowing-doing gap applied specifically to monitoring. You understand monitoring theoretically. You have a Monitoring Plan. You have dimensions — FSO, KFM, OO, SMP. You have Watson. You have a system. And still, in your own documented history, there are multiple instances of either watching the wrong things, or watching the right things but not having a pre-defined response, or having a pre-defined response but not executing it. Three separate gaps, all operating under the label "monitoring."

Steenbarger breaks performance discipline into three components. The first is *observation* — seeing what's actually happening. The second is *analysis* — understanding what it means. The third is *response* — acting on what you understand. Most investors are reasonably good at the first. Moderately good at the second. Consistently poor at the third. Not because they're indecisive, but because the response procedure was never written.

[PAUSE]

Now here's where I want to bring in Mark Douglas.

Douglas's sixth principle of consistency is: "I consistently act in accordance with my trading plan." Notice what he says next. Not "I follow my rules when it suits me." Not "I apply my system when the market cooperates." The word is *consistently*. And he builds the entire principle on a foundation: the rules have to be written before the market presents the scenario. Not during it. Not after it. Before.

Because when you're in a position, you're in an emotional state. The cognitive load of managing an active trade or investment is not zero. You have attention competing for resources. You have existing beliefs. You have sunk costs — not just financial but analytical. You built that hiring indicator. You spent hours on that S4 diligence. You can't expect System 1 to cleanly hand over to System 2 in the moment of a monitoring review. You have to reduce the decision to a procedure.

"If the hiring indicator reverses to negative and the CFO was already giving muted comments, the thesis is invalidated. Action: exit within 10 trading days or present explicit counter-case to Watson for challenge."

That's a monitoring procedure. It collapses the decision from a moment of cognitive load into execution of a pre-written instruction.

That's the system working. Observation → Pattern recognition → Pre-existing label → Decision rule → Action. Steenbarger calls this the expert system: practitioners who've built enough pattern libraries that recognition triggers the right procedure automatically. You did it with Corbion. The question is whether you can build enough procedure triggers that Corbion is the default, not the exception.

You were in risk-off mode at various points. And yet the position was held because the monitoring reviews consistently returned the same answer: thesis intact. Stage 2 trend intact. Thesis intact.

The Telecoms experience shows you what good monitoring feels like from the inside. It's not absence of fear. It's not effortless confidence. It's something quieter. It's the experience of running the review and having nothing to report. "All clear." And then running it again next month, same result. The discipline isn't in the action when things deteriorate. The discipline is in the consistent running of the review — in the months when there's nothing alarming to find.

Most investors only run serious monitoring when things feel uncomfortable. You run monitoring even when things feel good. That's the practice. That's what Steenbarger calls building a *maintenance protocol* — not just a crisis response, but a daily hygiene habit.

Kahneman explains this clearly. The availability heuristic — the tendency to weight recent, vivid experiences more heavily — works in your favour here. Your mental model of BFF was richly positive. Years of thesis confirmation. Multiple positive framework validations. The "quality that doesn't look like quality" insight. That positive history was more available, more vivid, than one weird IR call. System 1 weighted the history. System 2 needed to be given the task explicitly: "What does the weird IR call mean for the ACH probabilities?" Not "is the thesis still valid overall?" The narrower, more specific question cuts through the availability bias.

Your rule — correctly derived after the fact — is: ACH threshold breached equals exit. Not "ACH threshold breached equals investigate further." Exit. Because the investigation reflex, for a stock you love, is the rationalization trap in disguise.

Lanny Bassham has a framework that applies directly here. He calls it the "conscious competence ladder." At the bottom: unconscious incompetence — you don't know what you don't know. Above that: conscious incompetence — you know you have a gap. Above that: conscious competence — you can do the right thing but you have to think deliberately about it. At the top: unconscious competence — you do the right thing automatically, without friction.

Monitoring discipline, for you, right now, is at conscious competence. You know what good monitoring looks like. You can describe it precisely. You can execute it when you're deliberate. What you're building toward — what the daily podcast, the coaching log, the Monitoring Plan, the structured review cadence are all in service of — is unconscious competence. Where the review runs itself. Where the pattern recognition fires automatically. Where the response procedure executes without internal friction.

The distance between conscious and unconscious competence is repetition. It is, almost entirely, repetition. Bassham's mental rehearsal protocol exists because the mind doesn't distinguish deeply between a vivid imagined experience and a real one. Every time you visualise running a clean monitoring review — observing the signal, naming the archetype, executing the pre-written response — you're depositing a credit in the unconscious competence account.

Let me give you the practical architecture, as I see it from the inside of your system.

The monitoring process you've built has five dimensions: FSO, KFM, OO, SMP, and Technical. These dimensions are correct. They're comprehensive. The weakness isn't in the dimensions — it's in the *procedures* attached to each dimension. What are the decision triggers for each signal type? What's the response to a red FSO flag? What's the procedure when OO deteriorates for two consecutive quarters but remains directionally positive? You need the full matrix: signal type → response playbook.

The second structural piece is what Steenbarger calls the *exception report*. Not "here's everything that happened this week." Here's what's different from last week, and why. Your brain is pattern-sensitive. Novelty detection is one of its strongest circuits. The exception report format leverages that. It asks: "What has changed?" Not "what is true?" That question is answered once, at the start of a hold. The monitoring cadence answers: "What has changed since I last looked?"

The third piece is sequencing. You have an 8-point momentum model. You have a Monitoring Plan database. You have Watson running scheduled checks. The integration question is: in what order does information arrive, and does that order maximise early-warning capture? The momentum model gives you technical signals. The Monitoring Plan gives you fundamental signals. The question is: do you see the technical signal first, treat it as confirmation of an emerging fundamental deterioration you've already detected? Or does the technical signal often arrive before you've noticed the fundamental deterioration? In your Kainos case, the technical signal and the fundamental signal arrived simultaneously — multiple compression and hiring indicator reversal in the same month. The early warning was the CFO's muted comments, months earlier. Getting to the muted comments in time — that's the monitoring upgrade available.

[with emphasis]

Early warning systems win. Crisis management is expensive.

Let me return to Avanza for a moment, because it demonstrates something important about the emotional experience of good monitoring over a long hold.

You held Avanza for 72 months. 170 basis points — your best-performing stock. And across those 72 months, there were multiple pullbacks that could have triggered unnecessary exits. There were months where the monitoring review returned "thesis intact but stock under pressure." And the discipline was to hold through the technical noise while remaining genuinely alert to fundamental change.

That requires a specific kind of emotional state during the monitoring review. Not anxiety. Not complacency. What Rotella would call the Trusting Mind in monitoring mode — not re-analysing, not second-guessing, not looking for reasons to exit. Just asking: "Has anything actually changed?" And when the honest answer is no, filing the review and moving on.

The risk with long holds — and you know this intimately from Avanza — is that after 18 months, the monitoring review can become perfunctory. You've done it so many times. Everything is always fine. The review becomes a ritual without substance. That's the drift toward ostriching that doesn't feel like ostriching. It feels like confidence. It feels like "I know this business." Steenbarger warns against this specifically. He calls it *familiarity bias in monitoring* — the assumption that deep knowledge of a business means you'll automatically notice when something important changes. You won't. The review still has to be structural, even when it's been structurally clean for years.

It's asking: if I were starting fresh today, with no position, would I buy? For each one, write a one-sentence monitoring trigger: the specific observable event, data point, or signal that — if it occurs — mandates either an exit within 10 trading days or a Watson challenge session where you have to present the counter-case. Not "if things deteriorate." Something specific. Measurable. Observable. A cockroach name, before the cockroach arrives.

That's what Brett Steenbarger means by the performance review your positions deserve. Not more analysis. Better procedures.
