# Episode 4: The Discipline of Quitting Well (15-min)

You know what nobody talks about in investing? Quitting. Not failure. Not losing money. Quitting. The deliberate, conscious, skilled act of walking away from something that isn't working — before it destroys you.

[PAUSE]

Today I want to talk to you about Annie Duke's work. Duke was a professional poker player who won over four million dollars in tournament play, including the World Series of Poker Tournament of Champions. She has a degree in cognitive science from Penn, studied under the decision scientist Barbara Mellers, and she has spent the last decade thinking about one question that matters more to you than almost anything else right now: why do smart people stay in bad situations for too long, and what can you do about it?

The answer is not willpower. It is not discipline in the way you usually think about it. It is architecture. Pre-commitment. Engineering your exit before the fog descends.

[PAUSE]

Let me start with the central idea from Duke's book *Quit*. She argues that we are systematically biased toward persistence and against quitting. Society celebrates grit. We tell stories about people who never gave up. But the same psychological forces that make us persist through genuine difficulty also make us persist through deteriorating situations long past the point where quitting is the rational choice. The skill is not grit or quit. It is knowing which is which.

And here is the part that matters for you specifically. Duke says the opposite of a great quitter is not a gritty person. The opposite of a great quitter is someone who will not make hard decisions when the data demands it. Your self-image — decisive, dynamic, action-oriented — should include quitting as a form of decisiveness. Not as a failure of conviction.

[beat]

Think about that for a second. Quitting a deteriorating position is action. Holding a deteriorating position is passivity disguised as conviction.

---

Now, the framework. Duke's most powerful concept for you is kill criteria. These are specific, observable, measurable conditions defined at the point of commitment — not at the point of pain — that specify when you will exit. Every kill criterion combines two things: a state, which is an objective measurable condition, and a date, which is a deadline.

Why must they be set at the point of commitment? [slower] Because at the point of investment, you are rational. You are optimistic. You are thinking clearly. You have done the analysis. You understand the risks. You can define, calmly and precisely, what conditions would invalidate the thesis. Six months later, when the stock is down twenty-five percent and your ego is attached, you cannot think clearly about exit conditions. You will rationalise. You will delay. You will hope. The kill criteria bypass this impairment because they were set when your cognition was unimpaired.

Duke calls this a Ulysses contract. Ulysses had his sailors tie him to the mast so he could not steer toward the Sirens. The ropes were not there because Ulysses was weak. They were there because he was wise enough to know that his future self, hearing the Sirens' song, would be irrational. [PAUSE] The kill criteria are the ropes. They do not prevent you from hearing the biases. They prevent you from acting on them.

---

Now let me tell you why you need these ropes. Duke identifies three biases that compound to make exiting positions extraordinarily difficult. She calls them the triad: sunk cost, endowment effect, and status quo bias. And critically, they do not operate independently. They multiply.

Sunk cost is the tendency to keep investing because of what you have already invested — the weeks of research, the Claude and AlphaSense reports, the IR calls, the case files. All that analytical effort creates a massive weight that makes exiting feel like wasting the work.

Endowment effect means you value something more simply because you own it. Once you own a position, you psychologically overvalue it relative to alternatives — not because it is objectively better, but because it is yours.

Status quo bias is the preference for keeping things as they are. Exiting requires active effort and feels like a new risk. Holding requires no action and feels safe.

Together, these three do not just add. They multiply. And then on top of the triad sits the force Duke considers the single most powerful psychological barrier to exits: Fear of Future Regret. FOFR. Minervini nails this in his own language: "The real fear that keeps people from selling is that you're going to sell it and it's going to turn around and go back up. Being wrong twice is a powerful psychological force."

[PAUSE]

Now let me walk you through BFF. Through Duke's lens.

[slower] Reconstruct the decision sequence. At minus five percent: noise, the thesis is intact, hold. Reasonable in isolation. At minus ten: sell-side downgrade, but they are wrong, you know this company, hold. The endowment effect is now active — you know the company because you own it, which makes you think you know it better than the sell-side. At minus fifteen: the IR call was weird, but you have been following this for months. If you sell now and it bounces, you will have been wrong twice. FOFR has arrived. The sunk cost of all your analysis is now a weight keeping you in. At minus twenty-five: it cannot go much lower, the valuation is compelling. That is hope masquerading as analysis. At minus thirty-five: Consob file, governance risk, and finally you are out.

Each step was locally reasonable. The cumulative outcome was catastrophic.

[with emphasis] Duke's kill criteria prevent the first escalation step. If the kill criterion was "stock enters Stage 3 or eighteen-month forward EPS falls five percent, thirty-day shot clock, default exit" — the sequence stops at the first trigger. There is no step two. No step three. No FOFR spiral. The criterion fires, the process executes, and you move on.

---

Now XVIVO. Duke has a framework she borrows from Astro Teller at Google X called monkeys and pedestals. Imagine you want to train a monkey to juggle flaming torches while standing on a pedestal in a town square. The pedestal is the easy part — you can build it any time. The monkey is the hard part. Can you actually train a monkey to juggle flaming torches?

People instinctively build the pedestal first because it creates the illusion of progress. They do the comfortable work while avoiding the hard, uncertain core question. And all that pedestal-building creates sunk cost that makes it psychologically harder to quit when the monkey turns out to be untrainable.

Your own words on XVIVO: "Too much detail, no simple ACH invalidation guide." And: "Learning all that stuff was trees not wood. Narrow frame distraction." [PAUSE] You were building pedestals. The monkey — could XVIVO's management actually execute the multi-organ expansion — was left untested while you accumulated detail. When the monkey proved untrainable, the CFO bottom quartile, execution failures piling up, you had already invested so much in the pedestal that quitting felt like wasting all that work.

The triad in full force. Sunk cost: do not waste six months of work. Endowment: I understand this company better than anyone. Status quo: changing the portfolio is risky, better to hold and monitor.

---

Now here is what makes this especially tricky for you. Duke's sharpest insight in *Quit* is about identity. When your identity is tied to what you do, quitting what you do feels like quitting who you are.

[slower] Your self-image includes powerful statements. "I am a champion investment athlete." "I trust my judgement one hundred and fifty percent." "Outlier critical thinking and judgement." "Bold, big action." These are positive, strong identity statements. They serve you well most of the time. But when a thesis deteriorates, these same identity statements become traps.

"I trust my judgement" becomes "I should not sell — my judgement says this company is good." "Outlier critical thinking" becomes "I see something the market does not." "Bold, big action" becomes "selling would be timid." And "champion" becomes "champions do not quit."

[PAUSE]

This is where Lanny Bassham enters the picture. Bassham teaches that performance equals self-image. You cannot consistently outperform your self-image. Duke's resolution and Bassham's framework converge on the same point: the target self-image must explicitly include quitting as a positive identity marker.

Not "I am someone who holds through conviction." Rather: "I am someone who acts on evidence, including evidence that my thesis is wrong." That is not a weaker identity. It is a more complete one. It includes both holding through genuine noise — like telecoms, plus thirty-four percent — and exiting on genuine deterioration, the BFF rule.

The new directive affirmation: "I set kill criteria at entry and I honour them mechanically. That is like me."

Every kill criterion honoured is evidence for this identity. Every override is evidence against it.

---

And this brings us to telecoms. Because quitting well is not just about knowing when to walk away. It is about knowing when to stay. The telecoms case — plus thirty-four percent versus plus four — was not just a good outcome. Through Duke's lens, it was a case where none of the kill criteria were breached. The stocks were in Stage 2. Fundamentals were intact and accelerating. The theme was confirmed by price action. The decision to hold was process-consistent. The positive outcome was the probabilistic result of a good process, not luck.

The discipline of quitting well includes the discipline of not quitting when the process says hold. That takes the same kind of pre-commitment. The kill criteria protect you in both directions — they tell you when to exit and, equally importantly, they tell you when the noise is just noise and you should stay the course.

Your thirty-day shot clock is a pre-commitment contract. Duke would approve. But she would push you further. She would say: make the criteria more specific. Set them at entry, not at the first sign of trouble. Make the default exit, not reassess. And remove the discretion — because discretion is where the biases live.
