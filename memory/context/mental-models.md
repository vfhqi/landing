## IAJA / IAJ+2DSA

The core operational loop for all investment work.

**IAJA:** Information → Analysis → Judgement → Action

**IAJ+2DSA:** Information → Analysis → Judgement + 2 Downstream Actions (minimum)

Every judgement must generate at least 2 next steps. This prevents dead-end thinking and ensures the system keeps moving. The 2DSA rule applies at every level — from a single KQ memo to a full investment case review.

Watson must tag Notion outputs with the IAJA stages they cover (e.g., a KQ research memo covers Information + Analysis; a synthesis note covers Analysis + Judgement).

## Mission Command (von Moltke)

Richard's preferred framework for delegating work to Watson and structuring research requests. Based on Prussian military doctrine — give clear intent and constraints, not step-by-step instructions.

**Six elements:**
1. **Context** — What's the situation? What do we know?
2. **Higher intent** — Why does this matter? What are we trying to achieve at the strategic level?
3. **Objective** — What specific output or answer do we need?
4. **Suggested how** — How might we approach this? (guidance, not prescription)
5. **Constraints** — What are the boundaries? Time, scope, format
6. **Resources** — What tools, data, or inputs are available?

Richard uses this format when giving Watson tasks. Watson should use it for back-briefs (restate the task understanding before and after execution).

## Art of Action — Three Gaps (Stephen Bungay)

Applied at brief reception — before starting any complex work. The framework identifies where misalignment enters the system between what Richard wants and what Watson delivers.

**Three Gaps:**

1. **Understanding Gap** (between what Richard says and what Watson understands) — Do I understand what he wants? Am I working from his actual words or my paraphrase? Does my paraphrase preserve the details?

2. **Alignment Gap** (between what Watson understands and what Watson agrees to pursue) — Do I agree this is the right approach? Are there issues, tensions with other priorities, or risks I should flag before proceeding?

3. **How-To Gap** (between what Watson plans to do and what actually gets done) — Am I clear on the method, tools, and sequence? Is this how Richard would want it done? Will the execution preserve the intent, or will details get lost in delegation?

**Watson application:** Run the three-gaps diagnostic after Mission Command parsing of any complex brief. State gaps explicitly. Clarify before starting — not after delivering. This framework explains Watson's core failure pattern: understanding is rarely the problem; the how-to gap (delegation drops details) and the alignment gap (working from paraphrases) are where quality breaks down.

**Connection to Mission Command:** Mission Command parse extracts the WHAT. Three-gaps diagnostic checks the HOW and the WHY are aligned.

## OODA Loop (Boyd)

Observe → Orient → Decide → Act

Applied to market monitoring and position management. The key insight from Boyd: speed of the OODA loop is the competitive advantage. The investor who can observe, orient, decide, and act faster than the market consensus wins.

Practical applications:
- **Earnings reactions:** Observe the print → Orient (vs. expectations, vs. thesis) → Decide (thesis intact?) → Act (hold/add/reduce)
- **Macro shifts:** Observe the event → Orient (1st/2nd/3rd order effects on portfolio) → Decide (industry tilts) → Act (reposition)
- **Deterioration signals:** Observe the data → Orient (vs. degradation indicators) → Decide (trigger disprove-or-exit?) → Act (start 30-day clock or clear)

## Complex Systems

Richard explicitly draws on complex systems theory for both investing and self-management.

**Key principles:**
- Complex systems appear stable even while losing resilience
- Underlying stability is hard to assess
- Collapse often appears sudden and surprising
- Feedback loops can be positive (reinforcing) or negative (balancing)
- Small inputs can produce large, non-linear outputs

**Application to positions:**
- Three degradation indicators: slower recovery rates, rising autocorrelation, rising variance
- By the time negatives manifest visibly, there are many problems under the surface
- By the time you emotionally recognise deterioration, you are likely very late
- Solution: systematic tracking with predefined thresholds, not gut feel

**Application to self-management:**
- Performance is a complex system — many interacting variables
- Cannot optimise individual variables in isolation
- Focus on the system conditions (sleep, preparation, routines) that enable peak performance

## ACH (Analysis of Competing Hypotheses)

Heuer's framework, adapted for stock selection.

**Structure:** Matrix with hypotheses (profiles) across the top and evidence dimensions down the side.

**Three profiles:**
- "No" — reasons this is not an investment
- "False friend" — looks good but has structural flaws
- "Yes" — genuine investment opportunity

**Process:** Systematically evaluate evidence against each profile. The goal is to disprove "no" and "false friend" — not to confirm "yes." If the stock survives disproval, it might be an investment.

## Josh Tarasoff's Feedback Loops / Self-Driving Portfolio (Jul 2023)

Portfolio acts as a self-correcting system if monitored properly. Positions that weaken over time naturally signal through:
- Deteriorating tracking metrics (revenue miss, margin compression, guidance reduction)
- Valuation multiple compression (market pricing out the thesis)
- Competitive position erosion (losing market share, price pressure)

The "self-driving" concept: trust the feedback loops. A position that is breaking is sending signals. Don't need perfect analytical clarity to act. The data IS the clarity. Exit when deterioration signals appear; re-attack when clear evidence of recovery.

This is the opposite of "hold for long-term thesis" — it's "monitor like a hawk, move immediately when signals shift."

## Non-Ergodicity Risk Concept

Markets are non-ergodic — the statistical properties of the market in any given moment are not the same as the long-run statistical properties. A strategy that works in bull markets may catastrophically fail in bear markets, even though the long-run expected return is positive.

Implication: drawdown is not just noise to tolerate. It's a regime shift that demands tactical response. Portfolio constraints (diversification, position sizing caps) are not "optimal" in bull markets but are survival mechanisms in bear markets.

## Cone of Outcomes (Improving Over Time)

From Paolo Mortarotti (5-year lessons review, 2023): The range of plausible outcomes expands the further forward you forecast. The "cone" widens. At 3-year horizon, the outcome range is very wide. The value of an investment case is highest in the 18-24 month window where you have enough conviction to size meaningfully but low enough uncertainty to have edges.

Implication: don't try to be right on 5-10 year outcomes. Size positions to be right on 18-24 month outcomes. Let long-term optionality ride, but anchor sizing to the 18M+4Y time horizons where you have genuine predictive power.

## Flywheel vs Treadmill vs Fragile Classification

**Flywheel:** Network effects, economies of scale, increasing margins with scale. Positioning gets stronger as company grows. Low degradation risk.

**Treadmill:** Requires constant effort, capital, or inputs to maintain position. Margins stable or declining. Competitive intensity high. Degradation risk = default state unless management acts.

**Fragile:** Deteriorating positioning, rising competition, commoditising input/output. Business is in structurally declining position. Degradation risk is existential.

Portfolio should be 70%+ flywheel, 20-25% treadmill (opportunistic, sized small), 5% fragile or less (avoid).

## Bill Walsh Playbook Analogy (Sept 2023)

From Steve Ward framework: Great coaches (Bill Walsh in football, etc.) don't improvise on game day. They have a tested playbook. Every situation is mapped. When condition X appears, the team runs play Y. This removes the burden of deciding in real time and accelerates execution.

Investment analogy: Your process (6-stage pipeline, Key Questions framework, monitoring protocols, exit triggers) is the playbook. The game is the portfolio. When a stock shows signal X, the play is Y (30-day shot clock). When conviction is Y, position is Z%. No improvisation; just execution.

## "Complex = Confronted by Incongruities" (Paolo Mortarotti, May 2023)

A system is complex when you are constantly surprised by unexpected connections and emergent properties. An investment case is "simple" when you can predict outputs from inputs with reasonable accuracy. An investment case is complex when you keep discovering new factors that shift the outcome.

Reframe complexity as a red flag. If you're "constantly discovering new stuff," it's probably too complex to forecast reliably. 18M EPS for a simple business = edge. 18M EPS for a complex business = mirage.

## Strong Views, Weakly Held

Always have a conclusion, even if revisable. The worst state is ambiguity — it prevents action and wastes energy. Form the best judgement available with current information, act on it, and update when new information arrives.

Practical rule: Every Watson output should end with a judgement. Flag confidence level and key assumptions, but never end with "it depends" or "more research needed" without a directional view.

---

## Good Plan Executed > Great Plan in the Drawer

Action, dynamism, drive. Simple + completed beats complicated + incomplete. This applies to both investment decisions and Watson's work output.

If Watson has 80% of the information needed, produce the output. Don't wait for perfection. Flag the gaps and move.
