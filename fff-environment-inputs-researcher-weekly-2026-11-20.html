Report 1
G10 and European macroeconomic inputs (source #1)
Executive summary
  • The read for 13 to 19 November 2026: inflation is still worsening while growth holds, and the experts split on whether the worst is over
  • Strongest view: Goldman Sachs economists (17 November) call October "a third month of worse inflation news" yet judge the largest monthly effects of energy, artificial intelligence (AI) input costs and tariffs to be behind us
  • Sharpest disagreement: Goldman Sachs (13 November) says two-thirds of the energy pass-through into core prices is done; Federal Reserve economists say about one-third
  • New this week: Federal Reserve minutes show "almost all" officials expect further rate rises (Goldman Sachs, 18 November)
  • Watch: the next two United States inflation reports, French election polls, Iran

Inflation

Goldman Sachs economists (US Inflation Monitor, 17 November) hold that United States inflation is high and close to its peak. Why: they estimate core inflation on the Federal Reserve's preferred measure at 3.24% in October, of which the Iran war energy shock adds 0.5 percentage points, AI-related prices 0.45 and tariffs 0.4, and they expect each to fade through 2027. Goldman Sachs (Euro Area 2027 Outlook, 13 November) expects euro area headline inflation near 4% through the winter, a "short-lived hump" because wage second-round effects are limited.

Where they disagree: Chicago Fed President Goolsbee called the October consumer price report "borderline disturbing" and broad-based; New York Fed President Williams called it a "continuation", not a "broadening" (Pacific Asset Management weekly, 13 November; Goldman Sachs, 17 November). Bank of England Deputy Governor Lombardelli sees a "greater prospect of second-round effects" (Pacific Asset Management, 13 November); Goldman Sachs (15 November) expects no strong ones in the United Kingdom.

Why it matters: a short hump lets central banks stop early in 2027; persistence keeps discount rates rising against European equity valuations.

StatusAbove target in every G10 economy cited
TrendWorsening for three months in the United States
Change in trendNone yet; Goldman Sachs dates the turn to 2027
SkewUpside, per Federal Reserve staff

Economic health and consumer sentiment

Pivotus Partners (19 November) says there is "no wiggle yet" in the United States economy: jobless claims and tax receipts show no weakness. For 2027 Goldman Sachs expects global growth of 2.8% with risks to the downside (16 November), euro area growth of 0.9%, below consensus, as energy prices slow consumption (13 November), and United Kingdom growth above consensus at 1.4% (15 November).

Consumer surveys, from the week's web search because the notes were thin:

  • United States, University of Michigan: November headlines conflict, one reporting a three-month high of 51.0, another a slump; its one-year inflation expectations fell 0.4 points to 4.3% (Goldman Sachs, 17 November)
  • United States, Conference Board: headlines report a November fall, no figure
  • Germany, GfK: minus 30.8 against minus 30.6 before, slightly lower (Newsquawk, undated)
  • United Kingdom, GfK: headlines conflict on direction, no usable figure
  • Euro area, European Commission: no reading
  • No reading this week: France, Italy, Japan, Canada, Switzerland, Sweden, the Netherlands, Belgium

Why it matters: European consumer earnings face a winter in which Goldman Sachs expects spending to slow.

StatusUnited States strong, Europe soft
TrendUnited States steady; euro area slowing
Change in trendGoldman Sachs expects euro area growth below its recent run rate
SkewDownside to growth, per Goldman Sachs

Trust in fiscal and monetary leadership

Goldman Sachs (18 November) reports that the Federal Reserve minutes show two dissenters who wanted a rise in October and staff flagging that long-run inflation expectations "could have become unanchored". Goldman Sachs (13 November) names French budget tensions the main euro area risk: polls make Marine Le Pen the most likely next President, and investors could question her commitment to cut the deficit. Jens Eisenschmidt of Morgan Stanley (16 November) says France and Italy head towards debt of 150% of national output by 2040 without policy change. Against that, Jefferies' policy team (17 November) offers "10 reasons for optimism on EU policy".

Why it matters: Goldman Sachs sees French stress weighing on domestic assets and spilling over to other high-debt countries.

Report 2
G10 and European macroeconomic outputs (source #2)
Executive summary
  • The read for 13 to 19 November 2026: the discount rate is rising across the G10 and equities have responded only in part, with the damage concentrated in technology
  • Strongest view: Goldman Sachs (16 November) says markets price more tightening than its economists expect, the gap widest for the European Central Bank (ECB)
  • Sharpest disagreement: Goldman Sachs sees a lower bar for risky assets after the valuation reset; James Ferguson of Macro Strategy sees record margin debt and "amber" bubble warnings
  • Changed: Goldman Sachs's United States financial conditions index tightened 32 basis points since 9 November, on lower equities and a higher ten-year yield (18 November)
  • Watch: the December Federal Reserve and ECB meetings

Rates and the discount rate

Goldman Sachs (18 November) expects the Federal Reserve, now at 3.75 to 4.00%, to raise rates in December 2026 and March 2027 to a peak of 4.25 to 4.50%, then cut from November 2027. It expects one more ECB rise in December and cuts from late 2027, one Bank of England rise in February, and three Bank of Japan rises (13 and 16 November). Why: it expects the energy, artificial intelligence (AI) and tariff impulses to inflation to fade.

Where they disagree: on pace. Governor Waller raised a half-point move; New York Fed President Williams sees no call for "dramatic action" (Goldman Sachs, 17 November).

Why it matters: on the Goldman Sachs path the discount-rate headwind to European equities peaks by spring 2027; markets price more.

StatusRates rising in the United States, euro area, United Kingdom and Japan
TrendExpectations still moving up
Change in trendNone; Goldman Sachs dates first cuts to late 2027
SkewGoldman Sachs sits below market pricing; Federal Reserve officials lean higher

Bond yields and credit spreads

Pacific Asset Management (weekly, 13 November) reports parts of the yield curve 35 basis points higher in the week to 13 November, after Waller and the inflation print. Goldman Sachs (16 November) expects the largest ten-year bond rallies in the United States and United Kingdom over twelve months, and notes the option-implied probability of a further sell-off in long-dated Treasuries has eased. On credit, Daily Chartbook (13 November) headlines spreads on hyperscalers and large chip companies as having "blown out"; Goldman Sachs (17 November) expects contagion from AI-related widening to stay muted. Morgan Stanley (16 November) holds that European corporates are prepared for higher yields: 87% of the main index's debt matures in 2028 or later.

Why it matters: on these accounts credit stress sits in United States technology, not European balance sheets.

StatusYields higher; credit wider in AI borrowers
TrendBoth rising
Change in trendNone reported
SkewGoldman Sachs expects lower yields in twelve months and emerging market sovereign spreads about 40 basis points wider

Commodities

Goldman Sachs (16 November) sees the largest upside against futures in copper, where AI and electrification demand meets constrained supply, and gold, on central bank buying. Marc Kofler of Jefferies (13 November, sales commentary) calls trading resources "as treacherous as I can remember".

Why it matters: Goldman Sachs ties European consumption this winter to energy prices.

StatusOil in the low to mid $90s

Risk appetite and the index risk and reward

  • Are equities ignoring the discount rate: in part. Daily Chartbook (13 November) notes semiconductor shares down over 25% and technology over 15% from highs with the S&P 500 down 3%; in five earlier such episodes the index was already in a double-digit drawdown
  • Cracks underneath: yes. Goldman Sachs (16 November) says cyclicals sold off against defensives, equities underperformed bonds and credit widened, leaving its Risk Appetite Indicator near zero
  • Factor spreads, and small-cap growth against large-cap yield: no source spoke to this

  • Cone of uncertainty: wider. Goldman Sachs says "both upside or downside tails remain possible"; a Jefferies trading desk (via Duncan Farr, 18 November) calls the set-up "very binary"
  • Skew: downside. Goldman Sachs reports a rising implied probability of a further semiconductor sell-off

Report 3
Stock-market and business/demand-level thematic narratives and developments (source #3)
Executive summary
  • The read for 13 to 19 November 2026: artificial intelligence (AI) capital spending is still the theme that drives leadership, and this week the experts argued about how long it lasts
  • Strongest view: Goldman Sachs (16 November) counts strong AI spending as a support to 2027 growth and names disappointment in it one of three risks to its baseline
  • Sharpest disagreement: Jefferies' semiconductor desk (19 November) says a United States slowdown is cushioned by China; its own analyst Edison Lee expects memory prices to peak in mid 2027
  • Changed: since October markets have become more worried about AI spending (Goldman Sachs, 16 November); Jefferies dates new political scrutiny to the Democratic landslide in the midterm elections
  • Watch: the Jefferies data-centre call on 24 November and United States chip legislation before year end

Artificial intelligence capital spending

Jefferies' Global Semis Daily (19 November) holds that investors now care about the durability of spending in 2028 and beyond, shown by the fall in the leading chip designer's shares despite raised 2027 guidance; its analyst Blayne Curtis cut his price target. Jefferies argues against extrapolating a slowdown: China is about 29% of global memory demand and is building its own AI infrastructure, and one Chinese platform's comment that its spending peaks in 2027 is a poor guide to the world. Jefferies (15 November) also points to a networking equipment maker raising its AI revenue target by a fifth in three months and calling a "multiyear networking supercycle".

Where they disagree: inside Jefferies, Edison Lee expects memory prices to peak in mid 2027 as undisclosed capacity additions and Chinese supply arrive in 2028 (19 November). Goldman Sachs (17 November) expects memory price growth to slow from 250 to 300% this year to 5 to 10% in 2027. Chicago Fed President Goolsbee says the data-centre build-out is starting to look like demand overheating (Pacific Asset Management weekly, 13 November). Jefferies (16 November) reports investors asking how to reconcile implied chip demand with United States data-centre deliveries.

Why it matters: Morgan Stanley (16 November) estimates that about 12% of the MSCI Europe index is direct AI spending enablers, in capital goods and semiconductors, so the durability debate reaches a European long-only book directly.

StatusSpending strong, confidence shaken
TrendDemand rising; share prices falling
Change in trendThe debate moved from 2027 numbers to 2028
SkewDownside, per Goldman Sachs option pricing

Export controls and trade protection

Jefferies (15 November) says the turmoil around the leading chip designer's former chief executive is hardening United States views on China, raising the odds that three chip-control bills pass with the annual defence legislation. If one passes as drafted, Jefferies expects about a 15% hit to the revenue of Europe's largest lithography supplier. Goldman Sachs (17 November) expects new United States tariff increases after the midterms, lifting the effective tariff rate by a little under 2 percentage points. In Europe, Jefferies' steel analysts Cole Hathorn and Tommaso Castello (17 November) see policy curbing low-cost steel imports by 50% from July 2026, so prices are set by the marginal European producer.

Where they disagree: no house argued the other side this week.

Why it matters: controls cut European chip-equipment sales to China, while protection supports European basic industry pricing.

Europe's reform, power and defence agenda

Morgan Stanley (The Signal, 16 November) holds that fiscal, demographic and energy pressures are forcing a new European economic model: pension reform and a Savings and Investment Union to deepen capital markets, and power and grid investment that Renato Grandmont calls "essential to unlocking AI-related productivity gains". Jefferies' policy team (17 November) returned from Brussels with "10 reasons for optimism on EU policy", across energy, electrification, defence and China.

Where they disagree: Goldman Sachs (13 November) counts defence and AI adoption as opportunities but expects coming elections to hinder major reform in the near term. Morgan Stanley itself calls the opportunity "selective, not universal".

Why it matters: these are the domestic demand themes for European industrials, utilities and banks.

Banks

Duncan Farr of Jefferies (18 November) expects United Kingdom banks to "recapture some lost form". Why: capital rules are moving in the sector's favour ahead of the Financial Policy Committee on 26 November, NII is still rising after the downgrades, and relative underperformance leaves the shares open. Jonathan Pierce of Jefferies (7 November, recirculated 18 November) answers the fear of a 2023-style downgrade cycle: the rate impulse is much softer and sight deposits are down less than 1% since March, so he is "relaxed". For euro area banks, Farr flags the European Central Bank's pending decision on reserve remuneration: the earnings effect is low single digits "at best" but the headline risk is real.

Where they disagree: Pierce reports "clear concern" among investors that NII has further to fall.

Why it matters: banks are about 15% of the European index and are sensitive to both the rate path and regulatory decisions due within weeks.

Semiconductors within technology

Jefferies (Global Semis Daily, 15 November) holds four views. Equipment: cautious, because one bill would ban shipments of immersion lithography tools to China, a hit of about 15% to the largest European supplier's revenue and under 5% to a second; equipment makers "remain out of favour" even after a United States peer guided 2027 equipment spending up more than 30%. Automotive chips: likely to outperform into 2027, on restocking, price increases and a carmaker's warning of 2027 shortages. Networking: a "multiyear supercycle" led by artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure. Memory (19 November): makers have quietly accelerated capacity over three months, so concern is growing about prices when that and Chinese capacity arrive in 2028.

Where they disagree: Edison Lee of Jefferies expects memory prices to peak in mid 2027; Goldman Sachs (17 November) still forecasts 5 to 10% memory price growth in 2027; Jefferies' Korea view is that China, about 29% of memory demand, limits the downside.

Why it matters: European technology weight is concentrated in equipment and automotive chips, on opposite sides of this call.
