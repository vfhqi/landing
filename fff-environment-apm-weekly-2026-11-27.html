The week in brief

The regime: risk-off in the open, led by the artificial intelligence (AI) capital-spending unwind and its credit leg, with the squeeze running through financial conditions rather than the policy rate.

  • Last week's hidden signal became an open correction, because the index fell rather than breadth healing. The STOXX Europe 600 is 4.1% below its 52-week high, the share of stocks above the 200-day average fell to 54% from 63%, and defensives lead cyclicals on all five windows. For Richard: hold large, proven cases and do not chase strength; the week's 2.9% rebound was led by November's losers and reads as a short-covering bounce inside the correction, not a turn.
  • The causes were read with named reasoning for the first time in three weeks, and no reasoned house argues for Federal Reserve easing. The houses argue between a hike and a hold behind the curve, so the discount-rate squeeze has moved from the policy rate into financial conditions (tighter by 100 to 150 basis points) and into AI-linked credit. Conviction on the regime's direction rises to high and on its causes to moderate; the most likely error is the horizon, because a cyclical bounce could run for weeks without the twelve-month view changing.
  • Yields fell for the wrong reason. The United States ten-year at 4.83%, down 50 basis points in two months, is a safety bid, so the rate-sensitive Underweights hold 33 basis points above their trigger. They are the rows most exposed if the fall extends.
  • The AI capital-spending theme flipped from leadership to liability, and the table follows the epicentre. The environment

The macro causes

Inflation is high at the level, disputed at the core, and the houses disagree on the next Federal Reserve move, not on the level. United States core personal consumption expenditures (PCE) inflation is 3.3% (headline 3.8%), euro area core 2.6% with services at 3.7%, United Kingdom core 2.8% (Goldman Sachs Economics, 25 Nov). Goldman reads trimmed-mean inflation near 2.4% as the true signal and expects inflation to fall across developed markets by end-2027; Pivotus Partners (R. Farr, 25 Nov) reads the 78th consecutive monthly rise in core PCE and says "we think the Fed hikes"; MacroStrategy Partnership (Michael Wilson, 26 Nov) reads breakevens down to 2.1% as inflation rolling over. Judgement: the discount-rate squeeze is not ending through policy; it has moved into financial conditions. For a European book, Goldman's view that commodity spillovers into European core will be "more delayed" keeps the European Central Bank (ECB) boxed in through winter, and the ECB's minutes lean towards raising rates (27 Nov).

Growth is firm at the level in the United States and Germany and contracting in France; the consumer was read only for the United States, where it is strong. Third-quarter United States GDP was revised up to 4.3% annualised and Conference Board consumer confidence jumped 11.4 points to 103.3 (Goldman Sachs, 24 and 25 Nov). Germany's composite purchasing managers' index (PMI) is 55.0, a 56-month high; France's is 46.2, "a deepening downturn" (S&P Global via Pivotus, 23 Nov). Jan Hatzius (Goldman, 22 Nov) forecasts 2.8% global growth in 2027 with risks "tilted to the downside" and "early signs that AI is weighing on the job market"; Jari Stehn (Goldman, 22 Nov) expects high energy prices to slow euro area consumption over winter. European consumer sentiment and the University of Michigan survey were not read, so the European consumer is unread this week and is not inferred from the PMIs. Recession is not the modal case in any house's numbers.

Trust in fiscal and monetary leadership is deteriorating in the United States and France, read for the first time in a month; Greece is the counter-example. The Federal Reserve "feels meaningfully behind the curve" (Mark Wilson, Goldman Sachs, 20 Nov). Nvidia's chief executive was removed and reinstated "with senior US government input", and Farr concludes "you must account for this volatility with lower multiples" (Pivotus, 23 and 24 Nov). Macron is considering a constitutional "golden rule" if markets stay unstable in January, with Le Pen leading the polls (Pivotus, 23 Nov); Greece runs a 3.2% primary surplus (Jefferies, D. Farr, 26 Nov). No source argued trust is improving in the United States. The French premium in basis points was not read for a fourth week, so the France row now rests on this political and PMI evidence rather than on the stale spread.

Geopolitics is escalating on all three fronts a source spoke to; Ukraine was not read. The Section 301 tariff proposal is "largely as expected": the effective United States tariff rate rises 1.4 points, the European Union, Japan and Switzerland face 15% inclusive, China 24.9%, and the inflation effect is "very small" (Goldman Sachs Economics, 25 Nov), with the hearing on 25 January. On Iran "tangible progress feels limited" and Marc Kofler (Jefferies, 21 Nov) fears "escalating US military intervention". TSMC and Samsung have halted sub-16 nanometre shipments to Chinese designers (Edison Lee, Jefferies, 24 Nov). For a European book the tariff is a known cost and Iran is the one two-sided skew on oil.

The macro prices

Rates: the squeeze has moved from the policy rate into financial conditions, and the policy direction is now held as "held high, with a hike the live risk", no longer "not established". Financial conditions have tightened 100 to 150 basis points, the dollar is retesting its post-2024-election highs and Fed-speak is hawkish, a combination that produced 10% to 20% equity drawdowns in 2018, 2023 and 2025 (Mark Wilson, Goldman Sachs, 20 Nov). Pivotus (25 Nov) has the Fed hiking and the curve inverting. MacroStrategy (26 Nov) has the two-year to ten-year curve flattened to 23 basis points and the ten-year real yield down to 2.73% from 2.97%, the bond market pricing a slowdown. Goldman's rates team (William Marshall, 22 Nov) expects ten-year Treasuries at 4.6%, gilts at 4.8% and Bunds at 3.1% by end-2027, so European discount rates fall less than American ones. The ECB's and Bank of England's next moves were not read, because Barclays' European rates outlook arrived unreadable.

Yields: falling in the United States as a safety bid, rising in Europe this week. The United States ten-year is 4.83%, down 50 basis points in two months, with a 28 basis point one-day fall on 24 Nov (Outset Global); European yields rose into the week's close as the ECB minutes leaned to hikes (27 Nov). The telecoms and towers trigger and the Real assets trigger (the ten-year under 4.5%) are 33 basis points away, closer than at any read since the rows were set, so the table is most exposed here if the fall extends. The rows hold because a yield falling on growth fear is not the relief they were waiting for.

Credit: wider, read for the first time in four weeks, with the stress concentrated in the AI financing chain. Corporate spreads widened on a flight to Treasuries and AI credit default swaps (CDS) "had a very bad week" while consumer credit shows no weakness (Pivotus, 23 Nov); HSBC, ING and Societe Generale are listing hundreds of millions of AI loans for sale on worries about construction delays across $89 billion of project finance (Breakingviews via MacroStrategy, 26 Nov); SoftBank's five-year CDS is 516 basis points, the widest since October 2022 (Outset Global, 26 Nov). No source said spreads were about to tighten; European credit spreads specifically were not read. Judgement: this is the credit leg of a capital-spending bust, not a systemic one, and it is the direct evidence the Fragility row needed.

Commodities: energy lower in the base cases, metals sold with liquidity. Brent is near $90; Goldman (Daan Struyven, 22 Nov) has it averaging $88 in 2027 and easing to $78 by December 2027 as the market returns to surplus; European gas is expected at 45 euros per megawatt hour in 2027 against 70 to 100 recently (Samantha Dart). Copper is $5.50 a pound with persistent supply problems (Kofler, 21 Nov), and gold and copper joined the equity liquidation this week. Brent is $5 above the $85 trigger on the energy rows and Norway, with the consensus base case pointing through it over the next year; the energy letters hold, with that exposure said on each row.

Thematics

The AI capital-spending theme has flipped from leadership to liability, and the live question for European industrials is second-order: do cables and grid hold while builders and installers fall? Julien Garran (MacroStrategy, 20 Nov) rests the bear case on Nvidia's own disclosures: vendor financing up 1,600% in three years to $63 billion, off-balance-sheet obligations up from $184 billion to $530 billion in a quarter, and orders to swing from up 70% to "heavily down" within a year. Mark Wilson (Goldman, 20 Nov) reaches a softer version: the pace "is likely unsustainable" and investors must separate durable earnings from pulled-forward demand. Janardan Menon (Jefferies, 26 Nov) gives the mechanism: over-ordered order books get cut far more than marginal volume warrants, the first cuts come at fourth-quarter Asian server results, and semiconductor equipment re-rates first. Surplus evidence arrived this week: SpaceX cut graphics processing unit rental prices by a third, banks are selling AI loans and Nvidia halted South East Asian shipments. In Europe, Danske (Carl-Johan Kristell, 22 Nov) rates Bravida a Sell as "the last man standing" among Nordic data-centre installers with peak orders behind it; Danske's technology team (26 Nov) says the "AI hardware rewind" is overdone in HMS Networks; Kepler (Tom Donneky, 26 Nov) notes Prysmian still winning hyperscaler deals, with data-centre work at 12% of sales. Hatzius still expects strong AI capital spending to support 2027 growth. The table follows the epicentre: Hardware to Underweight, Semiconductors and Electrical equipment held at Underweight, with their Double Underweight conditions (a hyperscaler guide cut read in the sources) not yet fired.

AI adoption is the second-leg thesis the houses are moving to, with Europe said to gain most, but nobody quantified it. Goldman's "AI productivity champions" have diverged from capital-spending winners over six weeks, and Europe and the United Kingdom, services-heavy and ageing, stand to gain most (Mark Wilson, 20 Nov); Roche is "the clear AI leader in pharma" (Donneky, 26 Nov). AI adoption is weighed as a reason the Healthcare industry's condition is met, not as a tilt in its own right.

Energy relief is the consensus base case for Europe; defence sentiment is softening into its own test. "No region will be as large a beneficiary of declining energy prices as Europe" (Mark Wilson), against Kofler's Iran fear. On defence, Bilal Ismail (Jefferies, 26 Nov) is "nervous" ahead of Rheinmetall's capital markets day on 27 November, expects the 2030 revenue guide cut to 35 to 40 billion euros and says the sector cannot recover without Rheinmetall participating; Kepler is constructive on Indra and Guillaume Jaisson (Goldman) favours Aerospace and Defence. The capital markets day's outcome is not in the pack.

Relative strength (27 November against 25 September, not week on week) agrees with the defensive regime and disagrees with the energy rows. Healthcare A, Transportation A, Consumer staples B and Professional services B lead; Telecoms, Defence, Media and Real assets read F. The two-month falls: Technology to D from B, Semiconductors to F from A, Hardware to F from C, Electrical equipment to F from B, Metals and mining to F from B, Energy to C from A. Risk-on-ness is off on every window of the European test and on over the last week only in the names that fell most in November.

Topic 2: is the Consumer staples Overweight protection or a funding source? From the mini updates on the 23, 26 and 27-Nov-2026 closes

Judgement. Both, and the Overweight is still right: Consumer staples protects on falling days and over every window that matters to a twelve-month view, and it is the funding source on rebound days led by November's losers. The row holds at Overweight with moderate conviction, declining its own written condition for Double Overweight, which fired on its letter this week. Direction for a European long-only book: hold staples as the defensive core, do not add at the top letter, and manage the rebound-day cost through position lines rather than through the tilt.

Why, ranked by evidence. First, the protection case (strongest): over 20 sessions the Overweight leads (up 4.7% against the index down 1.9%, 26 Nov); in the week to 25 November staples beat the index by 2.6 points; defensives lead cyclicals on all five windows, +9.5 points at three months; the Consumer staples Industry Memo (26 Nov) reads the tailwind as supportive and moderate over three months. Second, the funding-source hypothesis (supported on three days): staples lagged on all three rebound days of the week, were the only industry down on 27 November (minus 0.2%), and Carlsberg fell 2.1% on above-average volume with no company news; two tight-line tranches in defensive holdings (Hilton Food, Helios Towers) confirmed exits on an up day. Third, crowding (supported at the margin): Jaisson's 14% rotation is of typical full size, and the Industry Memo says the tailwind is neutral by twelve months because its two strong forces (money returning to an out-of-favour industry, defensive buying) are flow forces with weak fundamentals beneath them. The hypothesis the commission states is confirmed as a description of rebound days and rejected as a reason to change the tilt.

Counter-case. That Overweight is already too much: if the correction ends here, the market's first act is to sell its hiding places, and staples lagging on every up day is the market saying so. What would refute the judgement is two further weeks in which staples lag on up days and no longer lead on down days, or the three-month defensives line falling under +2 points.
