APM Mini-Memo. Sectra. The sixth of November 2026.

Sectra is rank seven on the Ideas Bench in the lists drawn from the Pool of Stocks Eligible for Middle Innings Portfolio Selection, promoted this week from the top of the Shortlist.

The one-line view. Sectra is the share leader in hospital imaging software, converting a signed backlog worth three billion kronor a year into revenue and margin, and the case depends on how fast hospitals let it deploy.

First, what the company does.

Sectra is a Swedish software group that sells imaging systems to hospitals. Net sales were three and a half billion kronor in the year to April, up nine percent, with an operating profit of seven hundred and eleven million. Imaging makes six sevenths of sales. Its system is the one radiologists, cardiologists and pathologists use to view and report on scans, now sold as a cloud subscription called Sectra One. A second business, Secure Communications, sells encrypted communications and cyber monitoring to governments and defence. Secure Communications is about an eighth of sales and grew eleven percent. Sectra holds fifteen percent of the world market for imaging systems, ahead of Philips at eleven and Pro Medicus at six. Hospitals rank Sectra top for clinical satisfaction. Customer losses run at half a percent a year. The founders still run the company. Torbjörn Kronander has been chief executive since 2012, and he and the chairman each control seventeen percent of the votes.

Second, the thesis.

Sectra has moved from selling upfront licences to cloud subscriptions, and the accounts now show it. Recurring revenue is sixty-nine percent of sales. Cloud recurring revenue grew fifty-five percent in the year to April, and seventy-five percent in the latest figures on file, from September. Behind that growth sit signed contracts worth about three billion kronor a year in recurring revenue. The largest is Québec, at three point one billion kronor for a hundred and fifty hospitals over twelve years. Others are Emory Healthcare in the United States and a London hospital consolidation. Between thirty and forty percent of guaranteed bookings become revenue within a year.

Margin should follow revenue. Each deployment costs money up front for cloud hosting before the subscription scales, and Sectra runs three hosting platforms in parallel while it migrates customers. As contracts go live and old platforms retire, DNB Carnegie sees imaging margins rising by more than ten points from today's twenty percent. Mid-teens revenue growth and a rising margin together give the consensus earnings growth of about thirty percent a year. That is the engine of the case.

Track three things. The quarterly growth in cloud recurring revenue. The imaging margin against its twenty percent base. And the recovery of Secure Communications from product delays that management expects to last through this financial year.

Third, the Target Setup.

There is no Investment Analyst memo, so there are no Bottom-up, Stock-driven ratings, and the setup read is my own. The Target Setup is Demand-driven EPSU, with Big Corporate Change-driven EPSU alongside it. The demand is signed and contracted, and the change is the move to subscriptions that is now releasing margin. There is no cause for concern to clear. The shares sit five percent below their one-year high.

Fourth, Case Riskiness and simplicity.

The case is simple but single-threaded. The case rests on one variable, deployment speed, and hospital IT departments control that, not Sectra. A migration takes twelve to eighteen months and can stretch to twenty-four. European hospitals hesitate over public cloud. Hosting costs arrive before the revenue does, so slow onboarding is the single point of failure for near-term earnings. What needs to go right is that Québec, Emory and the other large contracts go live on plan through 2027, with cloud recurring revenue growing fifty percent or more and the margin holding at twenty percent or better.

Three smaller things sit beneath. Management reinvests everything above a fifteen percent margin into growth, so the margin ceiling is a policy choice. Over seventy percent of sales are unhedged, and each one percent move in the krona moves operating profit by nine million kronor. Capitalised development rose by a third to ninety-nine million kronor, which flatters reported profit if replacement orders slow. In plain words, this is a clear case with one real risk, not a simple one.

Fifth, business quality.

Quality is high. Healthcare imaging is non-cyclical spend with a demographic floor. Over-sixty-five populations grow about three percent a year in the United States and two percent in Europe, and the American radiologist shortage is expected to reach ten percent by 2034. Once a hospital puts radiology, cardiology and pathology on one platform, switching is painful, which is what half a percent churn shows. The balance sheet holds net cash worth one and a half years of cash profit. Management beat its five-year goal of fifty percent growth in operating profit per share, delivering a hundred and three percent.

The flaw is economics. Pro Medicus, with a third of Sectra's market share, earns a seventy-four percent operating margin against Sectra's twenty. Pro Medicus charges per study on one product. Sectra sells enterprise licences with integrated third-party applications and parallel hosting. Sectra buys its consolidation wins and low churn with lower revenue per exam, and the margin question is the one the thesis has to answer.

Sixth, returns and valuation.

The shares closed at three hundred and twenty-eight kronor on the sixth of November, a market value of four point seven billion euros. That is fifty-one times current earnings, which is the twenty-sixth percentile of Sectra's own ten-year history. Forward earnings are not in our files. The weekly routine's plausible return of the twenty-ninth of October is sixty-five percent a year, grade A, but it assumes the multiple rises to eighty-seven times, and I would not lean on that. On earnings alone your bar is met. Consensus earnings growth of about thirty percent a year on a flat multiple, plus a small dividend, clears twenty-five percent. The bear case of thirteen percent growth, an eighteen percent margin and forty times returns little. The weekly TSR/Valuation rating of the sixth of November is B, a valuation reading only. Sell-side Momentum of the fifth of November is B. Technical Momentum of the sixth of November is A, with the shares in a healthy pullback.

Seventh, the value chain and thematics.

For the case. The Sector Digest's first rule is to sort software by how the vendor charges. Sectra prices on enterprise usage, not on seats, so artificial intelligence that reads scans faster adds billable volume rather than cutting the bill. Hospital software fell a quarter in the twelve months to mid October, while the wider software sector turned up eighteen percent in three months and Nordic software rose nine percent in the month to late October as disruption fears faded. Sectra is the one name in its cohort whose transition is proven in the accounts. The new European cyber rules, with fines of up to two percent of turnover, turn compliance into procurement for Secure Communications. My own reading is that European sovereignty preferences favour a Swedish vendor with private cloud options.

Against the case. Secure Communications depends on government procurement, and its delivery delays persist into this financial year. Bookings fell forty-six percent against the Québec comparison, so the backlog is being drawn down faster than it is refilled for now. The weekly Thematic Fit/Momentum rating of the sixth of November is B, an environment reading with no stock layer.
