Report 1
G10 and European macroeconomic inputs (source #1)
Executive summary
  • The read: United States inflation surprised to the upside as the Iran re-escalation pushed energy higher; the houses argue whether it is a passing shock or a second leg
  • Strongest view: Goldman Sachs US Economics (13-Oct) say tariffs and the Iran energy shock explain 0.9 of the 1 point overshoot and artificial intelligence (AI) demand only 45 basis points, half mismeasurement, so core inflation still falls toward 2% next year
  • Sharpest disagreement: the International Monetary Fund's Tobias Adrian is "relatively sanguine" on growth; Moody's Mark Zandi sees one-in-three odds of the US going off the rails (Goldman Sachs Top of Mind, 13-Oct)
  • Change on the week: Goldman Sachs moved from "an October hike is unlikely" (12-Oct) to "October possible, December most likely" (15-Oct)

1. Inflation

Goldman Sachs (Jan Hatzius, 14-Oct) read September core consumer price index (CPI) of 0.33% as a genuine miss against their 0.24%, led by shelter. The 15-Oct producer price index (PPI) was worse, with energy up 10.8% "reflecting the re-escalation of the conflict in Iran". Yet their 13-Oct paper argues the overshoot is not AI-driven: AI adds about 45 basis points, peaking by end-2026 and fading to 10 by end-2027. Barclays Economics (11-Oct) call the UK equivalent "a renewed cost-of-living shock": CPI 3.1% in 2026, 3.0% in 2027, not entrenched.

Disagreement: the Federal Reserve blames AI demand in its hawkish pivot (Goldman Sachs, 13-Oct); Goldman Sachs dispute the size.

Why it matters: an energy and tariff overshoot fades in 2027 and takes discount-rate pressure with it; an AI-driven one does not.

StatusAbove target in the US and UK
TrendHeadline rising on energy, core sticky
Change in trendYes, US core stepped up after two benign months
SkewUpside near term, a fall in 2027 per both houses

Evidence: Goldman Sachs, Hatzius, 14-Oct and 15-Oct; Goldman Sachs, 13-Oct; Barclays, 11-Oct

2. Growth and the sentiment surveys

Goldman Sachs (15-Oct) call the US "a healthy pace of economic activity but no sign of a surge" and expect consumer spending to slow in the fourth quarter. Their global economists (Johan Allen, 13-Oct) estimate each 100 basis points of higher rates costs developed-market gross domestic product (GDP) about 0.6%, worst where mortgages reset (Sweden, Australia, Canada): a 0.4 to 0.6 point drag on 2027 growth.

Sentiment tracker: one survey reported inside the window. University of Michigan, United States, preliminary October (Goldman Sachs, 9-Oct): sentiment 46.3 from 48.1, down; current conditions 44.7, the lowest in the survey's history; expectations 47.3, up 1.0; one-year inflation expectations 4.7%, five-to-ten-year 3.5%, both up 0.1. No reading this week in the evidence for the Conference Board (US), GfK (UK, Germany), the European Commission flash, or national offices for France, Italy, Japan, Canada, Switzerland, Sweden, Norway, Australia and New Zealand; web searches 9 and 10 returned only 2025 and July 2026 releases.

Disagreement: Adrian sanguine, Goldman Sachs economists a modest drag unless yields stay here, Zandi one in three (Top of Mind, 13-Oct).

Why it matters: every house expects the consumer to weaken this winter; none forecasts recession.

StatusUS healthy, sentiment at a record low on conditions
TrendDecelerating into the fourth quarter
Change in trendNone in direction
SkewDownside, 0.4 to 0.6 points of 2027 growth

Evidence: Goldman Sachs, 15-Oct, 13-Oct, 9-Oct

3. Fiscal and monetary trust

Goldman Sachs (Mark Wilson, 9-Oct) frame the rate rise as a funding-competition regime: 98 of the 108 basis points of the 10-year sell-off are real rates and "investors see ghosts around every corner", so Le Pen's 2% French deficit target was read as unachievable. Barclays Rates (11-Oct) say gilt yields rose in line with peers, so Chancellor Healey should "let sleeping dogs lie". Morgan Stanley (Ariana Salvatore, 15-Oct) expect the US midterms to change little. Goldman Sachs see a December Fed hike under Chairman Warsh and a November Bank of England hike with less tightening than priced (9-Oct); Barclays (9-Oct) see a November Riksbank hike, the Swiss National Bank (SNB) on hold.

Disagreement: Goldman Sachs GOAL (12-Oct) call euro-area sovereign stress "elevated"; Wilson cites Ireland's turnaround.

Why it matters: fiscal credibility is now priced through real rates, the discount rate on every European equity.

StatusStrained, not broken
TrendFed, Bank of England and Riksbank tightening; SNB the exception
Change in trendFed hike timing pulled forward
SkewHawkish on rates; fiscal headlines are the tail

G10 and European macroeconomic outputs (source #2)
Executive summary
  • The read: the discount rate rose again on a hot United States inflation print and equities stopped ignoring it: the S&P 500 ended a 53-day run without a 1% fall, Asia hit circuit breakers, and the first artificial intelligence (AI) credit crack appeared (Outset Global, 15-Oct)
  • Strongest view: Goldman Sachs (Mark Wilson, 9-Oct): the AI investment cycle drives yields, so expect no major reversal, but stable energy would relieve bonds and let equities rally into year-end
  • Sharpest disagreement: Goldman Sachs GOAL (12-Oct) went overweight equities on three months after the de-rating; Jefferies' high yield desk (15-Oct) says credit needs NVIDIA and Anthropic to step in
  • Change on the week: October Federal Reserve (Fed) hike pricing back to 50% (Outset Global, 15-Oct); CoreWeave 2032 bonds below 78 cents (14-Oct)

1. Rates and bond yields

Goldman Sachs (13-Oct): global rates highest since 2007, not expected to stay there. Wilson (9-Oct) explains the move as real rates (98 of 108 basis points on the US 10-year), long yields "tightly correlated to 12-month forward crude prices", and the equity-to-rates correlation flipped to a 1990s level. GOAL (Christian Mueller-Glissmann, 12-Oct) went overweight bonds on 12 months, long duration offering "better medium-term value as inflation normalises". Policy: Goldman Sachs expect a December Fed hike, October possible (15-Oct), and a November Bank of England hike (9-Oct); Barclays (9-Oct) a November Riksbank hike.

Disagreement: Daily Chartbook (10-Oct): investors "aggressively betting on a sudden turnaround in interest rates".

Why it matters: Goldman Sachs Top of Mind (13-Oct): further rises in yields or rate volatility "could test risk assets".

StatusHighest since 2007, US 10-year about 5.1%
TrendUp, real-rate led
Change in trendYes, a hot inflation print reversed the prior week's relief
SkewGoldman Sachs expect lower; the near-term risk they name is higher

Evidence: Goldman Sachs, 9-Oct, 12-Oct, 13-Oct; Barclays, 9-Oct; Outset Global, 15-Oct

2. Credit

Jefferies' Michael Haber (15-Oct): hyperscaler free cash flow troughs near zero in 2026-27, so the next leg of a $5.4 trillion build to 2030 must be financed externally; NVIDIA backstopping the neoclouds and bullish Anthropic data are "key to calming credit and equity markets". Outset Global (14-Oct): CoreWeave's 2032 debt below 78 cents, credit default swaps implying over 50% default odds.

Disagreement: Goldman Sachs strategists (Top of Mind, 13-Oct) are "not especially concerned about banks or markets more broadly"; GOAL (12-Oct) is still underweight credit.

Why it matters: Jefferies (15-Oct) say slower data-centre investment hits ASML and ASM hardest in Europe.

StatusDistress in levered AI credits, no general widening reported

Why it matters: energy sets the cost line and, via Wilson's crude-to-yields link, the discount rate.

4. Risk appetite and Richard's diagnostics

Are equities ignoring the discount rate? No. Outset Global (15-Oct): higher volatility and yields "creating a selling signal for systematic investors", AI hardware down 5%, healthcare and staples outperforming, VIX at a four-week high. Asia: KOSPI 22% off its September high, record foreign selling in Taiwan, Asian long-short ratios still at five-year highs, "more pain to come".

Cracks underneath: defensives outperforming, yes. Momentum: Wilson (9-Oct) calls it "a painful headwind since the end of Q2"; US long-short net length is in the 1st percentile. Large over small, cash yield over growth, small-cap growth against large-cap yield: no source spoke to these; the nearest proxy is Goldman Sachs' non-profitable tech basket, down 5.3% then up 3.3% (Outset Global, 14-Oct and 15-Oct).

Is this 2007 into 2008? No source said so. Goldman Sachs date the rate level to 2007 (13-Oct), Wilson asks what the 1990s correlation level marked last time, Zandi puts one-in-three on going off the rails (Top of Mind, 13-Oct).

Index risk/reward: modal case, earnings still supportive, Europe on 13 times with 22% second-half earnings growth (Wilson, 9-Oct); cone, wider on "elevated rates volatility" (GOAL, 12-Oct); skew, GOAL say the de-rating "improved asymmetry" to the upside, while Jefferies (Dan Stratemeier, 13-Oct) count the seventh semis sell-off over 9% since June.

StatusRisk-off, led by AI and Asia

Change in trendYes, the US joined the sell-off
SkewGoldman Sachs upside on earnings; Jefferies downside on credit

Evidence: Outset Global, 14-Oct, 15-Oct; Goldman Sachs, 9-Oct, 12-Oct, 13-Oct; Jefferies, 13-Oct

Stock-market and business/demand-level thematic narratives and developments (source #3)
Executive summary
  • The read: the artificial intelligence (AI) theme split in two this week; suppliers say demand still exceeds supply; the market priced the financing as the weak link
  • Strongest view: Goldman Sachs (Mark Wilson, 9-Oct): the AI investment theme "is the central driver of cross asset price behaviour", and leadership flips from spend beneficiaries to the biggest investors now showing returns, so "don't fight market narrowness"
  • Sharpest disagreement: Jefferies (15-Oct) see ASML and ASM as Europe's most vulnerable names if AI end-demand disappoints; TSMC "sees no slowdown"
  • Change on the week: sceptics gained a credit event (CoreWeave) and a demand doubt (Anthropic's investor message "slightly short of the bar", Jefferies, 15-Oct); bulls gained TSMC guidance above consensus and ASML interest reaching 2029-30

1. AI capital spending

The bull case rests on the suppliers. Jefferies (15-Oct) relay TSMC guiding fourth-quarter revenue of $52.3 to 54.3 billion against $50 billion consensus, AI accelerator revenue compounding at a mid-to-high 50% rate, and power "not yet constraining the outlook". Jefferies (Janardan Menon, 14-Oct) report ASML's orders strengthening for 2028-29 and 2029-30 interest "much broader than just one specific customer". Morgan Stanley (Ariana Salvatore, 15-Oct) keep $1.5 trillion of hyperscaler capital spending for 2027 in a "conditional buildout". Goldman Sachs GOAL (12-Oct): AI-infrastructure beneficiaries generate more than half of third-quarter S&P 500 earnings growth.

The sceptics attack the money, not the orders. Jefferies' Michael Haber (15-Oct): hyperscaler free cash flow troughs near zero in 2026-27, cumulative needs reach $5.4 trillion for the four largest by 2030, and credit markets "are being asked to absorb a massive amount of new supply". Jefferies' Dan Stratemeier (13-Oct) counts the seventh isolated semis sell-off since June; narratives "ebb and flow with positioning".

Why it matters: Jefferies (14-Oct, 15-Oct) expect the European semiconductor sector to de-rate further even on strong earnings; Wilson expects narrowness to intensify, unhelpful for a diversified European book.

StatusSupplier demand strong, financing and sentiment weak

2. AI, power and inflation

Goldman Sachs US Economics (13-Oct) argue the Federal Reserve over-states AI as an inflation source: memory, electronics, data-centre electricity and industrial metals add about 45 basis points to core inflation, half mismeasurement, peaking by year-end and fading to 10 by end-2027. Morgan Stanley (15-Oct) see the power constraint turning political: governors in New York, Virginia and Texas attach conditions, and the likely middle ground is developers absorbing energy costs. TSMC (via Jefferies, 15-Oct) says power has not reduced its forecast.

Disagreement: the Fed's hawkish pivot cites AI demand; Goldman Sachs say it is near its peak.

Why it matters: utilities and grid suppliers are Europe's route into the theme; a "conditional buildout" keeps spending but moves it between regions.

StatusPower is a political constraint, not yet a physical one

SkewBalanced; spending preserved, location contingent
