APM Mini-Memo. Reply. The thirteenth of November 2026.

Reply is rank sixteen on the Ideas Bench, in the lists drawn from the Pool of Stocks Eligible for Middle Innings Portfolio Selection.

The one-line view. Reply sells technology projects at a fixed price, so automation can become Reply's margin, and the case depends on clients not taking that saving back when contracts renew.

First, what the company does.

Reply is an Italian technology consultancy. Reply is built as a federation of about three hundred small specialist firms, each with its own profit and loss account. Those firms design and build digital, cloud, data and artificial intelligence systems for large clients. Most of Reply's staff work close to the client. Revenue was about two and a half billion euros in 2025, at an operating margin of nearly sixteen per cent. Italy supplied about half of that revenue.

About seventy per cent of Reply's revenue is fixed-price work. Reply quotes for a finished result, not for hours. No client is more than a tenth of sales. About four fifths of growth comes from existing clients buying more.

Second, the thesis.

The story is that artificial intelligence raises Reply's earnings, while investors still fear that the same technology shrinks them. A fixed price lets Reply keep the saving when its engineers work faster. A consultancy that bills by the hour hands that saving to the client. That fear took Reply's shares from one hundred and sixty-eight euros in March 2025 to ninety euros in March 2026. The shares stood at one hundred and thirty-five euros on the thirteenth of November.

The evidence so far supports the growth half of the story. Organic growth rose to eight per cent in the second quarter of 2026, from under seven per cent in the first. EPAM grew three and a half per cent, and Globant shrank slightly. Deutsche Bank then raised its profit forecasts for Reply, by three per cent for 2026 and by eight to nine per cent for the two years after.

Three drivers carry the story to earnings per share. The first driver is organic growth of seven to eight per cent a year. The second driver is the operating margin. That margin fell to fourteen and a half per cent in the first half of 2026, from about fifteen and a half, because Reply launched thirty to forty start-up units. Nearly all of those units work on artificial intelligence. The third driver is the share buyback. Shareholders authorised up to five hundred and fifty million euros in April 2026. One of our research reports expects a slower pace, of eighty to one hundred million euros a year.

The next twelve months can improve all three drivers. The start-up units begin to earn their costs. German growth has already recovered, to five and a half per cent from under one per cent. And FedTec, the American government contractor that Reply bought in July, adds about fifty million euros of revenue in 2027, at group margins.

Third, the Target Setup.

No Investment Analyst memo exists for Reply. So there are no Bottom-up/Stock-driven ratings, and the Target Setup read is my own. The Target Setup is CfC Clearing in HQ low-cyclicality business on Q1/Q2 multiple, leading to reversion to normal. The cause for concern is artificial intelligence deflating consultancy fees. Accelerating growth is the evidence of that concern clearing. The fit holds only if Reply's earnings multiple really is low, and I question that multiple in part six.

Fourth, Case Riskiness and simplicity.

The case is simple to state and harder to verify. One thing needs to go right. Reply's contract prices must hold when fixed-price contracts come up for renewal. The main risk is that clients see engineers working about thirty per cent faster and demand the saving. Our research models contracts renewing at half the billable hours. The downside scenario in our research puts earnings per share at five and a half euros by 2028, and fair value at sixty-one euros. The research gives that scenario about a one in four chance.

Three signals show which way the risk is going. Average contract size falling below one million euros would show deflation arriving. Organic growth below four per cent for two quarters would break the case. And the German margin, about thirteen per cent in the second quarter, needs to move towards fifteen.

The first check is the third quarter. One research report in our files says organic revenue fell just over one per cent in the third quarter. That report is dated the eleventh of November, the day before Reply's results. Reply's share price was only four per cent below its high for the year on the thirteenth of November. In my reading, that price does not look like a miss. So the real third-quarter figure needs confirming before anything else.

Fifth, business quality.

Business quality is high. Free cash flow has compounded at nineteen per cent a year over the last decade. Capital spending is under two per cent of revenue. Net cash was about four hundred million euros at the end of June. Reply reports profit with no adjustments, while our research finds peers flattering theirs by thirty to sixty per cent. The founding family holds about seventy per cent of the votes. Family control brings stability, and leaves minority shareholders little say.

Sixth, returns and valuation.

Reply looks attractive on our numbers, but the key input needs checking. The weekly routine rates TSR/Valuation A, as of the thirteenth of November, on a valuation reading only. The same routine computes a plausible return averaging about forty per cent a year. Your bar for an A is twenty-five per cent or more. That return assumes earnings growth of about eight per cent a year. That return also assumes the price-to-earnings multiple rises from thirteen to nearly twenty-two.

Nearly all of that return is re-rating. Earnings growth, a dividend yield of about one per cent and the buyback give something near twelve per cent a year. Twelve per cent is the bottom of your scale. And the multiple of thirteen sits oddly with the rest of our files. Reply's shares are fifty per cent above the March low. Brokers' targets run from one hundred and twenty-one to one hundred and forty euros. The historic average multiple is twenty-three. Confirming the earnings figure behind the multiple of thirteen decides whether Reply is cheap.

Seventh, the value chain and thematics.

The pros are how Reply charges, and what forces its clients to spend. Our Sector work finds that fixed-price sellers keep the automation gain. European rules on cyber security, banking resilience and artificial intelligence make much of the demand compulsory. The weekly routine rates Sell-side Momentum B, as of the twelfth of November, and Technical Momentum B, as of the thirteenth.

The cons are a weak sector backdrop and Italy's public spending. The weekly Thematic Fit/Momentum rating is C, based on the sector environment alone. Faster price falls are the sector's main listed threat. Italy's recovery-plan funding peaks in 2026. The market studies in our files point to moderate growth afterwards, not a cliff.

In short, four things. Reply is interesting because of how Reply charges. Growth is improving now. The case depends on contract prices holding at renewal. And two checks come before more work: the true third-quarter growth figure, and the earnings behind the multiple of thirteen. The natural next step in our process is an Investment Analyst memo.

The one-line view. Reply sells technology projects at a fixed price, so automation can become Reply's margin, and the case depends on clients not taking that saving back when contracts renew.
