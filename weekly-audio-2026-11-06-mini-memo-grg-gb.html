APM Mini-Memo. Greggs. The sixth of November 2026.

Greggs sits at rank twelve on the Ideas Bench in the lists drawn from the Pool of Stocks Eligible for Middle Innings Portfolio Selection, promoted this week from the Shortlist.

The one-line view. Greggs is a cash inflection you can count, as capital spending falls from its 2025 peak, resting on a shop-volume recovery you cannot yet see.

First, what the company does.

Greggs is the United Kingdom's largest value food-on-the-go chain. It sells sausage rolls, bakes, sandwiches and coffee from over two thousand seven hundred shops, about four in five run by the company and the rest franchised. Revenue in 2025 was just over two billion pounds, up about seven percent. Nearly ninety percent came from the company's own shops, and the rest from franchise and wholesale, including frozen products in Tesco and Iceland. The unusual feature is that Greggs makes its own food in fifteen production and logistics hubs. That gives a gross margin of about sixty-one percent and an operating margin of nearly nine percent in 2025, against about five percent for rivals who outsource. Greggs holds about nine percent of the twenty-four billion pound daytime food-to-go market and leads out-of-home breakfast. Its prices are about forty percent below Pret A Manger, though a supermarket basket is still twelve percent cheaper.

Second, the thesis.

The story is a capital spending cycle turning into cash. Greggs spent nearly two hundred and ninety million pounds in 2025, the peak of a programme of new hubs at Derby and Kettering built to supply three and a half thousand shops. Spending falls to about one hundred and eighty million this year, below the original guidance of two hundred million, and to between one hundred and fifty and one hundred and seventy million from 2027. Our research puts 2027 free cash flow at about one hundred and fifteen million pounds, a six percent yield, against negative free cash flow in 2025. The first half of this year showed the turn. Operating cash flow after leases rose to one hundred and eleven million pounds, capital spending halved to seventy-eight million, and the company held forty-six million of net cash.

Earnings per share have a newer lever in costs. Greggs will close four manufacturing sites across 2028 and 2029 for twenty million pounds of annual savings. That is about twelve percent of last year's pre-tax profit, and brokers had not included it in their July forecasts. Because Greggs sets its own shop prices, the saving can stay in profit rather than go to customers, which a grocer cannot manage. Behind that sit one hundred to one hundred and ten net new shops a year, wage inflation halving to four percent this year, and an operating margin already up a percentage point in the first half.

Three drivers to track. Like-for-like volume separated from price, because each one percent of like-for-like sales moves earnings per share by about eight percent. Utilisation of the Derby and Kettering hubs, which cost forty basis points of margin a year through 2027 until volume fills them. And shop openings against the floor of one hundred a year.

Third, the Target Setup.

The Target Setup is CfC Clearing in HQ low-cyclicality business on Q1/Q2 multiple, leading to reversion to normal. That read is my own, since our card names none. The cause for concern is falling shop volumes and the fear that weight-loss drugs make the fall permanent. The business is steady daytime food. The multiple, fourteen point six times forward earnings, sits at the twenty-fourth percentile of its ten-year range, against a historical average near twenty-one times. The case matches this Setup only in part, and the Investment Analyst's memo of the fourteenth of August rated Setups' Fit C, because the concern has not cleared. Like-for-like growth slowed to one percent in the final seven weeks of the first half, with volumes about one percent down. A demand-driven step-up is the bull case if volumes turn, but our files do not yet support it.

Fourth, Case Riskiness and simplicity.

The case is simple to state and hard to confirm. The Investment Analyst's memo of the fourteenth of August rated Case Riskiness C, where A is simple and low-risk, and rated all four Bottom-up ratings C. Those grades were mapped from the memo and have not been re-judged since. One country, one brand and two levers, cash and volume, make the case simple. The number of headwinds makes it risky. What needs to go right is a return to flat or positive like-for-like volumes while the new hubs ramp, so that a fixed manufacturing base is filled rather than carried.

The main risk is that the volume decline is structural. Like-for-like volumes are down about five percent over two years. Weight-loss drug use in UK households has risen from about two percent in 2024 to over six percent this year. Grocers held their sandwich prices while Greggs raised its own by ten percent over two years. And the vertical integration that earns the margin turns any volume shortfall into manufacturing deleverage. The risk work on file reads the stock as a possible false friend, where strong total sales from new shops and wholesale mask weakening volumes in the existing estate. Its triggers to re-examine the case are managed like-for-like at one percent or lower, Derby costs above the guided ten million pound second-half headwind, and openings below one hundred. Smaller risks are nearly ten percent of shares on loan, two activists, Lauro and Silchester, and a chief financial officer handover at the end of December during the hub ramp.

Fifth, business quality.

Greggs is a strong franchise whose returns are at a low point. It gained visit share while total UK out-of-home visits fell, and its first-half revenue growth of seven percent led the sector against a median of four. The balance sheet is net cash where pub and restaurant peers carry debt. Return on capital fell from twenty percent to sixteen percent in 2025 under wage inflation and peak investment, and management targets a return to twenty. The volume problem reads as sector-wide rather than company execution. Pay outcomes look honest, with the bonus at eighteen percent of maximum in a weak year.

Sixth, returns and valuation.

The weekly reading is generous and mine is more cautious. At twenty-three pounds forty on the sixth of November, three percent below the fifty-two week high, the shares trade at fourteen point six times forward earnings. The weekly TSR/Valuation rating of the sixth of November is B on valuation alone. The routine's plausible return of the twenty-ninth of October averages thirty-three percent a year, grade A, on consensus earnings growth of nearly eight percent a year and the multiple moving from fourteen point six to twenty-one times, dividends included. That multiple carries the return. The shares have risen from eighteen pounds forty-six in August to above the sell-side median target of seventeen pounds forty, so the ex-growth discount is partly closed. My own read is this. With the multiple unchanged, consensus earnings growth plus the dividend is a D. With volumes confirmed flat and the 2028 savings entering forecasts, a move toward eighteen to twenty-one times gives a B to an A.

Seventh, the value chain and thematics.

The pros. The weekly Thematic Fit rating of the sixth of November is A, from the food retail environment. Danske Bank names discount food retail as the place to be with rising inflation, and defensives with pricing power led in October. Greggs is domestic, in sterling, net cash and value-positioned, and its new hubs and hedged inputs give savings that it keeps.

The cons. Demand is the weak link. Visits are falling, shoppers resist price rises, grocers take share with held sandwich prices and meal deals, Gail's and Wenzel's expand at the premium end, and weight-loss drugs cut appetite for the core savoury range. Regulated labour is thirty-nine percent of costs, nine percentage points above the retail median. The Lists judgement's doubt stands. The neighbouring consumer row sits at a heavy underweight in your target allocation.

The one-line view. Greggs is a cash inflection you can count, as capital spending falls from its 2025 peak, resting on a shop-volume recovery you cannot yet see.
