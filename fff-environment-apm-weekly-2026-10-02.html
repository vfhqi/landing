The regime in one line

A rates-led late cycle in which the hiking path was marked down for the first time this week, growth is fine at the level and rolling over at the margin, the index is calm, and the risk-off is already running underneath it through breadth, size and rate sensitivity rather than through defensives. Last week's regime (late-cycle hiking into a five per cent discount rate) stands; what changed is the first sign of relief in the rates path and the first widening of credit outside this year's ranges, and those two point in opposite directions.

Macro inputs: the causes

1. G10 inflation (read, United States only). United States core personal consumption expenditures (PCE) inflation printed 3.01% year on year for August, below expectations, and Goldman Sachs now expects 3.0% core PCE for 2026 against the Federal Open Market Committee median of 3.4%. Vice Chair Jefferson still fears energy and artificial intelligence costs leaking into core; James Ferguson (MacroStrategy) says there is no inflation outside energy and airfares, 8% of the basket, with core consumer price index inflation at 2.4%. Conference Board inflation expectations are 6.1%. No source addressed European, United Kingdom or Japanese inflation data, so the European leg of this row is not read this week. My read: core inflation is decelerating, energy pass-through is the live risk, and the direction of the discount rate turned from "still rising" to "two-sided" this week.

2. G10 economic health and consumer surveys (read, with a European consumer hole). United States third-quarter growth tracks +3.4% and the ISM is 54.5. French and German purchasing managers' indices (PMIs) are back in expansion and the euro area shows "a firm expansion" (Marshall, 25-Sep). Swedish consumer confidence is above normal for the first time since 2021, with forestry orders and construction order books improved "tangibly" (DNB Carnegie, 29-Sep). United Kingdom unemployment is expected to peak at 5.2% in early 2027. Consumer sentiment: the United States Conference Board fell to 81.9, the lowest since 2014, while the retail control group grew 5.6% year on year, so confidence and spending disagree. No University of Michigan, GfK, European Commission or national-office consumer reading reached the evidence, so European consumer sentiment is not read this week and I do not infer it from spending or results. My read: growth is above trend in the United States and expanding in Europe, labour and confidence are softening, and Mark Wilson's "the second derivative will slow" is the mix in which cyclicals disappoint before the data does.

3. G10 trust in fiscal and monetary leadership (read). Fed credibility is judged intact after the September hike (Molavi), but President Trump said Chair Powell should be "forced to resign" and Treasury Secretary Bessent urged an "open mind". Every source put the fiscal risk on sovereign debt, naming France (spreads over Germany, the CAC down on the year) and a United Kingdom budget "between a rock and a hard place". No expert set out a view on the European Central Bank's October meeting. My read: the political component of the European discount rate is still rising, and it is concentrated in two countries.

4. Global geopolitics (read for Iran and China; Ukraine unread). The United States rejected Iran's seven-day ceasefire, then Iran offered inspections for sanctions relief while the United States sent 10,000 more troops; Wilson says Iran's leverage window runs to the 2-Nov midterms, so a deflationary energy outcome is live. The Trump-Xi summit brought "stability without resolution" and a two-month truce extension; China's markets are shut until 8-Oct and its activity indicator fell. Ukraine drew one line in one note, which is an absence. For European defence the thematics report says primary issuance is clearing with domestic money (Sensofusion's EUR 317m IPO, 62% taken by Finnish pension funds) and Metlen added defence to its roadmap; the topic evidence adds, as headlines only, that Germany shelved a major warships contract in June. My read: the two legs of the Iran question both route through oil, and that makes energy the most two-sided row in the table.

By 25-Sep the market priced a 4.80% peak, three more hikes than three weeks earlier; by 1-Oct Goldman Sachs had dropped its October hike and expects one in December, possibly none. The Bank of England should hike in November but four priced hikes are "less likely to be realised"; the Bank of Japan is more hawkish; the European Central Bank is called hawkish; Norges Bank hiked and the Riksbank signalled a hiking cycle; the Swiss National Bank held. European bonds underperformed on energy and fiscal concerns, the curve is flatter and macro funds are "taking turns to stop out" (Marshall). My read: the risk-off is real and is being expressed through size, rate sensitivity and breadth, not through sector defensiveness, which is exactly Richard's own point that investors crowd to the simple, proven case within a sector rather than to the defensive label.

Thematics, and risk-on-ness

Artificial intelligence capital spending still carries demand and leadership (United States firm adoption 23.8%, hardware investment $555bn above its 2022 level), but the week's argument was about the bill: Jefferies' Taiwan checks say the high-bandwidth memory despec cuts system cost 44% and the "$1.5-2trn" capital spending numbers should now be lower; foundry and optical are constrained, memory's peak is visible in 2027. Consumer artificial intelligence agents (Meta's Muse) are a demand-destruction theme for brands and retailers whose loyalty is habit, and Amazon blocking Muse is the tell; most value investors think it overblown. Energy is a cash machine the market will not pay for (XOP down 4.8% on WTI down 3.6%). Defence is funding well in primary. Spain's data-centre decree is the live regulatory question for utilities and renewables. Risk-on-ness: equity volatility is low against rates volatility, the index is calm, and the weakness is beneath it; nobody in the evidence made the 2007 into 2008 case, and Wilson's analogy is the mid-1990s.

What would change my mind

Toward risk-on: United States yields actually falling after the Fed path (two consecutive weekly declines in the 10-year), European breadth on the 200-day average recovering above 65% (the 10th percentile on near-high days), an Iran settlement taking Brent toward $80, and credit spreads back inside this year's ranges. Toward a harder risk-off: a second consecutive week of credit widening or any European credit reading outside range, the STOXX Europe 600 breaking lower with defensives outperforming over four weeks and three months at once, a French or United Kingdom sovereign event, and United States payrolls turning negative. The Nordic recovery (Sweden) is the one place demand is visibly turning up, and if it spreads to German and French order books the "rolling over at the margin" half of the regime is wrong.

Topic 1. Why the Recruitment Services Sector's Relative Strength rose so far, and what it means for us

1. The judgement. The Recruitment Services Sector (K.9: Hays, PageGroup, Adecco, Randstad) sits at composite Relative Strength rank 98 because a relief rally off a twelve-month return of -59% was measured by a composite that puts 60% of its weight on the last six months, and the relief was global, synchronous across every labour-service business that artificial intelligence was supposed to automate, and not confined to recruiters or to Europe. Direction, for the group's Relative Strength as a hold for a European long-only book over twelve months: bearish to neutral, with medium conviction. As a hiring-cycle signal: weak, and not an early-cycle signal I can act on; conviction a working hypothesis, because the only hiring evidence in the pack is United States, secondary, and contradicted by the same sources (July job cuts with earlier months revised down).

2. Why it is happening, ranked by how well the evidence supports each explanation.

  • First, the reversal of the artificial-intelligence-disruption fear across the labour-service businesses. For: the move was global and synchronous (ManpowerGroup +120%, Robert Half +94%, ZipRecruiter +183%, Recruit Holdings ADRs +170% between March and August; a professional services index +44% from April), the trade press names the earlier fall a "SaaSpocalypse" sell-off and the rebound a change of narrative (artificial-intelligence-generated application volumes make human screening more valuable; staffing firms use artificial intelligence for productivity), and inside Industry K the same relief lifted the other artificial-intelligence-exposed service names: WPP +56.6%, Teleperformance +41.6%, MITIE +38.6% (Table 4), while sectors with no such fear moved little (Testing and inspection +2.5%, Security and facilities -6.3%). Against: two secondary trade articles, not checked against results; nothing in the pack on the four European recruiters' own trading; the thematics report says most value investors think the consumer-agent theme overblown, which cuts both ways
  • Second, the construction of the composite and the base effect. For: rank went from 1 (the lowest of 95 sectors, twelve-month return -59%) on 3-Jul to 58 on 17-Jul to 98 by 28-Aug, with 40% of the weight on the three-month return and 20% each on six, nine and twelve months, so a +61.9% July alone scores near the top of the universe; the twelve-month return is still negative against the index (Table 1).   • Third, a genuine staffing-cycle turn.   • Fourth, positioning and short covering off a -59% floor. Plausible, and untestable: short interest is not in the pack, and I do not infer it

What the topics teach together

Read a Relative Strength rank once, with its level and its history beside it. A rank is a position in a short queue: in a ninety-five-sector table a floor rebound of one summer scores 98 while the twelve-month return is still negative, and in a sixteen-industry table a seven-point move in the composite is a full grade. The Recruitment rank overstates a recovery and the Defence rank overstates a collapse for the same reason, the weighting toward the last six months and the coarseness of the table. The tilt letter is a twelve-month statement about what we want to own, so it reads the rank as evidence of what the market has already done and never as the answer; where the two disagree, the page says so, as it does for K.9 (Relative Strength A, tilt D) and for Defence (Relative Strength F, tilt C).
