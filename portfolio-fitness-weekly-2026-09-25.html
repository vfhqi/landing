Macro inputs, G10 and European: the causes

1. G10 inflation. Input prices in developed markets reaccelerated in September after months of decline, and US services prices are at their highest since November 2022 (Goldman Sachs, Dong 24 Sep and Hatzius 23 Sep). Inside the same house Mei (24 Sep) says much of the move is "nominal bias" in the purchasing managers' index (PMI) data, with overheating confined to a few AI-linked manufacturing industries. Judgement: the direction is up and the skew is up; I do not need to settle the Dong-Mei argument to know that central banks are hiking into it. Richard's line "inflation is high" is confirmed and now has a fresh upward impulse behind it. The European inflation prints themselves were not readable this week.

2. G10 economic health. Growth is more resilient than the headlines: the US expanding solidly (third-quarter gross domestic product tracking 3.3% annualised, Hatzius 24 Sep), Europe surprising on the upside, China's slowdown "manageable" (Barclays Macro Outlook, 24 Sep). Barclays's own counterweight (Millar, 24 Sep) is a labour-supply fall sharper than the aggregates show, which is a reason hikes persist. Judgement: this is the category where the sources disagree with Richard's view. He wrote "consumer confidence is low... Recession potential"; the sources say US consumer spending exceeds expectations and no house has a recession in its base case. The European consumer evidence is company-level and softer: H&M beat on margin with September sales up 1%, On The Beach's bookings accelerated to 9%, Vistry swung to a loss (Outset and Investec, 24 Sep). I weight the sources on the level of activity (it is firm) and Richard on the mechanism (hiking into a firm economy with a labour squeeze is how late cycles end), and I hold that the recession is a 2027 question inside our twelve-month window rather than a 2026 fact.

3. Trust in fiscal and monetary leadership. Central banks moved from holding to hiking this quarter and yields are sharply higher (Barclays Foreword, 24 Sep). Fiscal credibility is not a debt-ratio question but a political one: the US keeps flexibility as the reserve-currency issuer, while in the euro area "a rise in political uncertainty could prove to be the sufficient condition" for markets to challenge a weak sovereign (Pradhan, 24 Sep). France is the named pocket: an unsettled bond market, a primary deficit near 3% that must reach zero, a spread beta now rivalling Italy's, and Marine Le Pen's October economic plans as the first catalyst (Fiotakis, 24 Sep). Judgement: deteriorating, with a dated catalyst inside the window. This is the one category where the sources supplied a specific European finding that last week's table does not hold.

4. Geopolitics. The US-Iran standoff that Barclays assumed would de-escalate has flared again; a phased Hormuz deal is being explored but Iran's "diplomacy yes, surrender no" left no off-ramp (Outset, 24 Sep). The US-China trade truce was extended to 10 January ahead of Xi's visit (Outset, 23 Sep). Ukraine: no source spoke to it and I say nothing about it. Judgement: two-sided on Iran, low-delta on China until January, and the transmission to a European book is through energy and rates, not through defence budgets, which no source addressed.

Thematics, and risk-on-ness

AI capital spending is the theme every house argues about, and the argument moved from "how much" to "what revenue it must earn": hyperscalers need about $300bn of annual AI revenue to break even, and even the bull case has capex growth slowing in 2027 (Goldman Sachs, Hammond 24 Sep); Burry reads the same spending as a mid-point capital cycle with write-offs "perhaps in 2028 or 2029". Second-order themes that touch European names: optical supply is capacity-constrained and Nokia's 2027-28 consensus is too low (Jefferies, Menon 24 Sep); Meta's "Muse" transacting agent knocked UK price-comparison and classifieds shares 4-8%, and Investec argues the moat holds; a Super El Niño is named as a 2027 food-inflation theme (Jefferies, Shah 24 Sep); private defence "neo-primes" threaten listed European defence multiples (Jefferies, 23 Sep). Judgement on risk-on-ness: the index is risk-on and the factor tape is risk-off. Richard's 2021 example (small-cap growth crushed in the third and fourth quarters before large caps broke on 1 January 2022) describes exactly this configuration, and it is why the fragility axis stays where it is.

The number

The reasoning

The rule is that the larger the gap, the more the lists chase stocks that close it, and the smaller the gap, the more they simply chase good businesses. The gap this week is unmeasured on the book side (component 4) and material on the target side (component 5): 48 of 129 target rows are non-Neutral, Fragility is at Double Underweight, one geography is newly Underweight, and the read behind the table was confirmed this week by the first source pack the system has had, with the factor tape beginning to price it. The choice is between 0.50 (Neutral, treating an unmeasured gap as a moderate one) and 0.625 (Overweighting, treating it as large). I set 0.625 on an asymmetry, not on a number: if the gap is in fact small, the cost of chasing shape is a few good businesses displaced from the lists for a week, which the next week can put back; if the gap is in fact large, the cost of chasing good businesses is a book that is fragile into a trigger, which no later week can put back. Richard's own 2021 example says the factor turn comes first and the index turn after, and this week's tape is the first evidence of the factor turn. Setting 0.50 would be the averaging failure mode the instruction forbids: an unmeasured book is not a moderately fit one.

The counter-case

The gap may be small and I cannot show otherwise. Sixteen holdings on a European quality process could already be large, simple and proven, in which case 0.625 pushes the lists toward shape over quality for no gain, and a week of memos at roughly three quarters of a million tokens each is commissioned on the wrong criterion. The sell side is unanimous that the earnings backdrop does not reward hiding, and Goldman Sachs reads the yield jump as an overreaction; if they are right, the shape the lists are chasing is the wrong one. And 0.75 was available: I did not take it because the factor evidence is one session against a two-month persistence reading, and Double Overweighting on that would be a forecast.

What moves it

  • Down to 0.50: the book measured and shown to sit mostly in the B rows with large, proven names; or a hiking pause with input prices falling back
  • Up to 0.75: a credit spread index widening, a second reading of small caps and momentum leading declines, or the book measured and shown to sit in the D and F rows
