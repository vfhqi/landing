Overview (including process/structure) of the Middle Innings Lists in the Week of the sixth of November 2026.

This is Watson with the overview of this week's Ideas Bench, Ideas Shortlist and Ideas Longlist: what on the Lists deserves your investigation this week, and why, grouped by the topics that connect the stocks.

The headline.

This week's Lists say four things. The top of the Ideas Bench is, for the first time this autumn, made of cases whose next move is a dated test with a number attached, and two of those tests fall within days. Healthcare is the strongest group, nine of the twenty Bench stocks sell into it, and the way to own it is the single case, not the group. Energy is the hedge the target portfolio wants and every memo-backed major refuses to pay for. And most of this week's promotions were charts moving ahead of cases, so the Bench is as much a list of memos to commission as of stocks to buy. Under all four sits one fact: no memo on the Bench argues for capital at today's price.

The first topic: dated tests decide the top of the Bench.

My judgement is that this is the most useful Bench of the autumn, because five of its cases will be settled by scheduled results, and each memo wrote down what a pass looks like before the result was known. That is the condition under which a result can change a view rather than confirm one.

Kainos comes first in time. It sits tenth, but its half-year results arrive on Monday the ninth of November, and its memo is the only one on the Lists that contemplates buying near today's price: accumulate below about eleven pounds sixty, with a ceiling at twelve pounds fifty, and the shares sit between the two. The story is a people business migrating into proprietary software, where the Workday Products line earns twice the gross margin of services. Monday needs to show falling contractor headcount and a recovering services margin, proof that the capacity Kainos hired is being absorbed. Kainos also leaves the Pool of Stocks Eligible for Middle Innings Portfolio Selection next week unless its readings recover, which argues for deciding now.

Demant is second in time and second on the Bench, new this week. Its memo of the eighth of October is bullish on the business, neutral on the shares, and names its test: progress at or below three hundred and five Danish kroner, or sooner if the third-quarter statement due in early November shows wholesale organic growth of eight per cent or more with the gross margin held. The business is a hearing-care oligopolist at its best product-cycle moment in years, repairing a stretched balance sheet out of cash flow, and graded A or B, the top two grades, on five of the Eight Master Ratings. The risk is concentration: ninety per cent of global volume comes out of one Polish plant.

WPP holds first place as a working hypothesis, not a conviction. Six of the eight grades read A or B, the memo of the thirteenth of October progresses the case to an Early-Stage look, and the chart shows a stock qualifying up from Stage one into Stage two, the first tactical shape you asked to upweight. WPP is first because SBM Offshore, above it last week, lost its chart and WPP did not move. The case is a holding company becoming one operating company while artificial intelligence deflates the fee pool around it. Its third-quarter trading update is not yet in our pack, and Foundations' Robustness is the weakest grade on the Bench.

Clariant, promoted from the Shortlist to sixteenth and at your highest interest level, has already had its test. The memo named the third-quarter print on the third of November as the date that decides the Catalysts leg, and the first reading since is Sell-side Momentum rising from C to B. The case is a cause for concern clearing, Middle East catalyst utilisation and German courtrooms, with a self-help engine underneath, and the memo calls the shares compelling at about nine francs thirty-five. One notch of estimate rise is thin evidence, and the print itself has not been read.

BP, eleventh, waits for its third-quarter print in November. The August memo parks it with three numeric release triggers and an automatic reassessment at that print, the right way to hold a leveraged, sub-scale major part-way through a repair. The whole case is net debt inside fourteen to eighteen billion dollars at the end of next year with buybacks reinstated. BP is one of three Bench stocks the Pool marks as leaving next week unless its readings recover.

The second topic: healthcare is where the market's rotation and the Lists' best cases meet.

Healthcare's relative strength went to A on a two-month base while the market bought quality defensives and sold leveraged cyclicals, and the target portfolio holds pharma at Overweight. Healthcare is the defensive with growth attached. My judgement is that the group is right and the way in is the single case where a product cycle, a memo or a dated read-out gives you something to test.

The hearing-care pair is the cleanest expression, because Demant and Sonova face one driver at two prices. Sonova is new at fourteenth with a memo that is bullish on the business and neutral on the shares: self-help on top of a product cycle at the oligopoly leader, with the Sennheiser consumer business being sold. The shares re-rated from about sixteen to twenty times forward earnings between May and October, paying in advance for a launch window not yet won, and the memo's central-case return of about eight and a half per cent a year is an F. Where two oligopolists face the same window, my reading is that you buy the one the market has not paid for.

Orion, fifth, could become the first call on your capital without any new fact about the business. It is a Nordic generics company becoming an oncology-royalty business on Nubeqa, graded A or B on five of the eight, in the pharma row the target Overweights. The memo parks it above seventy-three euros on a return grade set before the prostate-cancer read-out, and this week's Portfolio Fitness for Fighting judgement again asks the Investment Analyst to re-base that return. The risk is that Orion's economics are almost entirely rented from a partner.

Genmab, fourth and up three, is the memo I want most on the Bench. It is an antibody platform with eight approved medicines whose sales grew more than fourfold in six years, overwhelmingly on partner royalties, now spending that franchise to buy its way out of a dated terminal problem. The pullback moved the valuation reading to B this week, and three binary read-outs are near. Royalties were nearly eighty per cent of revenue, and whether the acquisitions can replace them is exactly the question a memo answers.

Sectra, promoted from the top of the Shortlist to seventh, waited there because nothing was on file, and now the research has arrived. It is a share-leading medical imaging software franchise converting a signed three billion Swedish kronor recurring backlog into revenue, with cloud recurring revenue up fifty-five per cent, and the chart is at A on a healthy retest and a pullback. Everything load-bearing resolves to deployment speed, which the customer controls, so track the backlog's conversion rate by half-year.

Merck, new at twentieth, closes the pharma gap and the size gap at once: a large, simple pharma-and-science group under new leadership since May, where Sell-side Momentum moved to A this week. The August memo parked it on four reasons, of which weak estimate momentum was one and is now gone, so the park needs a re-read. Against it, two large acquisitions in a year are the opposite of the simple case the memo asked for.

The third topic: energy is the hedge the target wants and the memos refuse to pay for.

The target portfolio holds energy upstream and services at Overweight and downstream at Double Overweight, the live portfolio holds none of it, and the majors' charts all went to A together this week inside a proven Stage two, the second tactical shape you asked to upweight. Yet every memo-backed major is parked: TotalEnergies' central case falls well short of twenty per cent a year, Eni's memo names a re-entry price below today's, and Repsol's memo is bearish. My judgement is that the energy hedge is a portfolio decision, sized on the Portfolio Fitness for Fighting judgement, and no stock memo will make the case for you.

Shell, eighth and sixteen places above its score, is the largest and simplest form of the hedge, closing the two gaps the live-portfolio judgement ranks first, energy and size. The research describes two clocks: the best near-term clock in the European peer set, trading, refining capture and buyback velocity, and a long-duration clock running down on a reserve life under eight years. For our horizon, my reading is that the near-term clock pays. Shell has no memo and Sell-side Momentum slipped to C, so a memo on Shell would most reorder the energy group: every memo-backed peer reads as already paid for and Shell has not been tested.

TotalEnergies, promoted to nineteenth, is the memo-backed form of Shell: the only European major with organic production growth guided to twenty-thirty, a quality income compounder bought at the top of its own earnings cycle, parked on a central-case return of about eleven per cent a year. It stays on the Bench because it closes the energy and size gaps with a memo behind it, if you decide the hedge is worth its cost in return.

Below the Bench, the group moved. SBM Offshore fell from first on the Bench to first on the Shortlist after a failed retest, but its memo's first re-entry trigger is a price below about twenty-eight euros, so if the shares keep falling, the chart's failure becomes the memo's entry, and that makes it the one demoted name already worth your investigation. Harbour Energy, down to the Longlist, pairs a free cash flow yield near thirty-six per cent with a chart at D and a Pool exit; a yield like that beneath a falling price is either a value trap or the skew you look for, and nothing on file decides which. Aker BP, Ithaca Energy and Equinor left on broken charts and falling estimates, Neste and Galp left the Pool, and the tankers and refiners, Hafnia, TORM, HELLENiQ Energy and Motor Oil, earn record profits on chokepoints they do not control.

The fourth topic: charts moved without cases, so this week's promotions are a list to commission memos on.

The Portfolio Fitness for Fighting judgement held Momentum at Underweight again, because the recovery in breadth is led by defensives and strength is not being rewarded, so a chart alone buys a memo, not capital. My judgement is that the money leaving capital-spending cyclicals wants growth that does not depend on the cycle, and it is trying healthcare, freight and food production first. The cleanest names are those where the chart and a second signal agree and a business is on file.

SCHOTT Pharma, promoted from the Longlist to ninth, improved on the broadest front in the Pool: the chart to A on a Basing setup inside a proven Stage two, Sell-side Momentum to A and the valuation reading to B, all in one week. The quality is not in dispute, with ninety-seven per cent repeat revenue and drug-master-file lock-in. What holds it at ninth is that the Pool marks it as leaving, week one of two, and near-term earnings are hostage to one segment's utilisation and one customer's order book.

Kerry, sixth and up three, reads first on the raw score because the environment likes it. The chart moved to a healthy retest and a pullback inside a proven Stage two, and food production is turning on the Stage two screen. What is on file is unchanged: a taste and nutrition company winning volume and losing margin, with last year's revenue below the year before. My reading is that the market's story is volume share compounding into margin in time, and whether that is a change thesis or a management plan is the memo's question, because return on capital and cash conversion both sit below peers.

Bonesupport, new at thirteenth, is a Swedish orthobiologics company whose injectable bone graft substitute elutes antibiotics, with a grade-A chart, Stage one and Stage two both rated Probable, and the cheapest valuation reading on the Bench. My reading is that the market's story is United States adoption as a standard of care. Nothing is on file, and a small single-product medtech name is the shape the book already has too much of, so a memo would move it most, in either direction.

Bavarian Nordic, new at eighteenth, is two engines in one company: Travel Health, compounding at twenty-six per cent organic growth with a seventy-eight per cent United States rabies share, and Public Preparedness, which is episodic and has fallen by about two fifths in two years. The chart went to A in the pharma row the target Overweights. Group growth says little until the market picks an engine, and a memo valuing Travel Health alone would settle whether the price already does.

Made Tech, up five to fifteenth, is a debt-free United Kingdom public-sector digital specialist whose bookings and margin both inflected, revenue up twenty-seven per cent and adjusted operating profit up sixty-nine per cent. Only the chart moved, to A on a Basing setup; estimates and valuation are blank, and the memo progresses it at low priority with its first dated test in February.

The reverse happened too. Tenaris fell from third on the Bench straight off the Lists because its memo, published on the fifth of November, graded the change thesis F, and Renishaw left because the base its memo said would fail quickly did fail. Below the Bench, Bell Food Group is new to the Shortlist, qualifying up from Stage one into Stage two with a valuation reading of A and nothing on file, so it is the name a memo would move most there.

The fifth topic: three single cases that share no theme.

PGE, third and up five, is the integrated utilities row the target Overweights and the book does not hold: a regulated-network compounder inside a state-controlled coal balance sheet, where distribution earns a return on the regulated asset base of up to thirteen per cent. Sell-side Momentum is A, the chart is at A, and the Pool no longer marks PGE as leaving. There is no memo, and the question for one is whether the network half can be valued apart from the lignite half, because state control means the market can refuse to pay for the network for as long as it likes.

Greggs, promoted to twelfth, is a free cash flow inflection that is near-mechanical on the cost side and unresolved on the demand side. Capital spending peaked last year and falls by more than a third this year, converting into about one hundred and fifteen million pounds of free cash flow at a six per cent yield from over two thousand seven hundred shops taking share. Estimate cuts eased for the first time. The memo's four bottom-up grades are all C, and demand is the side the capital-spending story cannot supply.

Sinch is held at seventeenth, twelve places below its score, because a buyback is not a change thesis. Sell-side Momentum A, a proven Stage two and a valuation reading of B are all real, and fifteen per cent of the capital has been retired since July last year, but organic gross profit grew two per cent in the second quarter, so earnings per share are driven by share count rather than the business. A memo would decide whether the buyback masks decay or buys time.

The recap.

The top of the Bench is decided by dated tests: Kainos on Monday, Demant within days, then Clariant's print, BP's print and WPP's trading update. Healthcare is the strongest group and the way in is the single case: Demant over Sonova, Orion on a re-based return, Genmab and Sectra on memos not yet written. Energy is a portfolio decision the memos will not make for you. Most of this week's promotions are charts ahead of cases, so the memo queue is the real list.

Two next actions. For you: read the Kainos and Demant memos this weekend, because both tests arrive before the next note and both memos name the price at which a pass becomes a purchase, then listen on Monday for Kainos's contractor headcount and services margin. For me: commission the memos on Sectra and Genmab, re-read Merck's park against the new estimate reading, and have the Kainos and Demant results read against their memos' conditions on the day they land.

An endnote on how the lists were made.

Two things changed this week. The candidate pool was the Pool of Stocks Eligible for Middle Innings Portfolio Selection alone, so seven names on last week's lists that sit outside the Pool, among them ASML, Neste and Galp, left as dropped rather than judged, and no override lines were carried. The Pool holds one hundred and seventy-five stocks from a universe of about one thousand three hundred and sixty, and marks thirty-seven of them as leaving; three of those are on the Bench, SCHOTT Pharma, Kainos and BP, and they leave next week whatever this ranking says unless their readings recover. Twenty-seven names left the Lists in all. Two gaps limit the reading. Nine of the twenty Bench stocks have no memo, so their four bottom-up grades are an average that fills a gap, not a judgement about the company. And the week's causes were not read: the strategist and newsletter files did not reach the pack, so no inflation print, yield, oil price or central-bank decision, including the Bank of England's on Thursday, was read, and the WPP, Clariant and BP results are not in the pack either. The environment judgement is held a week stale, and the Lists rest on it.
