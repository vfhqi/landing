Key Themes of the Middle Innings Lists in the Week of the thirteenth of November 2026.

What this script covers.

Richard, this is Watson. This script is about shape. It covers what the Pool of Stocks Eligible for Middle Innings Portfolio Selection and the three Lists would do to the industries, size, valuation and countries of your portfolio, and what is missing from them. The individual stocks are in the Overview, so here I name a stock only where the stock proves a point about shape.

The headline.

The Lists this week are close to a mirror image of the portfolio you hold. The portfolio is mostly small companies, with industrials and consumer staples in the lead, listed largely in the United Kingdom and Sweden. The Ideas Bench is mostly large companies, with healthcare and technology in the lead, spread across twelve countries. So the Lists are doing their job as a source of difference.

Two cautions sit beside that message. The first caution is that the Lists fill only part of the portfolio's largest gap. Large pharma and integrated utilities are well supplied, while insurance and energy are missing from the Ideas Bench. The second caution is that the lean toward large and defensive companies comes from this week's Portfolio Fitness for Fighting judgement. That same judgement says the cautious mood in the market weakened this week, for the first time.

Theme one: the Ideas Bench is built from what you do not own.

By industry, the Ideas Bench and the portfolio barely overlap. Six of the twenty Ideas Bench stocks are healthcare companies and five are technology companies. Materials has three, and utilities and banks have two each. The portfolio's largest industry is industrials, with four of fifteen holdings, followed by consumer staples with three. The Ideas Bench holds no industrial company and one staples company.

The reasoning matters more than the count. This week's Portfolio Fitness for Fighting judgement wants more large pharma, more integrated utilities, more insurance and more consumer staples than the market holds. Those are the businesses the judgement expects to hold up if the squeeze from interest rates persists. The portfolio owns only the staples. So I gave some places on the Ideas Bench partly for closing that gap. PGE and Orion each sit five places above their scores for that reason.

For the shape of the portfolio, one position from the pharma or utility group would be the first holding in a favoured area outside staples. The supply of names is good and the supply of research is not. Of the five pharma and utility names on the Ideas Bench, three have no memo. So what I would prepare is research on that group, before any ranking of the five against each other means much.

The sentence to carry away: the Ideas Bench is built from what you do not own, so the research queue matters more than the ranking.

Theme two: the Lists are large because the environment judgement says large.

The Lists would move the portfolio a long way up in company size. The typical holding in your portfolio is worth about one point seven billion euros. The typical Ideas Bench stock is worth about eight and a half billion euros, five times as much. Nine of your fifteen holdings are worth under two billion euros. Only one Ideas Bench stock is that small.

The cause is one setting in Portfolio Fitness for Fighting. The target portfolio wants far fewer small, unproven and complex companies than the market holds, and that preference sits at its most negative setting. For a stock with no memo, fragility is measured by size alone. So the unresearched part of the Pool is in effect ranked by a large-company screen. The Pool already leans large before that screen, with a typical stock near five billion euros against a little over three billion for the universe.

The evidence behind that setting is ageing. The setting rests on European credit evidence that is three weeks old. Over the same three weeks, the share of European stocks trading above their two-hundred-day average has risen, which is smaller companies recovering. The setting eases by one notch if that share reaches sixty-five percent with the index near its high. The share today is sixty-three percent.

For the shape of the portfolio, the size gap could narrow without any change to the holdings, simply because the setting lifts. The Lists would then look different too, with smaller cases moving up. So I would keep the small companies with real research ready. Made Tech and Bonesupport are the first two.

The sentence to carry away: the Lists are large this week because the environment judgement says large, and that judgement is two percentage points from easing.

Theme three: the Pool is expensive, and the Ideas Bench only looks cheap.

The Pool is dearer than the universe it comes from. Across the universe, about one stock in five carries the worst grade for valuation. In the Pool, more than one stock in three carries that grade. A Pool chosen for strength looks like that, because strength has been paid for.

The Ideas Bench runs the other way. Fourteen of the twenty Ideas Bench stocks sit in the two cheapest valuation grades, and none sits in the worst grade. The ranking has pulled the Pool's cheaper stocks to the top.

That cheapness needs testing, because the valuation grade measures how cheap the shares look on the screen. The grade does not measure the return a memo expects. Where a memo exists, the memo is usually less generous than the screen. Ten Ideas Bench stocks have a memo, and only two of those memos lean bullish on the shares. Three of the best-rated cases have memos that grade the expected return a fail at the price when the memo was written.

There is a second surprise in the table. Your portfolio already looks cheaper on the screen than the Ideas Bench does. Six of your fifteen holdings carry the cheapest valuation grade, against six of twenty on the Ideas Bench. So the Lists offer you difference and size. The Lists do not offer you a cheaper portfolio.

What I would prepare is one sheet that sets each memo's entry level beside today's price. That sheet would show, for every researched name, how far the shares are from a decent return.

The sentence to carry away: the Ideas Bench looks cheap on the screen and full in the memos, and the memos are the better witness.

Theme four: the target's favourite sectors do not reach the Ideas Bench.

Portfolio Fitness for Fighting set the direction of the Lists, and price and evidence chose the stocks. The mix table shows this clearly. Sixteen of the eighty places on the three Lists belong to sectors the target portfolio most wants to hold. None of those sixteen is on the Ideas Bench. Four of the twenty Ideas Bench stocks sit in sectors the target least wants.

The clearest example is downstream energy, meaning refiners and tankers. Downstream energy is the sector the target portfolio favours most strongly. Every refiner and tanker on the Lists carries the worst grade for valuation, on earnings the research reads as a peak. So those names stay on the Ideas Longlist.

Energy as a whole also stepped down this week. The target portfolio moved the energy industry from more than the market holds to the same as the market holds. Oil had fallen below one hundred dollars, and energy shares had lost strength against the market. Five energy names now rank in the top forty by score, down from eight a week ago.

The other side of the theme is software. The target portfolio does not favour enterprise software, yet five Ideas Bench stocks sit in software and information technology services. Those five earned their places on their own evidence, against the environment view. That independence is healthy in a research list.

For the shape of the portfolio, a software holding would add to an imbalance that already exists. Almost a third of the portfolio's invested weight sits in sectors the target wants less of. I have proposed a rule that would make any further weight in such sectors an explicit decision, taken holding by holding, with a written reason that the case outruns its sector. A software candidate would need that written reason from the start.

The sentence to carry away: the environment view chose the direction of the Lists, and price and evidence chose the stocks.

Theme five: what is absent.

The absences fall into three kinds, and each kind means something different.

The first kind is absent although wanted. Insurance is part of the portfolio's largest gap, and no insurer is on the Ideas Bench. Only three insurers appear anywhere on the Lists. One of the three has a memo, and that insurer, Hiscox, is due to leave the Pool. Energy is also absent from the Ideas Bench, with thirteen energy names lower down. So the Lists fill the gap lopsidedly, with pharma and utilities and little else.

The second kind is absent from your home ground. Industrials is the portfolio's largest industry. Industrial companies are about one in eight of the universe, about one in seventeen of the Pool, and three of the eighty list places. All three sit in industries whose shares are weak against the market. So the Lists offer no ready successor for an industrial holding. Bravida, on the Ideas Shortlist, is the one I would resolve. You marked Bravida at your highest level of interest, and the research is split between a data-centre growth story and acquisitions that mask organic decay.

The third kind is absent by the environment's choice. Consumer discretionary has twenty-one stocks in the Pool and one place on the Lists. The target portfolio holds consumer discretionary at its most negative setting. Weak consumer confidence in Germany and the euro area now supports that setting. The market disagrees in places, because online retail, hotels and apparel shares are all strong. So twenty candidates sit unexamined on the strength of one environment call.

Five whole industries also have no stock in the Pool at all. The five are real assets such as property, telecoms, defence, infrastructure and media. Together those industries are about one in eight of the universe. The market and the target portfolio agree on all five. You hold one infrastructure company and one defence company, so the Lists offer no successors there either.

The sentence to carry away: the Lists fill the pharma and utility part of the gap and leave insurance empty.

Theme six: countries.

The Ideas Bench would spread the portfolio across Europe. Nine of your fifteen holdings are listed in the United Kingdom or Sweden. Seven of the twenty Ideas Bench stocks are listed in those two countries, and the other thirteen are spread across ten more. Germany and Switzerland supply two Ideas Bench stocks each, and the portfolio has no holding listed in either country. Switzerland is one of only three countries the target portfolio wants more of.

France shows the environment view at work at the top only. France is the country the target portfolio wants least. No French stock is on the Ideas Bench, yet eight French stocks appear lower down, in line with France's share of the Pool.

Poland is the hidden concentration. Polish stocks are about three percent of the universe and ten percent of the list places. Five of the eight Polish names are banks, in a country where interest rates, courts and taxes set the next three years of earnings. So Poland on the Lists is one risk repeated eight times. One caution on all of these counts: the table records where a share is listed, and not where the company earns its revenue.

The sentence to carry away: the Ideas Bench spreads the portfolio across Europe, and Poland is the one country to count before adding.

The counter-case.

The strongest case against my reading is about timing. The Lists point toward large, defensive companies after three months in which the market has already moved there. That move began to fade this week. Defensive shares stopped leading over one week and over twelve months, and lead now only over three months, by about six points. The direction of interest rates is no longer established, and the headlines say the Federal Reserve is easing. The record of the target portfolio's sector calls is also unproven. Eight of the nine weekly target tables have lost to the market so far, over a period too short to judge. On that view, the Lists favour yesterday's winners, and the small companies you already own are better placed than the Lists imply.

My reply has three parts. First, the Lists are research priorities, and a memo takes weeks. The memos also protect you from crowded prices, because nearly every researched defensive name is parked until the shares fall to a stated level. Second, the candidates are not pure defensives. Each pharma and utility name has a change story of its own, which a memo judges on the company's merits. Third, the turning point is written down in advance. If the share of stocks above their two-hundred-day average reaches sixty-five percent, or the three-month lead of defensive shares falls under two points, the smaller cases gain and I re-rank. So if I am wrong, you have research on good companies you do not own. If the counter-case is wrong and nothing is prepared, the portfolio has no candidates in the areas expected to hold up.

The recap.

Three things to keep. The Ideas Bench is built from what you do not own, in industry, size and country. The Ideas Bench looks cheap on the screen and full in the memos. And the gap is filled in pharma and utilities and left empty in insurance.

Your next action, Richard: listen first for the share of European stocks above their two-hundred-day average, which stands at sixty-three percent. That number tells you how long the large and defensive shape of these Lists deserves your trust.

My next action: I will prepare a map of the gap, covering insurance, large pharma and integrated utilities. For each area the map will name the best candidate on the Lists, the research on file and any entry level a memo sets, starting with insurance.

That is the end of the summary.
