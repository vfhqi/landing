Overview (including process/structure) of the Middle Innings Lists in the Week of the thirtieth of September 2026.

This is the overview of this week's three lists: how they were made, what they say, and what changed since last Friday. It runs about fifteen minutes. It covers the process, the headline, the Ideas Bench name by name, the four flags, the Ideas Shortlist and the Ideas Longlist, the changes, the pull of Portfolio Fitness for Fighting, the caveats and two next actions.

Part one, the process and structure. The lists are sound but provisional: this is a test run, on the older scorer, in the first week the lists are drawn only from the Pool of Stocks Eligible for Middle Innings Portfolio Selection. Here is how they were built.

The Pool holds one hundred and fourteen stocks out of a universe of one thousand three hundred and sixty-one. Four of them are live holdings: Arteche, Hexatronic, Sdiptech and Volex. All sixteen live holdings are excluded from the lists, which leaves one hundred and ten candidates. Every candidate has already passed the Pool's entry test: twelve-month relative strength in the top fifth of the market, in a sector at least in the top half. Seventy-five of the one hundred and ten have no published memo. There were no spoken overrides from you this week.

The scoring. Each candidate gets a blended score with two halves: a bottom-up half built from the ratings, and a fitness half built from the Portfolio Fitness for Fighting target. The tilt was nought point six two five, so the fitness reading carries five eighths of the blend. The scorer is the older version. It has a fitness column and no separate top-down column, and it was scored against last Friday's target, set on the twenty-fifth of September. The six Portfolio Fitness for Fighting components are also last Friday's, and they stand as this week's top-down judgement. The columns for the Eight Master Ratings were absent from this scorer, so the call counted the six headline ratings by hand: technical momentum, paradigm fit, fundamental change, foundations, sell-side estimate momentum and valuation. Which language model ran the judgement session is not in our files.

The judgement call. The score sets a queue, and the call then places each stock against it. It moved forty-nine stocks by more than five places. Its checks: only a confirmed Stage Two uptrend can sit on the Ideas Bench; a stock marked Thin, a fitness reading of seventy or better with no memo, is read as a sector tilt; valuation ranks but never excludes; and every placement carries its strongest counter. The biggest single move was Lundin Mining: second on the score, placed fiftieth, at Ideas Longlist ten.

Part two, the headline. The Ideas Bench is two benches in one. Ten names are memo-backed industrial, technology and healthcare businesses, led by ASML, Cenergy, Diploma and Renishaw, and every one of them is at a full price. The other ten are commissions with no memo, seven of them energy names, placed on the energy Overweight and a few readable letters. The research-backed half is where conviction can be built now. The energy half is a research queue for the yield-and-inflation shape the top-down read asks for, and should be treated as one until the memos exist.

Part seven, how Portfolio Fitness for Fighting pushed the lists, and the strongest counter-case. At five eighths of the blend, the fitness reading largely wrote the score, and it pushed in three directions. It put the miners at the top: KGHM first, Lundin Mining second and Glencore third, all scored with an energy label the top-down call itself says is a notch too generous. The call pulled every one of them down. It pushed seven energy commissions onto the Bench on the energy Overweight. And it penalised size, which put Cenergy fourteenth, Instalco forty-fourth and AT and S fifty-fifth; the call overrode that for memo-backed grades. The strongest counter-case is that the energy half of the Bench is the top-down read restated, not seven pieces of stock evidence. That read is a week old, oil's skew is two-sided, and a phased Hormuz deal takes twenty-five dollars off oil, which would remove seven Bench places' reason at once. My reply is that these places are commissions, not cases. The Bench is the right place to commission them, because the shape closes Gaps One and Two. But none of the seven should be treated as Bench-grade until a memo fills its three blank ratings.
