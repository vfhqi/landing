Part 1 — The corrections, and the verified definitions

BLUF: three concepts were wrong in the prior work. Here is each one stated correctly, verified against the Master Dashboard source code rather than the handoff notes, because the handoff notes carried the same errors.

1.1 Valuation grade — a universe ranking, not an own-history level

  • The error: the prior memo said "an A rating covers the bottom 2nd percentile of the stock's own history; expensive stocks in the top 22% of own history rate D or F". That states a snapshot reading as if it were the rule
  • The correct definition (high conviction, read from the dashboard build script): there are two different measuring sticks, and the prior work fused them. The percentile is where today's forward price-to-earnings sits inside the stock's own ten-year history (0 means cheapest in a decade, 100 means dearest). The A-to-F grade is a separate cross-sectional ranking of the whole universe by that percentile: A is the cheapest tenth of all stocks, B the next 15%, C the next 25%, D the next 25%, F the most expensive quarter
  • Why it matters: "A" does not mean a stock is near its own historic floor. It means it is among the 10% of the universe trading cheapest relative to their own histories. The "2nd percentile" and "12th percentile" numbers the prior memo quoted were simply the own-history readings that happened to fall at this week's decile boundaries, which I reproduced exactly (the A/B line sat at the 2nd own-history percentile, B/C at the 12th, C/D at the 44th, D/F at the 78th). They describe one day's distribution, they are not the grade
  • The concrete consequence I found: when I re-graded every stock by the correct universe bell curve, the prior per-stock valuation grades were one notch too generous on seven of the twenty holdings. Greatland was labelled B at the 39th percentile when the B band ends at the 12th, so it is a C. Técnicas Reunidas was a C, correctly a D. REN was a D, correctly an F. Meliá, Irish Continental, Xvivo and Croda were each over-graded by one notch too. This is not a rounding quibble: it means the prior book looked cheaper than it was

1.2 Sell-side estimate momentum — real, but secondary, and read line by line

  • What it is (plain English): this score, labelled SSEM on the dashboard, measures which way the analysts are moving their forecasts. It counts, across five things (earnings, EBITDA, sales, target price, the share of analysts saying Buy) over the last one, three and six months, plus one for every estimate raised and minus one for every estimate cut. The range runs from minus-fifteen to plus-fifteen. The A-to-F grade is again a universe bell curve on that count
  • Correction A, weighting: it is now a real but secondary input. It no longer overrides the sector view on its own. In the prior book it did exactly that: Hill & Smith and Meliá were held because their estimate momentum was top of the universe, in spite of a Normal-weighted and an Underweighted sector respectively. Under the correction that override is removed
  • Correction B, the line-item read for proven names (this is the sharpest change): for a stock whose price trend is already established, a cut to any one of earnings, EBITDA or sales over the recent windows is a major negative, even when the headline score is still positive. The headline can be propped up by target-price or analyst-rating moves while the actual profit lines are being cut underneath. So for every proven name I read the three profit-line revision series directly, not just the composite grade. This single check removed three holdings from the book (see Part 3)
  • Correction C, the light touch for young trends: for early Stage 2 names and Stage 1 turnarounds, estimate momentum is only a light tilt: a negative reading is mildly unhelpful, an upgrade is mildly helpful, neither is decisive, because the price setup is doing the work and the trend is too young to lean on the fundamentals hard

1.3 Proven Stage 2 record length — prefer the shorter record

  • What "Stage 2" and "proven" mean (plain English): Stage 2 is the technical term for a confirmed price uptrend (the advancing phase after a base). "Proven" means the uptrend has registered as qualifying in at least four of the last twelve month-end readings
  • The correction: a full twelve-month qualifying record is accepted but not preferred. A stock that has qualified every month for a year has, in most cases, already delivered much of its move, leaving less ahead. The preferred profile is a proven trend with a shorter record, around five to seven months: the uptrend is genuinely confirmed, but less of it has elapsed, so more upside remains. A twelve-month record is taken only when the rest of the case is strong
  • What this exposed in the prior book (high conviction): all seven of the prior "proven" holdings had ten-to-twelve-month records. The proven sleeve was built entirely from extended trends, the opposite of the stated preference. The corrected sleeve leads with the genuinely fresh names instead

Part 8 — Caveats and things to weigh

BLUF: the re-run is sound but bounded. Four honest caveats.

  • Data vintage (medium conviction): the estimate-momentum and valuation exports are a few weeks old (the same vintage the prior run used). The relative picture is reliable for comparison, but an early-July refresh should re-confirm the fresh names before sizing up
  • The valuation grade is relative, not absolute (high conviction): a corrected "C" means middle-of-the-universe, not cheap in absolute terms. Several proven names (Tenaris D, Roche D, Hexatronic D) are fairly valued, not cheap. The book is not a value book, it is a trend book with valuation as a guardrail
  • Estimate momentum is a lagging, sell-side input (medium conviction): it tells you where analysts have been moving, which can itself lag the fundamentals. Treating it as secondary, as the correction now does, is the right response, but it is not a leading signal
  • Record-length preference is a heuristic, not a law (medium conviction): "fresh beats extended" holds on average because extended trends have less room left, but specific extended names with strong, clean, cheap profiles (Hellenic Telecom, Fineco, Greatland here) are rightly kept.
