Part 0 — The vocabulary, so nothing downstream is ambiguous

BLUF for this part: roughly a dozen terms carry the whole argument. Each is defined technically and then in plain English. Skim the bold labels; read the plain-English line for any term you want nailed down.

  • Stage 2 (a confirmed price uptrend). Technical: the advancing phase in Stan Weinstein's four-stage model, a stock trading above a rising long-term moving average after breaking out of a base. In plain English: the share price is in a confirmed, established climb, not bottoming and not topping
  • Stage 1 (a base that is turning up). Technical: the basing phase that precedes Stage 2, where a downtrend has stopped and the price is going sideways before a possible breakout. In plain English: the price has stopped falling and is coiling, with a turn up not yet confirmed
  • Persistence / qualifying month. Technical: the dashboard records, at each month-end for the trailing twelve months, whether the Stage 2 signal was strong enough to "qualify" (rated Probable or Plausible). In plain English: a monthly tick-box for "was the uptrend real at that month-end", giving a twelve-box track record
  • Record length. Technical: months elapsed since the first qualifying month in the trailing-twelve window. In plain English: how long the confirmed uptrend has already been running. Short means fresh, long means it has been going a while
  • Proven Stage 2 (Bucket A). Technical: four or more qualifying months in the trailing twelve. In plain English: the uptrend has shown up enough times to be taken as real, not a one-month fluke
  • Early Stage 2 (Bucket B). Technical: a young or partial Stage 2 signal, one to three qualifying months, or a current Stage 2 reading that has not yet built a record. In plain English: the uptrend is real but new, so higher potential and higher uncertainty
  • Stage 1 Transition (Bucket C). Technical: a Stage 1 base rated Probable or Plausible, paired with a cheap valuation. In plain English: a cheap stock whose long decline has stopped and which looks ready to turn
  • Sell-side estimate momentum (the dashboard labels it SSEM). Technical: a score from minus-15 to plus-15 counting, across five measures (earnings per share, EBITDA, sales, target price, the share of analysts rating the stock Buy) over the last one, three and six months, plus one for every forecast raised and minus one for every forecast cut. In plain English: a single number for which way the analysts are moving their forecasts, up or down, and how broadly
  • Earnings per share, EBITDA, sales (the three profit lines). Technical: earnings per share is net profit divided by share count; EBITDA is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, a proxy for cash operating profit; sales is revenue. In plain English: the three core measures of how much the business actually earns and sells, as opposed to what analysts think the share is worth
  • Target price and percent-Buy. Technical: the average analyst price target, and the share of analysts with a Buy rating. In plain English: two sentiment measures that often follow the share price rather than lead the fundamentals
  • Valuation percentile (own-history). Technical: where today's forward price-to-earnings multiple sits within the stock's own ten-year monthly history, from 0 (cheapest in a decade) to 100 (most expensive in a decade). In plain English: how cheap or dear the stock is versus its own past
  • Valuation grade A to F (universe ranking). Technical: a bell-curve rank of all stocks by that own-history percentile, A = cheapest 10 percent of the universe, B = next 15, C = next 25, D = next 25, F = most expensive 25. In plain English: how cheap the stock is versus every other stock, graded on a curve. A means among the cheapest tenth of the market, not necessarily at its own floor
  • Sector weight (your sector view). Technical: your manual Overweight-to-Underweight stance per sector, A = Strong Overweight, B = Overweight, C = Normal, D = Underweight, F = Strong Underweight (excluded). In plain English: how much you want to own a given slice of the market, set by you, not the machine
  • Timeliness. Technical: the dashboard's best-setup grade A to F, summarising whether a high-quality entry setup is present now. In plain English: is this a good moment to be looking, technically
  • Bucket / Very-Short-List (VSL). Technical: the three quality tiers (Proven, Early, Stage 1) and the reserve bench of best non-portfolio names per industry. In plain English: the portfolio's three drawers, plus the substitutes waiting in the wings
  • Falling knife. Technical: a cheap stock whose forecasts are still being cut. In plain English: cheap for a reason, and likely to get cheaper, a value trap rather than a value turn

Part 1 — The three corrections, verified from source

BLUF for this part: three concepts were wrong in the prior work, and each error pushed the book toward extended, expensive, signal-overridden names. I re-derived all three from the Master Dashboard build script, not the handoff notes, because the handoff notes carried the same errors.

1.1 Valuation grade is a ranking against the universe, not a level against own history

  • The judgement first: the prior note's description of the valuation grade was wrong, and correcting it makes the prior book look cheaper than it actually was
  • What was said (the error): that an A valuation "covers the bottom 2nd percentile of the stock's own history". That treats a single day's reading as if it were the rule
  • What is true (verified in the dashboard build script, lines 6330 to 6358): there are two different measuring sticks. The percentile is own-history, where today's multiple sits in the stock's own ten-year range. The grade is a separate ranking of the whole universe by that percentile, A = cheapest tenth of all stocks, B = next 15, C = next 25, D = next 25, F = dearest quarter
  • In plain English: an A does not mean "near its own floor". It means "among the 10 percent of stocks in the market trading cheapest versus their own history". The "2nd percentile" and "12th percentile" numbers the prior note quoted were simply the own-history readings that happened to fall at this week's grade boundaries, and those boundaries move every week
  • The concrete consequence, graded: re-grading every name on the correct universe curve, the prior per-stock grades were one notch too generous on 7 of the 20 holdings. Greatland was called B at the 39th own-history percentile when the B band ends near the 12th, so it is a C. Technicas Reunidas was a C, correctly a D. REN was a D, correctly an F. Meliá, Irish Continental, Xvivo and Croda were each over-graded by one notch too. This is not a rounding quibble: the prior book looked cheaper than it was
  • Conviction: high. I reproduced the dashboard's exact ranking and the boundary readings match the prior note's own quoted numbers, which proves they were boundary readings, not the definition

1.2 Sell-side estimate momentum is a supporting check, read line by line, not a decider

  • The judgement first: forecast-revision momentum is real but secondary, and for a stock whose price has already run it must be read at the level of the individual profit lines, not the blended headline
  • What it is (plain English reminder): a score for which way analysts are moving their forecasts, raised versus cut, across earnings, EBITDA, sales, target price and the Buy share, over one, three and six months
  • Correction A, weighting. It no longer overrides your sector view on its own. (The prior book held Hill & Smith and Meliá purely because their forecast momentum topped the universe, in spite of a Normal-weighted and an Underweighted sector. That override is removed.)
  • Correction B, the line-by-line read for proven names (the sharpest change). For any name whose price trend is already established, a cut to any one of earnings, EBITDA or sales over the recent windows is a major negative, even when the headline score is still positive. The headline can be propped up by target-price and rating moves while the actual profit lines are cut underneath. How specifically: on this run the check removed three holdings whose composite read fine, Ariston (earnings and EBITDA both cut at one and three months), Alcon (all three profit lines cut at three months), Carmila (EBITDA and sales cut)
  • Correction C, the light touch for young trends. For Early Stage 2 and Stage 1 names, forecast momentum is a light tilt only, a negative reading mildly unhelpful, an upgrade mildly helpful, neither decisive, because the technical setup is carrying the case
  • Conviction: high on the principle, verified against the score's source code (it counts five measures over three windows, range minus-15 to plus-15)

1.3 A shorter proven record is preferred to a year-long one

  • The judgement first: a full twelve-month qualifying record is accepted but not preferred, because a year-long uptrend has usually already delivered most of its move
  • Why (the mechanism, in prose): the preferred profile is a confirmed uptrend with a short record, around five to seven months. It has cleared the same evidential bar (it is genuinely proven) but has spent less of its expected life, so more of the move remains ahead. A year-long record mostly reflects gains already banked. Take a twelve-month name only when the rest of the case is strong, meaning clean profit-line forecasts, a sensible valuation, and a favoured sector
  • What this exposed, graded: all seven of the prior "proven" holdings carried ten-to-twelve-month records. The proven sleeve was built entirely from extended trends, the opposite of the stated preference. The corrected sleeve leads with the genuinely fresh names instead
  • Conviction: medium-high. "Fresh beats extended" holds on average and is consistent with the separate finding that very extended relative moves tend to mean-revert, but it is a preference that orders the choice, not a mechanical law
