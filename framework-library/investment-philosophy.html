The Core Belief

"Doing investing" is not the top priority. Executing the system in an appropriately innovative and dynamic manner to prioritise profiting in the environment — delivering on the investing ETC — that is the priority. The system serves the strategy. The process serves the system. Everything is means to an end.

The end: "Have a portfolio and squad that makes outlier returns (5-10% outperformance per annum) on a rolling next-two-year basis by continually, fluidly and successfully adapting to any unfolding circumstances."

The method: "Consistently execute my process. Mark Douglas style." Conservative analysis, aggressive execution. Simple + completed beats complicated + incomplete.

What Makes a Great Investment

Richard works "right to left" — start with the financial output (predictable 18M-3Y EPS), then work backwards to understand what investment case delivers it, then what inputs and transmission mechanism deliver the investment case.

The singular obsession is predictable 18M-3Y EPS. The target: 25%+ IRR with high predictability. Every dimension must rate A/B or top quartile — anything less is "fail." This is not a business where second-best gets rewarded.

The best ideas are confluences of at least two, ideally three, of four simple patterns: an animal CEO (the Slootman archetype — relentless, commercial, ambitious), a massive corporate change plan, a huge demand explosion at sector level, and a very large share price decline onto bottom quartile multiples.

Richard explicitly inverts: he does not solve for "yes." He disproves why it's a "no" or a "false friend." If it survives disproval, it might be an investment. This is the ACH (Analysis of Competing Hypotheses) approach applied to stock selection — profiles of "no," "false friend," and "yes" along one axis, dimensions of evidence along the other.

Setup Profiles — What to Own and What to Avoid

Three acceptable profiles: Predictable CfC (bankable earnings + change catalyst + reasonable multiple), EPT (earnings power transformation from demand surge), and EPSU (earnings power step-up from internal change/push).

Six sub-setups detail how these manifest in practice — from big corporate change plays to oversold rebounding quality compounders to demand pulse transformations.

Equally important is what to avoid. "Unacceptable" setups include silver medallists, weak management, large call options with no edge, event-driven names where you'd exit on a negative node, and generic business pitches that are "sorta quality maybe." Also: any company with fewer than 100 employees or less than $5m daily liquidity.

"False friends" are subtler and more dangerous. These are setups that look good but have structural flaws that will eventually surface. The defining characteristic: zero clarity of the transmission mechanism from company actions to EPS. If Richard cannot clearly explain how strategy translates into financial outputs — and predict what tracking data will look like quarter by quarter — it's a false friend. Examples from experience: HBX (no clarity of tracking inputs to EPS), Adevinta (no "soul"), S4 (egotism in communication), Nexi (deep value but defensive strategy only, no moat-widening).

The phrase "Pret sandwich risk" captures the pattern where nothing is individually inoffensive but the collection is fatal.

The Predictability Obsession

Richard's 8-step EPS predictability checklist is one of his most important operational tools. It reflects hard-won lessons from positions where unpredictability caused pain.

The checklist tests: Can I clearly bridge strategy to financial outputs? Is guidance conservative vs. modal case? Do I understand the transmission mechanism? Will IR give me useful intra-earnings operating momentum? Can I track quantitative data on key drivers? Can I Bayesian update my forecasts as data comes in?

"It is easy to accept 'D' or 'C' for new stocks... Until they SNAFU me. After that, it's very clear. These risks are structural and rarely change. The only viable strategy is avoidance. Discretion is the better part of valour."

This connects to a deeper insight about the nature of information in investing. More information does not equal better judgements — often the opposite. What matters is having the right information channels (trackable, quantitative, cross-referenceable with qualitative) and the right mental framework to process them rapidly.

On Deterioration and Exits

Richard's thinking on exits is shaped by his understanding of complex systems. Complex systems appear stable even while losing resilience. Underlying stability is hard to assess. Collapse often appears sudden and surprising. This is directly applicable to investment positions.

Three cognitive biases make deterioration especially dangerous: endowment bias (no objectivity due to ownership and identity attachment), normalcy bias (extrapolating current trends), and the cockroach theory (bad news is always first suppressed by the actors in the system — including management who lie to themselves before they lie to you).

The practical implication: "By the time negatives manifest, there are many problems under the surface. By the time I mentally/emotionally recognise this in this specific situation, I am likely very late."

The solution is analytical, not emotional. Two-mode approach: pre-ownership, assess and seek quality/predictability. Once live, total dispassionate assessment with a paranoid tilt. Frame exits as "reallocating capital," not "accepting a loss" — the latter carries too much negative inertia.

Three system degradation indicators to watch for: slower recovery rates (speed to recover from interjections), rising autocorrelation (bad quarter follows bad quarter), rising variance (larger fluctuations in financial outputs vs. past).

The disprove-or-exit framework is the operational expression of all this: any emergence of deterioration triggers a 30-day shot clock, default is reduce to 0%, and the position can be re-argued from zero immediately if the risk is genuinely disproved.

On Operator Quality

Richard's "great operator" checklist reveals what he actually looks for in management — and it's action-based, not conjecture-based.

Three dimensions: Right direction (big ambition, great strategy with growth as priority, dynamic strategy for a range of environments). Right resources (great CEO measured via action, hiring as top priority, well-resourced/well-invested business, holistic congruent culture, conservative guidance, clear communication). Right record/action/behaviour (high RONIC allocation, acts not just talks, outlier standards, shareholders first, dynamic buybacks, prudent governance).

The phrase "I love Slootman operators with big ambition with holistic/congruent cultures. I detest Meh-ness" captures the essence. Richard wants to see commercial intensity married with sound strategy — not just defence and returns, but attacking and widening the moat.

The lower the quality of the business, sector, or value chain, the more essential the great operator becomes. A "C" rated operator needs "B" or better on everything else. The best setups have great operators on top of great businesses in supportive value chains.

On Themes and S-Curves

Richard's thinking on thematic investing has been shaped by studying ARK Invest and Cathy Wood's approach. Two mental models integrate well into his framework: "stacking of S-curves" (sustained innovation is not a one-off but a series of overlapping S-curves that reshape production capabilities and capital allocation) and "co-evolution of disruption technologies" (adjacent technologies simultaneously enable each other — GPUs for AI, AI for multiomic sequencing, etc.).

For a European-focused investor, this translates into finding listed companies exposed to these themes that may command a scarcity premium. The key is finding them early, before proof points. "Deliverability doesn't matter for the first 12 months — the first part of the S-curve is about changing expectations without need for proof."

But demand pulses are dangerous: "Most demand pulses totally reverse to trend. Assume 100% cyclical/temporary, even if for multiple years." The lesson from Avanza (lazy extrapolation of cyclical high trading activity) and Hemnet (lazy extrapolation of earnings growth rate) is to stay disciplined on the cyclical risk, even in structural themes.

On Process and Internal Change Forces

Richard's "Internal Changes/Push" checklist is one of his longest and most worked SOPs. It maps every dimension of potential positive internal change: leadership changes, ambition/targets increases, strategic priority shifts, big growth building blocks (new products, markets, pricing), financial improvements (growth, margins, leverage, cash conversion), organisational restructuring, M&A/divestitures, changes in investment intensity, stock market messaging improvements, capital returns increases.

A key insight: "Any change on any of these fronts — the more the better — is a high, high priority for workflow prioritisation."

The most attractive scenario is a company coming out of a large investment phase: multi-year margin/EPS depression from doing the right thing, then revenue growth accelerates, margins expand as one-off costs fall away and operating leverage materialises, and the market trusts it because it's congruent with the past story.

On the Investment Lifecycle

The modal case expectation framework establishes clear time horizons: 3Y+ case is a requirement, normal expected ownership is 18 months, enter within 0-6 months of trough/turn, alpha decay curve apex normally around 18 months.

First 6 months: Accept slightly different profile of uncertainty. Grace period. But must prove a winner by 6 months (4 months normally) otherwise the investment case is incorrect. Two other exit triggers (value chain deterioration + insufficient operator quality) are active from day 1.

This lifecycle framework directly shapes position management and creates the accountability mechanism Richard needs to avoid holding deteriorating positions too long.

On Behavioural Change and Self-Awareness

Richard's coaching work with Steve Ward reveals deep self-awareness about what holds him back and what he needs to do differently.

The core tension: Richard loves to tinker, create, and improve — but this often comes at the expense of consistently executing what he already knows works. The solution is a creativity loop: one day per month for strategic reflection and course-correction, with the remaining days devoted to disciplined execution of checklists.

Three drivers of behaviour change (from Steve Ward, referencing "Change or Die"): relationships/social support (not trying to solve it alone), mindset/reframing (see myself as a checklist executor who is creative monthly), repetition until habitual.

Key reframing: "I am on the path to becoming a great investor" — not "there" or "not there," but "going towards." This gives emotional wiggle room that the gold medallist platform (which tells you you're a mug if you're not already at the top) does not.

Limiting beliefs to manage: catastrophising about permanence of negative developments (bad returns = no job = erroneous and unhelpful), the gold medallist mindset (drop it — ref. Jonny Wilkinson who was sick, many others who felt unworthy).

The "unbung" concept (from Kahneman): what is blocking me from doing what I know I need to do? Usually over-complication (solving for "perfect" not "alpha test"), creativity/growth drive pulling away from execution, and demand exceeding capacity.

10 Principles (Distilled from Journals)

  1. Discretion is the better part of valour — Act ex ante on probabilistic deterioration in 12-18M forward EPS. Solution for conviction erosion: erosion in a driver that would reduce 18M EPS by 5%+ or trading multiple by 20%+.

  2. Track and flag — Define all drivers, track inputs fortnightly, rate any deterioration RED, review monthly, move to disprove-or-invalidate list.

  3. Exit early and re-attack once concern cleared — Visualise a quick cut and re-attack lower. Use checklists to form judgement, not hand-wringing. Enjoy high throughput of stocks and judgements.

  4. Form a judgement, then invert — Run lessons bible + ACH. See if ACHs are plausible. Embrace inversion.

  5. Worser, odder, longer, further — Things can get worse than expected, in stranger ways, for longer, and go further than imagined.

  6. Never try to solve deteriorations analytically — Too complex, fast negative OODA loop. Wait until troughing pattern. Investigate via "positive patterns, negative patterns, ACH."

  7. Meet tonnes of management — First-hand evidence of operator quality, strategy clarity, commercial intensity.

  8. Rate tonnes of stocks — Build the database. Breadth creates context for depth.

  9. Focus purely on making money — Avoiding detractors, generating P&L. Being analytically right ex ante is irrelevant and arbitrary (Mark Douglas probabilities).

  10. Do the mundane consistently — Foundation of high performance. Simple + completed. What would Flow State Richard do?

Key Lessons from Specific Stocks

  • Attendo: Leading share/business with mediocre economics is never as good/bad as peaks/troughs imply. Takes 2-3 years for CfC and SP to turn. Can be great rebound setups because of multi-year upgrade path.
  • Avanza: Beware lazy extrapolation of cyclical high activity. Also: increased investment risk as catchup 18-24M later.
  • Sdiptech: Pret sandwich risk. Also: unclear on growth transmission mechanism to EPS.
  • S4: Obvious, silly egotism in communication is a red flag. Cut.
  • Ryanair: Lessons documented (public stock note context).
  • Nexi: Deep value (5x) but no conviction in MT + defensive strategy. BCG Cash Cow strategy = no.
  • Fevertree: Clear on USA + UK drivers, unclear on Europe/Australia. Difficulty of vertical integration step-function.
  • Covid stocks: Most demand pulses totally reverse to trend. Assume 100% cyclical/temporary.
