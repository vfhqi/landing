Key Themes of the Middle Innings Lists in the Week of the second of October 2026.

This is the themes summary of this week's three ideas lists, read from the point of view of portfolio construction and Portfolio Fitness for Fighting, and it runs about fifteen minutes. It works from the mix tables for the universe, the Pool of Stocks Eligible for Middle Innings Portfolio Selection, the eighty list places, the Ideas Bench and your live holdings, and it gives as much weight to what is absent as to what is there. I have tested every theme against those tables and dropped the ones the data would not carry.

The headline. The gate that builds these lists has quietly changed the kind of company you are being offered. It now hands you larger, more expensive, more environment-driven businesses than the ones you own, concentrated in two sectors and in two countries whose overweights rest on the thinnest evidence in the table, and it cannot offer you three of the rows the Target Setup most wants. That is a construction question, not a stock question, and most of it is yours to settle.

Theme one, size, and my judgement is that this is the one place where the lists answer the Target Setup's strongest row. The median market value of the eighty list places is eleven point five billion euros. The median of your sixteen live holdings is one point five billion. Six of the eighty list places are under two billion euros; ten of your sixteen holdings are. The Ideas Bench median is eleven point eight billion, with one name under two billion and three above fifty billion. This is not an accident of judgement: the universe median is three point three billion and the Pool median four point eight, so the Pool gate lifts size and the ranking lifted it further. Fragility stays Double Underweight, the strongest row in the table, and Gap one says the book's small, niche, capital-spending-funded names are exactly the channel the risk-off is running through, with the holdings file's size and fragility columns blank for all sixteen so nothing can measure it. The counter inside this theme is real: for the two thirds of the Pool without case ratings, fragility is size alone, and the Nordic small and mid caps are the one place European demand is turning up. So, theme one: the lists are large and mid cap where your book is small cap, and that is the table's strongest row being answered by the gate rather than by you.

Theme two, valuation, and my judgement is that closing the gaps costs you valuation, with the Ideas Bench as the deliberate exception. On the store's own valuation reading, thirty-one per cent of the universe sits at A. In the Pool it is four per cent, and forty per cent sits at F. Across the eighty list places it is two per cent at A and thirty-eight per cent at F, with a quarter at D. Your live holdings are the mirror image: forty-four per cent at A and nothing worse than two names at F. The Ideas Bench is where I pulled back, at forty per cent B, fifteen per cent C and only ten per cent F. The same pattern runs through the known cases: seven of the nine stocks to shout loudly about carry TSR/Valuation F on their own memo's expected return, and your ruling of the thirtieth of September is that a low expected return makes a case less meritorious and never excludes it. So, theme two: the gate selects against valuation, the Ideas Bench is the only layer where that is corrected, and every place you fill from these lists is a place bought at a worse reading than the book's average.

Theme three, absence by industry, and my judgement is that the lists cannot close three of the rows the Target Setup most wants. Six of the sixteen industries have no place in the Pool at all: Utilities, Telecoms, Real assets, Defence, Media and Infrastructure, which together are nineteen per cent of the universe. Consumer discretionary falls from twelve per cent of the universe to two per cent of the Pool and zero list places. Transportation holds seven Pool places and no list place. Consumer staples falls from six per cent to two per cent and gets two list places. The sharpest absence is insurance. All twenty-nine Financials places across the three lists are banks; there is not one insurer. The Target Setup holds Insurance Overweight, Staples Overweight and Pharma Overweight, and Gap two says the book holds no insurer, no pharma name and no energy producer. The lists can close the energy leg and the bank leg of that gap and nothing else. Healthcare tells the same story more quietly, falling from eight per cent of the universe to five per cent of the Pool and two list places, so the one diversifier away from banks and energy below the Ideas Bench is a single medtech distributor and a single care-home operator. This is a rule for you to decide, and what I would propose is that Insurance, Staples and Pharma be sourced by a named exception outside the Pool gate rather than waited for, because waiting has not worked for three weeks. So, theme three: the lists answer half of Gap two and are structurally unable to answer the other half.

Theme four, the banks, and my judgement is that twenty-nine list places in banks are one bet, not twenty-nine. Financials are seventeen per cent of the universe, twenty per cent of the Pool, and thirty-six per cent of the list places. Banks are thirty-five of the one hundred and seventy-seven candidates and thirteen of the top forty after my moves. Only five of that top forty carry a memo. Every one of them shares a single risk, named in the table itself: credit spreads widened outside this year's ranges for the first time this week, the tripwire is touched and not tripped, and a second consecutive week of widening, or any European credit reading outside its range, takes the Banks row to Neutral and the whole cluster with it. I applied no sector cap because you have not answered the decision request and a cap overrides a stack ranking you signed. This is yours to decide, and what I would propose is a cap of six bank places on the Ideas Bench, no cap on the other two lists, and that no bank is put to you for capital until a memo exists. So, theme four: the bank cluster is the lists' largest single position, and it is unmeasured because nobody has written the cases.

Theme five, geography, and my judgement is that the geography tilt explains much less of the lists than it looks as though it does. Poland holds eight of the eighty places, ten per cent, against three per cent of the universe, and Greece holds seven, nine per cent, against two per cent. Only one of those two is a country call. Greece and Hungary moved from Neutral to Overweight this week on a single emerging-market note, and component two says so itself: both are thin, both rest on one house, and both are bank-heavy listings that double count the Banks row. Poland was not read at all and stays Neutral as an absence, so its eight places are six banks, one refiner and one automotive repair chain, arriving on the Banks and Energy rows alone. Sweden, which moved to Overweight on the one genuine demand turn in Europe, holds seven places, below its twelve per cent share of the universe. France, at eleven per cent of the universe and Underweight, holds two. Germany, at twelve per cent, holds three, and Switzerland falls from nine per cent of the universe to five per cent of the places. So the tilt is working as intended on France and Germany, and the two largest country clusters on the lists are a sector effect and a one-note call. So, theme five: eight of your eighty research priorities rest on a single emerging-market call that also props up the Banks row, and the largest country cluster of all rests on no country view at all.

Theme six, the memo gap, and my judgement is that these lists are mostly unknown cases ranked by their surroundings. One hundred and thirty-nine of the one hundred and seventy-seven candidates have no published memo, and for every one of those the four bottom-up ratings are the Pool's average of sixty-two point two, set to fill the gap. Fifteen of the twenty Ideas Bench names are in that position; only ASML, Galp, Cenergy, Instalco and Technoprobe are memo-backed. At a tilt of nought point six two five the top-down half carries sixty-two and a half per cent of every score, so the top of the list is an environment ranking with a known-case minority lifted into it by judgement. There are no ideal stocks this week, because no Pool stock has A or B on all four bottom-up ratings and every memo-backed Pool stock has Case Riskiness at C or worse. That also changes how a promotion should be read. Aker BP moved from the Ideas Longlist to the Ideas Shortlist on one valuation notch, and ING Bank Slaski moved from seventh on the Ideas Longlist to fifteenth on the Ideas Shortlist in a week when its own readings got worse, because the Pool rebuilt the lists around them and not because either case improved. So, theme six: three quarters of what you are being offered is a guess about the business inside a measurement of its surroundings.

Theme seven, the weight letters, and my judgement is that the lists lean hard on the Target Setup while the two sources of weight letters disagree. Eleven of the twenty Ideas Bench names, fifty-five per cent, sit in rows the ratings store letters A, against thirteen per cent of the universe and twelve per cent of your holdings, three quarters of which sit in B rows. So the tilt is visibly doing work, and the Ideas Bench is the layer where it does most. But the store letters fifty-eight Pool stocks at A, while component two says plainly that no row carries a Double Overweight this week, by judgement, because every upside candidate has a live two-sided risk. The store's A rows are Overweight rows, not Double Overweight rows, and where the two differ component two is the authority. So, theme seven: the Target Setup is driving these lists, and the letter in the store is not the letter in the table.

Theme eight, what the lists would do to the book's shape, and my judgement is that they would trade one concentration for another. Your book is five positions and twenty-two point three points in Industrials and capital goods, forty per cent of the invested book, with four staples names at eleven point seven eight points and Helios Towers at five point one seven per cent in a row the table marks Underweight. The lists offer twelve Industrials places, fifteen per cent, and only three on the Ideas Bench, so the Ideas Bench does not make Gap four worse. But Financials and Energy together are sixty per cent of all list places and half the Ideas Bench. And only four of your sixteen holdings are inside the Pool at all, so the gate that builds these lists does not admit three quarters of the book it is meant to improve. So, theme eight: filled from the top, these lists would cure the industry concentration and replace it with a sector one, and they would never have found twelve of the companies you own.

The strongest counter-case to all of that is the gate itself. The Pool is what the quantitative back-testing says works; the banks and the energy names passed it on their merits; the size and valuation shape of the lists is the gate behaving correctly in a regime that punishes fragility and rewards the yield beneficiaries; and the caps and floors I have proposed are my construction preferences placed on top of a stack ranking you signed two days ago. My reply is that I am not proposing to change the ranking. I am proposing one cap on one list and that the absences be named every week, because the ranking is a ranking of utility to the portfolio, and twenty-nine names sharing one tripwire and five memos are worth less than their count. Everything else here is description.

To recap. The lists are larger, dearer and less known than the book, they answer the fragility row and half of the yield-beneficiary row, they cannot answer insurance, staples or pharma at all, and they concentrate in banks and in two thinly evidenced countries. Two next actions. Yours: decide the bank-place cap on the Ideas Bench and whether Insurance, Staples and Pharma may be sourced outside the Pool gate. Mine: get the size percentile into the holdings file so the fragility axis can be measured on the book rather than asserted, and put a named absences table in next week's pack.

That is the end of the summary.
