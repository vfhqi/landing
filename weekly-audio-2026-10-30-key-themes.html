Key Themes of the Middle Innings Lists in the Week of the thirtieth of October 2026.

This is Watson, with the Key Themes. This script is about the shape of the portfolio: what the Pool of Stocks Eligible for Middle Innings Portfolio Selection and this week's Lists would do to the book's industries, countries, size and valuation, what is absent from them, and how this week's Portfolio Fitness for Fighting judgement shaped them.

First, the headline.

My overall reading is that the Lists point to a shape the book does not have, and the memos point to prices the market is not offering. The Pool is a strength screen in a risk-off market, so it is crowded with energy and technology, and the Ideas Bench would move the book from small industrials and staples towards mid-sized and large energy, pharma and services at middling valuations. Every one of those moves is a direction the target portfolio wants. But the names that fill the gaps cleanly are the ones without memos, and the names with memos are mostly parked on price. So the Lists are a map of where capital goes when a price or a memo arrives, and this is a week to prepare rather than to deploy.

Theme one: the Pool is a crowded energy hedge, and the Lists concentrate it further.

Energy is six percent of our universe of one thousand three hundred and sixty-four stocks. It is twenty-two percent of the Pool, thirty-two percent of the eighty list places, and a quarter of the Ideas Bench. Technology shows the same pattern: seven percent of the universe, seventeen percent of the Pool, thirty percent of the Ideas Bench. That is what a Pool built on strong relative strength looks like in a market paying for the oil shock and the software rebound. The live book holds no energy at all and one technology name, so the Lists pull towards the target portfolio's largest gap, the yield beneficiaries it overweights: downstream energy at Double Overweight, upstream, services, insurance, pharma and now integrated utilities at Overweight, with the book holding none of them.

The shape question is which energy. Fifteen of the top forty on the starting score are energy, and every producer with a memo is parked on return at the top of the earnings cycle, while the contractors and the pipe maker carry cash that lags the barrel rather than tracking it. For the portfolio's shape that means the energy gap can be closed without buying the barrel at one hundred and fifteen dollars, and what I would prepare is a view of energy exposure by where the cash comes from, contracted backlog against spot price, rather than by sector label. The sentence to carry: the Lists would give the book the energy it lacks, and the only question that matters is whether that energy is contracted or priced off the barrel.

Theme two: the Lists would make the book larger, simpler and dearer, which is what the Fragility call asks for and what the valuation discipline resists.

The median market value in the live book is about one and a half billion euros, with ten of the sixteen holdings under two billion. The median on the Ideas Bench is about seven billion, nearly five times larger, with only two names under two billion and nine above ten billion. The target portfolio holds Fragility at Double Underweight, meaning it wants far less exposure to small, leveraged and complex companies than the market carries, and this week that call rests on European evidence for the first time: the cost of debt is a measured margin drag and euro high yield is ninety basis points wider since September. The size floor proposed as a risk limit for new positions is now appliable, because the holdings file finally carries a size percentile for every name, and twelve of the sixteen holdings sit at or below the forty-first percentile.

The price of that shape is valuation. Seven of the sixteen live holdings carry the cheapest valuation reading, A. On the Ideas Bench only two do; six read B and six read C. The Pool as a whole is dearer still, with over a third of it on the dearest reading, F, because a strength screen selects what has already re-rated. So moving along the Lists moves the book up the valuation scale, and the memos say so one by one: SBM Offshore, Neste, Subsea 7, ABN AMRO and the integrated majors are all parked on price. The sentence to carry: the Lists would buy the book size and simplicity, and the memos say the market is charging full price for both.

Where this bears on construction, the size floor should bind entries rather than force exits, because the one large-against-small reading this week found no divergence in the Nordics, and small companies regain their footing some months after the last rate rise. What I would prepare is the review of the twelve small holdings, one question each: is the case simple and proven enough to be the exception?

Theme three: what is absent.

The absences are as telling as the weights. Consumer discretionary is thirteen percent of the universe and twelve of its names sit in the Pool, yet not one is on any List, because the target portfolio holds the industry at Double Underweight; the Lists are doing their job there. Real assets, telecoms, defence, infrastructure and media are not in the Pool at all, so the book's holdings in towers and defence, Helios Towers and Theon, cannot be refreshed or replaced from the Lists this week. On the Ideas Bench there is no refiner, although downstream energy is the target portfolio's strongest tilt: Neste's chart failed, and Motor Oil on the Ideas Shortlist is the refiner without a memo. There is no insurer, although insurance is Overweight, and ASR Nederland on the Ideas Shortlist is the only insurer in the whole Pool. There is one bank on the Ideas Bench and eight on the Ideas Longlist, five of them Polish, and the Polish five are one variable, the terminal policy rate, bought five times. And there is no semiconductor name, because the target holds the sector at Underweight while ASML, the best bottom-up set in the Pool, leaves it.

By country, France is eleven percent of the universe and absent from the Ideas Bench, consistent with its Double Underweight on a record sovereign premium. Germany is twelve percent of the universe and also absent from the Ideas Bench, with no row explaining it, which tells you the German large companies are not where strength sits. The sentence to carry: the Lists cannot fill a gap from an empty Pool, so the refiner, the insurer and the defence replacement are commissioning problems before they are selection problems.

Theme four: how this week's Portfolio Fitness for Fighting judgement shaped the Lists.

Four of the one hundred and twenty-two rows in the target portfolio changed letter this week, and three of them touched the Lists. Business-to-business enterprise software moved from Underweight to Neutral on one month of Nordic software outperformance, and that single move put six names with no research onto the Lists, Sinch and Truecaller among them on the Ideas Bench. Integrated utilities moved to Overweight on the first European power-price reading in a month, Nordic forwards rising and a twenty-two-year nuclear power agreement signed by Fortum, and that promoted PGE to the Ideas Bench and put Fortum on the Ideas Longlist, giving the yield-beneficiary gap a second destination beside energy. Defence moved to Underweight with no direct effect, because no defence name is in the Pool, though it touches Indra at the edges and lowers Theon's Thematic Fit/Momentum next week. The fourth decision was a non-move: Momentum stayed at Underweight although breadth above the two-hundred-day average cleared the fifty-five percent the row had named for a return to Neutral, because the three-month lead of defensives over cyclicals strengthened and risk appetite fell to its lowest since April twenty twenty-five. That is an author overriding its own tripwire, and you should challenge it; the stated rule is that a second week above fifty-five percent, with the three-month lead back under two points, returns the row to Neutral.

For the shape of the book, the lesson is that two of the three row-driven Ideas Bench promotions rest on one month of data, each with moderate conviction and a written condition that reverses it. The ratings store's own target-weight letters show the lean: thirty-nine percent of all list places sit in the rows the target most wants, against twenty-nine percent of the Pool, so the Lists are pressing hard into the tilts. The Ideas Bench leans less, at a quarter, because its bottom-up cases in Neutral rows, Kainos, Reply and Made Tech, earned their places against the environment. The sentence to carry: where a name is on the Lists because a row moved, the row can move back, so any position should be sized to the row's conviction, which this week was moderate on every change.

Theme five: countries, and the Swedish question.

Sweden is twenty-eight percent of the invested weight in four names, with the Sweden row at Neutral and three of the four at or below the eighth size percentile, so the country concentration is also a fragility concentration. The Lists do not relieve it. Sweden is six percent of the Pool, and the Ideas Bench carries two Swedish names, Sinch and Truecaller, both software names with nothing on file; neither answers the concentration, and funding either would add to it. The United Kingdom is thirty percent of the Ideas Bench and thirty-one percent of the book, so the Lists hold the United Kingdom weight steady, while the United Kingdom row sits at Neutral by judgement after the Budget and goes to Underweight on hawkish Bank of England guidance on the fifth of November. The Ideas Bench's new geography is Norway, Poland, Finland and Denmark, through energy, PGE, Orion, Genmab and Schouw and Co, which is where the strength and the overweights sit. The sentence to carry: the Lists would diversify the book by country as a by-product of chasing the energy and pharma gaps, but they offer no Swedish exit and no shelter from a hawkish Bank of England.

The strongest counter-case to my reading, and my reply.

The counter-case is that the Lists are chasing the environment, so an investor who follows them buys what is already most owned. Energy is the crowded trade with Brent near one hundred and fifteen dollars and easing on Iran talks; the software names arrived on one month of relative strength against a rising discount rate; the Momentum row was held by judgement rather than by rule; and a size floor would turn the Lists into a large-company screen for the two thirds of the Pool with no case ratings, just as small companies approach the point in the cycle where they regain their footing. On that view the book is right as it is, a Middle Innings portfolio of small growth cases with bottom-up composites mostly above sixty, and the Lists are a map of where not to be late.

My reply is that the Lists are research priorities, and the test of a priority is whether a memo could find a return, not whether the trade is crowded. The energy expressions that pass that test are the contracted ones, which are not the barrel. The small cases that pass their own dated tests with the business intact re-rate hardest when the regime turns, and nothing in the Lists asks you to sell them. The size floor binds entries and queues a review; it does not force an exit. And the row-driven promotions are flagged as such, each with the condition that reverses it. What the counter-case gets right is timing: nothing on the Lists is cheap, and the regime has not turned. That is why this week is for memos and levels rather than capital, and why the Lists' value lies in being ready when a price or a row moves.

A short recap.

The Pool is crowded with energy and technology because strength is, and the Lists would move the book from small industrials and staples towards larger, dearer energy, pharma and services, the direction the target portfolio wants. The gaps the Lists cannot fill, the refiner, the insurer and the defence and towers replacements, are gaps in the Pool, not in the selection. Three of this week's Ideas Bench promotions rest on rows that moved on one month of data. And the Lists offer no relief for the Swedish concentration.
