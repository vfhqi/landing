Overview (including process/structure) of the Middle Innings Lists in the Week of the thirteenth of November 2026.

What this script covers.

Richard, this is Watson. This script tells you which stocks and groups on the Ideas Bench, the Ideas Shortlist and the Ideas Longlist deserve your curiosity this week, and why. The shape of the portfolio is in the second script, Key Themes.

The headline.

The top of the Ideas Bench became better researched this week. Six stocks now combine a memo with a grade of A or B on five or more of the Eight Master Ratings. The six are Sage, Demant, Orion, WPP, ABN AMRO and Sonova. Demant is the nearest of the six to a decision, and one third-quarter statement stands between you and that decision.

The second message is about where the fresh ideas are. The earliest turns are showing in software and services that the market marked down for artificial intelligence. Sage enters the Ideas Bench in first place on that story.

The third message is about memos. New memos were the strongest force on the Lists this week, and every new memo cut. Four memos graded the change thesis a fail and removed the stock: Shell, Maersk, Kuehne and Nagel, and Var Energi. Half the Ideas Bench still stands on no judgement of the business. That unresearched half is where the largest surprises can come from, in either direction.

Topic one: hearing care.

Hearing care is the group I would attack first, because two of the six best cases share one driver. Demant and Sonova both sell premium hearing aids in a market run by a handful of companies. Both are launching into a crowded window in the second half of this year. The research calls both companies structurally advantaged, and both are repairing their costs at the same time. A product cycle on top of self-help is the kind of change you look for.

Demant holds second place on the Ideas Bench. Demant's chart and Demant's valuation both improved this week, and analysts' estimates for Demant are already rising. Demant's memo is neutral on the shares but names its terms. The memo moves Demant forward at or below about three hundred and five Danish kroner. The memo also moves Demant forward sooner if the third-quarter statement passes two tests. The first test is a share of the United States Veterans Affairs channel of twenty-two percent or more. The second test is wholesale organic growth of eight percent or more, with the gross margin held.

Sonova sits in twentieth place, down six, because analysts' estimates for Sonova eased this week. Sonova's memo likes the business and parks the shares on price. The memo judges that Sonova's shares re-rated ahead of a launch window Sonova has not yet won. I read Demant's improvement as the market warming to that window for both companies.

What needs to go right is easy to state. Both companies cannot win the same window. A weak statement from Demant would mean the better chart only moved the entry price further away, and Sonova would move down with Demant.

Topic two: software and services priced for artificial intelligence.

The market is starting to sort the companies that artificial intelligence deflates from the companies that capture its gains. That sorting is my working hypothesis, and six Ideas Bench stocks let you test it. The useful sorting tool is Sell-side Momentum. Rising estimates on contracted revenue mark a turn. A cheap valuation on falling estimates marks a trap.

Sage is new and goes straight to first place. Sage rebuilt its product and strategy leadership this year, to scale business software powered by artificial intelligence. Sage's share price is moving from a base into an early uptrend, which is the first chart shape you asked us to favour. Sage also brings size. The weakness is that Sage's memo dates from July and names no expected return. So the A that Sage scores on TSR/Valuation rests only on the shares looking cheap, while estimates for Sage are not yet rising.

Sectra, in eighth place, has the best evidence in the group. Sectra leads its market in medical imaging software. Sectra holds a signed recurring backlog of three billion Swedish kronor, and almost no customer leaves. Sell-side Momentum for Sectra rose to A this week. The open question is how fast hospitals deploy that backlog, which Sectra only partly controls.

Made Tech, in twelfth place, is the small-company version of the same story. Made Tech builds digital services for the United Kingdom public sector and carries no debt. Revenue at Made Tech grew by about a quarter last year, on a contracted backlog. Made Tech's memo moves the case forward at low conviction, and the first dated test is the half-year results in February.

Reply is promoted to sixteenth place. Reply is a federation of specialist consultancies that charges fixed prices, and Reply's shares look cheap. My own reading is that automation gains stay with a firm that prices by outcome and not by the hour. The opposite reading is that the cheap price is the market counting work Reply is about to lose.

Sinch, in fourteenth place, is the one to be sceptical about. Sinch shows three positive chart signals at once. But earnings per share at Sinch are growing because the share count is shrinking. Sinch has retired fifteen percent of its capital since July last year, on organic gross profit growth of two percent. A buyback is not a change thesis.

WPP belongs in this topic, because artificial intelligence threatens the creative fees WPP earns. WPP slips from first place to fifth without anything changing at WPP itself. WPP and Made Tech have the only two memos on the Ideas Bench that lean bullish on the shares. WPP is rebuilding itself into a single operating company. The weakness is that Foundations' Robustness and Case Riskiness both read D for WPP. The third-quarter trading update is WPP's next test.

The cautionary tales sit just below the Ideas Bench. Kainos drops to the Ideas Shortlist, because a new memo lowered both of Kainos's fundamental grades in the week the Kainos chart rose. Dassault Systemes joins the Ideas Shortlist looking cheap, with estimates easing. Wolters Kluwer and SAP left the Lists altogether. In this group, estimates are the thing to track.

Topic three: the defensive businesses the portfolio lacks.

Large pharma and integrated utilities are good assets whose returns are set by someone else. Carry that judgement into all five Ideas Bench names in this group. In each case a partner, a patent date, a power price or a state decides how much of the quality reaches shareholders. The research question is the same each time: who keeps the return?

Orion rises to fourth place and is the cleanest of the five. Orion is a Nordic generics company turning into an oncology royalty business. Sell-side Momentum for Orion moved to A this week, which is the confirmation the case lacked. Orion's memo waits for a price of seventy-three euros or below, because Orion's economics are almost entirely rented from one partner.

Genmab, in seventh place, has first-class economics in antibody discovery, with royalties providing about four fifths of revenue. Genmab's chart strengthened this week while estimates fell, ahead of trial results that will come out clearly good or clearly bad. Nobody here has assessed that setup, so a memo on Genmab comes before anything else.

Merck, in eighteenth place, deserves a second look. Merck's August memo parked the shares partly because estimate momentum was in the weakest quarter of the market. Sell-side Momentum for Merck now reads A, and nobody has re-read the memo. Merck is also failing one of the entry tests for the Pool of Stocks Eligible for Middle Innings Portfolio Selection. Merck drops out of the Pool next week unless its readings recover.

VERBUND is new, in third place, and has no memo. VERBUND owns an exceptional hydropower fleet on long-life water concessions, and hydropower earned about three quarters of last year's operating profit. VERBUND is the highest-quality asset in this group. The hydropower earns whatever the power price gives, so a memo must say how exposed VERBUND's earnings are, and for how long.

PGE, the Polish utility in sixth place, is two companies in one. PGE owns a regulated electricity grid worth having, and a lignite coal fleet. The Polish state holds about sixty-one percent of the votes. So the state decides whether the grid's returns reach minority shareholders or pay for coal. A memo on PGE has to judge whether the grid grows faster than the coal business bleeds.

Endesa, on the Ideas Longlist, shows what the group looks like after the re-rating. Endesa's memo finds a central return of under two percent a year.

Topic four: banks, energy and shipping.

Banks, energy and shipping are good businesses at full prices, and this week's moves make the point. Twelve banks saw their charts improve together. That group move lifted two Polish banks, AIB Group and Bank of Cyprus from the Ideas Longlist to the Ideas Shortlist. Yet every bank memo and every energy memo on file says the price already pays for the case. The stories in this group are about a level being sustained, high interest rates or tight oil supply. The stories are not about a company changing.

ABN AMRO is promoted to eleventh place as the best bank case in the Pool. ABN AMRO is a Dutch deposit bank whose income reprices mechanically, so returning capital is the thing ABN AMRO has to deliver. ABN AMRO's memo calls the change compelling and the price too high, with an entry level of about thirty-two euros.

Banca Transilvania is promoted to nineteenth place, although the score alone would put Banca Transilvania fifth. Banca Transilvania is the only bank on the Lists that combines a cheap valuation with a strong chart. I ranked the Romanian bank far below its score because nothing about the business is on file, and because the chart moved with the whole group. The score may be right and I may be too cautious, so any research at all would settle the argument. Banca Transilvania also drops out of the Pool next week unless its readings recover.

Energy thinned out sharply. TotalEnergies fell from the Ideas Bench to the Ideas Shortlist, and no energy company remains on the Ideas Bench. The tanker, refiner and car-carrier names have the strongest charts on the Lists and the worst grade for valuation, on earnings the research reads as a peak. Repsol is the one name that could prove the memos wrong. Estimates and chart for Repsol both rose to A this week, against a bearish memo. If Repsol's estimates keep rising, the memos in this group are early and not right.

Topic five: flavours, fragrances and medical technology.

Two smaller groups offer cheap prices on good charts, and both need business judgement more than data.

Symrise is new in ninth place. Symrise combines a strong chart and a cheap valuation in an ingredients sector that is leading the market. Nothing about the Symrise business is on file. My own reading is that Symrise is one of the large flavour and fragrance houses, where steady volume growth meets margins recovering as input costs settle. A memo decides whether that reading holds.

Kerry, in tenth place, is the self-help case beside Symrise. Kerry plans to lift margins to twenty percent or a little more by the end of the decade, and Kerry is buying back shares. The research calls Kerry structurally second-tier, earning under eleven percent on capital. The question for Kerry is whether that return can rise.

On the Ideas Shortlist, Bonesupport and Tecan are the two medical technology names to investigate. Bonesupport owns its category, with gross margins above ninety percent, and the research reads this year's sales miss as administrative. Estimates and chart for Tecan both rose to A this week, which points toward the order recovery Tecan's memo is waiting for.

Topic six: three stocks that stand alone.

Greggs, in thirteenth place, is a mechanical recovery in cash flow with demand unresolved. Capital spending at Greggs peaked last year and falls by about a hundred million pounds this year, which turns into free cash flow almost automatically. Like-for-like sales decide the rest of the Greggs case.

Clariant, in fifteenth place, is a stock you marked at your highest level of interest. Clariant's memo calls the shares fair at just under eleven Swiss francs and compelling nearer nine and a third. Clariant's third-quarter results and the German court cases are the two things to track, and Clariant controls neither.

Attendo is promoted to seventeenth place. Attendo runs care homes, and Attendo's earnings rest on one mechanism: occupancy, the share of homes Attendo operates itself, and price indexation. Estimates for Attendo rose to A this week. Sales barely grew last year, so occupancy is the number to watch.

The recap.

Three things to keep. Hearing care is the best-evidenced group, and Demant is one statement away from a decision. Software and services marked down for artificial intelligence hold the freshest ideas, with Sage on top and estimates as the sorting tool. And half the Ideas Bench has no memo, in a week when memos removed four stocks.

An endnote on how the lists were made.

Three points help you weigh this week's reading.

First, this week's candidates came only from the Pool. Ten stocks on last Friday's lists sit outside the Pool and left for that reason alone. BP, SCHOTT Pharma, SBM Offshore and QT Group are among the ten. Their departure says nothing new about those businesses.

Second, the Pool holds one hundred and eighty stocks, out of a universe of nearly fourteen hundred. Seventeen of the hundred and eighty are failing an entry test and are due to leave. Hiscox and Swedish Orphan Biovitrum are among the seventeen, alongside Merck and Banca Transilvania. My evidence does not give last week's Pool size, so I cannot tell you whether the Pool grew or shrank.

Third, the gaps in the data. Seven in ten Pool stocks have no memo. The third-quarter statements from Demant, WPP and Clariant were due and are not on file. The recorded reasons for the stocks that left were cut short in my evidence. And no memo this week compared its case with the target portfolio's sector preferences, so the environment view and the company cases have not been checked against each other.

That is the end of the summary.
