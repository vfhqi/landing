G10 and European macroeconomic inputs (source #1)
Executive summary
  • The read: inflation is an energy story in every G10 economy, growth is holding up better than the energy shock implied, and the central banks chose credibility over patience. The Federal Reserve (Fed) hiked on 16 September, the Bank of Japan (BoJ) hiked on 18 September, and the Bank of England (BoE) held but opened the door to November
  • Strongest view: Goldman Sachs Marquee's Rikin Shah (18 September) calls the Fed move a "credibility hike", backward looking after five years above target, and argues hikes do little against the real drivers of this cycle: fiscal deficits, defence spending and artificial intelligence (AI) capital expenditure
  • Sharpest disagreement: the direction of the next inflation surprise. Goldman's new Euro area nearcast (Giovanni Pierdomenico, 19 September) has end-2026 headline just under 4% with upside risk to core goods; Goldman Marquee's Mark Wilson (18 September) says the surprise distribution now skews lower because the Iranian conflict has still not produced a supply break big enough to push oil to new highs
  • What changed: Goldman's US economists (David Mericle, 16 September) added an October Fed hike after a meeting more hawkish than they expected; Goldman's European team (Sven Jari Stehn, 18 September) added a December European Central Bank (ECB) hike to 2.75%
  • Watch: gas flows through the Strait of Hormuz, the Trump-Xi meeting on 24 September, and whether the nearcast keeps drifting up

G10 inflation: an energy story, and the houses split on the next surprise

Goldman's Pierdomenico (19 September) launched a daily bottom-up Euro area inflation model because core forecasts for 2027 keep drifting up despite benign spot data. The model's end-2026 headline reading has risen from below 2% in February to just under 4%; core settles a touch above 2.6% in December against 2% expected before the Middle East war, and core pressures "firmed up again since August". Stehn (18 September) gives the cause: natural gas prices have almost doubled since June and product spreads widened further. Rikin Shah (18 September) adds the counterweight: realised European headline prints have surprised 40 basis points to the downside cumulatively since the war began, helped by exported disinflation from China, yet the ECB is acting on fear of new-year price resets and wage rounds. In the UK, Goldman's Sharon Bell (18 September) notes inflation rose to 3.1% in August; the BoE (17 September) projects around 3.75% in the fourth quarter and slightly above 4% in early 2027. For the US, Mericle (16 September) has core personal consumption expenditures (PCE) inflation at 3.2% this year against the Fed's 3.4%, with a methodological revision later this month worth minus 0.2 points. Bank of America's Flow Show, relayed by Outset Global (18 September), says "diesel says 5% CPI". Switzerland is the exception: Goldman's Niklas Garnadt (16 September) finds the rise confined to oil products with core subdued.

The disagreement is about skew. Pierdomenico's model and Bank of America lean up; Mark Wilson leans down. For a European long-only book, the inflation path is the ECB path, and Shah says front-end European rates will trade with a high beta to energy until core peaks, which Goldman expects in the second quarter of 2027.

G10 economic health: resilient data, miserable sentiment

Stehn (18 September) says Euro area first-half growth ran at 1.2% annualised despite the energy shock, which he attributes to reduced energy sensitivity, German fiscal expansion and spillovers from the global AI cycle; he expects 0.9% this year rising to 1.3% in 2027. Goldman's Joseph Briggs (17 September) documents the paradox: consumer sentiment is very weak despite a healthy labour market, solid spending and growth, and he concludes sentiment now reflects a downbeat view of institutions rather than the economy, so it will stay a poor predictor. Daily Chartbook (16 September) relays the Atlanta Fed tracker at 5.1% for the US third quarter. The BoE (17 September) records UK growth of 0.4% in the second quarter, more resilient than expected, though Bell (18 September) flags weakening payrolls. Garnadt (16 September) raised his Swiss third-quarter forecast after a 1.5% second quarter driven by pharmaceuticals exports.

The sceptics are MacroStrategy Partners (18 September): James Ferguson argues government, energy and AI infrastructure are crowding out everything else and that the rest of the US economy shrank in the first quarter; Andrew Lees says the US cannot grow out of a savings deficit funded 80% from abroad. Bank of America says "GDP boom hopes are over". Shah offers a third reading: resilient growth means this is a demand shock as well as a supply shock. The matter for European earnings is whether Stehn's resilience or Ferguson's crowding out is right.

Trust in fiscal and monetary leadership: a credibility hike

Shah (18 September) reads the Fed's unanimous 25bp hike, the higher dot plot and Chair Warsh's repeated "removed a dose of accommodation" as a credibility decision, and expects the Fed can string hikes together without waiting for data. He says hikes cannot fix the long-end supply and demand imbalance: US real yields make new highs while breakevens stay in a range, and fiscal consolidation "seems farfetched". He calls the ECB "front footed" and notes its reaction function no longer distinguishes a supply shock from a demand shock; the BoE "toed the line" because anything less hawkish would have traded as a policy error. Bank of America agrees: no Fed has been this terse since 2007, credibility's return is long-term bullish but "require[s] higher bond risk premiums". Outset Global (18 September) notes the yen weakened 1.3% after the BoJ hike because markets were not convinced by its hawkishness, and the BoJ ran a rate check by 20 September. On fiscal trust, Marvin Barth (Seriously Marvin, 16 September) says the US is not going bankrupt: yields reflect strong growth and competition for savings, and sustainability turns on real rates against real growth. Lees disagrees. Goldman's Christian Mueller-Glissmann (16 September) flags widening French sovereign spreads as Europe's pressure point.

The discount rate: policy rates and bond yields

Goldman's David Mericle (16 September) reads the Fed meeting as more hawkish than expected: a 16 to 2 majority projecting another hike this year, the neutral rate estimate raised to 3.25%, and Chair Warsh describing "removed accommodation". Goldman now adds an October hike but holds its terminal rate at 3.25 to 3.5%. Goldman's Central Bank Policy Tracker (Jan Hatzius, 20 September) shows the pattern: 83% of developed-market central banks hiked in the last three months, Goldman raised its end-2026 forecasts for the US, UK, Euro area and Japan, yet its forecasts sit below market pricing in every developed market. That gap is Mark Wilson's argument: the fear is priced. Rikin Shah (18 September) disagrees on the long end: hikes calm credibility but the drift higher in US real yields from the supply and demand imbalance continues, and the market prices three more European Central Bank (ECB) hikes with the front end trading on gas prices. Christian Mueller-Glissmann (17 September) says yields have only returned to long-run averages, term premia remain low, and continued yield increases "could weigh more on equities" than on bonds. Daily Chartbook (16 September) relays Bespoke: equity returns with a ten-year yield above 5% are among the worst of any range. The Bank of England held at 3.75% by 6 to 3 (17 September).

For a European book, the discount rate is the ECB path and Bund yields, and Shah expects the front end to carry a hawkish premium until core inflation peaks in the second quarter of 2027.
