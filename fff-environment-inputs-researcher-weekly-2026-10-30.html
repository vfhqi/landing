Report 1
G10 and European macroeconomic inputs (source #1)
Executive summary
  • The read: inflation remains the problem, framed as an overshoot from tariffs, energy and artificial intelligence (AI) memory prices, against which central banks lean slowly
  • Strongest view: Goldman Sachs Economics (25 and 28 October): the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) held at 3.75 to 4.00%, hiking every other meeting with a final hike due in December; the market's four priced hikes are too hawkish
  • Sharpest disagreement: the United States (US) consumer. Conference Board confidence jumped to 91.7 (Goldman Sachs, 27 October) while the University of Michigan finished at 48.0, the second lowest reading on record (Pacific, 26 October)
  • Changed this week: the Fed hold was confirmed with two dissents for a hike; US manufacturing output prices hit a post-pandemic high (Goldman Sachs, 23 October); the French bond risk premium reached 150 basis points (Pacific, 26 October)
  • Watch: the December FOMC, Iran talks, the US midterms and the United Kingdom (UK) Budget

1. Inflation: a supply-shock overshoot the Federal Reserve is leaning against

Goldman Sachs Economics (29 October) treats core personal consumption expenditures (PCE) inflation at 3.12% as overstated, trusting the trimmed mean at 2.27% because the gap is one-off: tariffs, energy and AI-related price pressure. Its FOMC preview (25 October) expects the overshoot to be "waited out", with the risk of oil staying elevated on Iran.

Pacific (26 October) lists the prints that argue against waiting: US core consumer price index (CPI) 2.6%; UK CPI 3.5%, cementing a November Bank of England hike above 90%; Canadian inflation expectations at 4.0%.

For a European book this sets the discount rate and the margin squeeze: energy pass-through against tighter financial conditions.

StatusAbove target across the US, UK and Canada; China at 0.9% (Pacific)
TrendHeadline rising on energy, core sticky
Change in trendUpside surprises in US and UK CPI; US output prices at a post-pandemic high
SkewUpside near term (oil, January pricing); Goldman Sachs expects fading through 2027

2. Economic health: strong United States, softer euro area surveys, firm United Kingdom

Goldman Sachs Economics (29 October) reports US gross domestic product (GDP) growth of 3.4% annualised, but expects a slowdown to 1.8% in the fourth quarter as the fiscal boost fades.

Europe is softer: Goldman Sachs' GOAL team (26 October) notes euro area flash purchasing managers' indices (PMIs) missed while US PMIs held, and its European Economics team (25 October) sees the fiscal tailwind fading to neutral in 2027; the UK beat on every count (Pacific, 26 October).

4. Trust in fiscal and monetary leadership: France, the Fed, the United Kingdom Budget

Pacific (26 October) is sharpest: the French ten-year risk premium at 150 basis points is "a lot for a core European country"; the cause is politicians unable to speak truth, and with the European Central Bank (ECB) draining liquidity, "this is just the warmup". Goldman Sachs (25 October) puts France's 2027 deficit at 5.4% of GDP; DNB Carnegie (26 October) counters that Moody's affirmation removed a sovereign tail risk.

Goldman Sachs (28 October) called Fed Chair Warsh "balanced", noting financial conditions have tightened "but are not tight"; its UK Kickstart (23 October) expected a low-key Budget with modest tax rises.

1. The discount rate: policy rates and bond yields

Goldman Sachs Economics (28 October) reads the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) hold at 3.75 to 4.00% as hiking every other meeting, with a final hike in December; it thinks the market's four priced hikes are too many, expecting cuts later (25 October). Chair Warsh gave no forward guidance, keeping the ten-year above 5% "even as equity volatility capitulated" (Outset Global, 29 October). Pacific's writer (26 October) notes the five-year fell 15 basis points in a day during the AI sell-off, which Goldman Sachs' desk reads as a sign of slower AI capital spending rather than relief. The United Kingdom and Canada are both priced for hikes above 90% (Pacific).

2. Credit spreads and the cost of debt

Goldman Sachs Europe Kickstart (23 October) says higher borrowing costs are already hitting earnings: interest expense has risen from 1.25% to 1.85% of sales since 2022, a 60 basis point margin drag worth more than 5% of earnings; weaker balance sheets are exposed and Quality should benefit, though no systemic credit event is expected. GOAL (26 October) reports a second "multi-standard-deviation" sell-off in credit excess returns. Jefferies (28 October) says debt markets have "almost entirely shut off" for AI data-centre borrowers without independent revenue, though it sees financing as a 2028 problem.

StatusSpreads wider, yields materially higher since September

4. Risk appetite and the cracks underneath (Richard's diagnostic questions)

Are equities ignoring the discount rate? At the index level yes: the S&P 500 sat within 1% of its high after three gains (Outset Global, 29 October). Underneath, no: Goldman Sachs' desk (26 October) reports the Nasdaq down over 6% in two weeks, momentum down about 15%, with rotation to defensives and low leverage. Cracks named: defensives outperforming, yes; negative over high momentum, yes; large over small, only one Nordic read, no divergence (DNB Carnegie); cash yield over growth, mixed (Goldman Sachs: Quality benefits from debt costs; Outset Global saw growth beat value on 29 October).

Is this 2007 into 2008? No comparison was made: Goldman Sachs does not expect a systemic credit event, and the desk reads this as "closer to the end than the beginning".

Index risk and reward: the modal case has not deteriorated for GOAL, though the skew moved down: "good news judged with a higher bar, most news treated as bad news" (the desk). Jefferies (24 October, citing Vanda) argues the set-up is less fragile than July, though "the washout isn't complete".

1. The AI build-out: demand intact, payoff and financing in dispute

The bulls argue from the supply chain. Jefferies (28 October) relays SK Hynix's long-term agreements covering more than half of 2027 and 2028 capacity, and its Taiwan analyst Sherman Shang (25 October) models cloud capital spending of $1.43 trillion in 2027 and $1.85 trillion in 2028, noting that TSMC checks data-centre construction and power availability with its customers' customers before planning capacity.

The sceptics argue from the income statement. Pacific's writer (26 October) says AI "is everything, everywhere all at once, except in corporate earnings", cites Torsten Slok that outside technology little has changed, and calls Barclays' 5,000 job cuts "AI as a cover story". Danske Bank's C-J (25 October) observes that ABB fell 6% on 40% order growth because the strength was data centres: "market is not willing at all to pay up for beats related to DC and AI". Jefferies (29 October) concedes the Microsoft capex cut "fuels the concerns about the datacenter bottlenecks", and sees financing as the key long-term risk, though for 2028 deployments rather than 2026 or 2027.

For a European book, the theme sets both the leadership of semiconductors and capital goods and the earnings bar they must now clear.

Change in trendHyperscaler prints gave no incremental capex lift
SkewJefferies and Danske two-sided with an upside lean on Anthropic data; the desk and Pacific downside on returns
