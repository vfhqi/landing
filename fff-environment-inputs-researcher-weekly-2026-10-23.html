Executive summary
  • The read: the United States (US) economy is strong enough that the Federal Reserve (Fed) debates how many more rate rises, not whether; Europe's input story is fiscal trust in France
  • Strongest view: Goldman Sachs (21 Oct) says US inflation "is not broadening out", because one-off tariff, energy and artificial intelligence (AI) effects explain about 75% of the gap between core and trimmed-mean inflation

Inflation: pipeline pressure or calm trend

Goldman Sachs (US Daily, 21 Oct) puts September core personal consumption expenditures (PCE) inflation at 3.1% against a trimmed mean of 2.2%, and holds the trimmed mean is the better guide because its outliers are mostly one-off. Jefferies (17 Oct) relays Tom Simons: a 1.1% rise in import prices, plus freight carriers passing on price rises, is "significant inflation pressure in the pipeline". The Fed is split the same way (Goldman, Fed Chatterbox, 21 Oct): Hammack and Goolsbee call pressure broad-based; Williams and Jefferson, speaking before September's firmer inflation print, saw no spillovers.

Why it matters: if Simons is right, US rates keep lifting the discount rate on European equities.

TrendFirm, with energy feeding goods prices
Change in trendNone agreed; the dispute is over breadth
SkewUpward on Simons and Hammack; balanced on Goldman

Economic health and consumer sentiment

Goldman Sachs is constructive: third-quarter US growth tracking raised to 3.3% (16 Oct), a "resilient" consumer and a "stabilized" labour market at 4.2% unemployment (19 Oct), and a global activity indicator "notably above potential" (19 Oct). The sceptic is Chris Wood (relayed by Jefferies, 17 Oct): a "harder US backdrop" and a K-shaped economy where real wealth rose for the top decile and fell for the bottom fifth. Goldman (22 Oct) adds that AI job displacement would amplify any downturn.

Consumer sentiment and expectations, latest readings:

Why it matters: US strength lets the Fed keep tightening; falling sentiment is the crack Wood points to.

Trust in fiscal and monetary leadership

Goldman Sachs (16 Oct) says fiscal concern has pushed French spreads to decade highs with tentative spillover to Italy, and France's fiscal challenge looks "harder now than in 2011-15"; contagion risk is lower because growth, banks and European Central Bank tools are stronger. Its fragmentation index (19 Oct) shows risk "remains limited". The Fed Chatterbox (21 Oct) records near-unanimity that more tightening is needed; Chair Warsh says the world "has broadly conformed" to expectations, while Hammack warns of an "inflationary mindset".

Why it matters: French fiscal credibility is the European input most directly priced into the equity discount rate.

Artificial intelligence capital spending: demand intact, financing questioned

Goldman Sachs (16 Oct) says second derivatives of capital spending and earnings growth "likely slow from here" but the "mega-theme" is intact, and that it found no evidence of slowing compute demand or pricing. Jefferies (21 and 22 Oct) cites SpaceX's AI business targeting 13 gigawatts or more of compute by end-2027 at sub-one-year paybacks, Supermicro's preliminary sales well above consensus, and Meta raising its capital spending guide. Jefferies sales (Dan Stratemeier, 17 Oct) calls this the seventh "isolated" semiconductor sell-off of more than 9% since June, driven by positioning.

The sceptics: Wood questions how the boom is financed, citing widening Oracle and CoreWeave spreads; Goldman's asset allocators (19 Oct) note credit and hyperscaler default swaps widening; a Reuters report of lower Anthropic initial public offering valuations "add[ed] fuel" (Jefferies, 17 Oct). Daily Chartbook (17 Oct) relays FactSet's John Butters: the S&P 500 outside the seven megacaps is expected to outgrow them in fourth-quarter earnings for the first time since 2022.

Why it matters: European AI suppliers carry the theme's volatility without the spenders' returns Goldman expects to drive leadership.

Memory and the China threat

Jefferies' expert call (22 Oct, former Samsung executive Harald Lee) sees AI memory "structurally tight through 2029-30": high-bandwidth memory takes three to five times the wafers of standard memory and new fabs take 3.5 years. Bear case: consumer memory weak, Chinese makers gaining commodity share, flash memory balancing by late 2027. Jefferies' Korea analyst Jeff Kim (18 Oct) calls China's memory threat "overblown", putting Samsung five to seven years ahead.

Why it matters: memory pricing feeds the semiconductor equipment orders European suppliers depend on.
