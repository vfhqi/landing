APM Mini-Memo. Kerry. The thirtieth of October 2026.

Kerry is new this week at rank nine on the Ideas Bench, in the lists drawn from the Pool of Stocks Eligible for Middle Innings Portfolio Selection.

The one-line view. Kerry is the volume winner of food ingredients priced as a margin loser, and the case depends on margin and cash now following volume.

First, what the company does.

Kerry is an Irish company that designs and makes flavours, enzymes and nutrition ingredients for food, drink and restaurant companies. Since the dairy business was sold at the end of 2024, Kerry does nothing else. Revenue was about six point eight billion euros in 2025. The Americas are fifty-five percent of sales. Retail customers are sixty-eight percent and restaurants and foodservice thirty-two percent. Taste technologies are about four fifths of sales and biotechnology, meaning enzymes, fermentation and cultures, about one fifth. Kerry holds about nine percent of a seventy billion dollar specialty ingredients market, just behind Givaudan at ten percent. Kerry leads the world in smoke, grill and dairy tastes. The market value is about thirteen billion euros at a share price of ninety-eight euros.

Second, the thesis.

The story is volume accelerating on real demand. Volume grew four point one percent in the third quarter of 2026, up from three and a half percent in the second, and above the consensus of three point six. The Americas grew four point six percent. In the same quarter, Givaudan's North American taste sales fell seven percent. The engine is American recipe renovation. Synthetic dyes are coming out, and sodium and sugar are coming down. Renovation is now over half of Kerry's North American pipeline, more than double a few years earlier, as management said at the investor day in October. A recipe change sold as a package of ingredients earns far more than a single flavour. A former commercial director puts single flavours below twenty percent margin, and flavour combined with sugar reduction above forty-five percent.

Volume reaches earnings per share through a fixed cost base and a cost programme. Jefferies says each extra unit of volume earns about twice a company's average margin. The cost programme, called Accelerate two point zero, adds one hundred million euros of annual savings by 2028. Management raised its margin target for 2030 to twenty to twenty-one percent, from just under eighteen percent in 2025. Consensus has earnings growing about ten percent a year. The market has not paid for this yet. The shares trade at sixteen times earnings, in the cheapest quarter of their own ten-year range. The discount to Givaudan and Symrise, somewhere between a quarter and a third depending on the broker, sits at a five-year low.

Four drivers to track. Foodservice volume, which is a third of sales and grew five percent in the second quarter. Americas volume staying above two percent. The margin reaching eighteen to nineteen percent in 2026. And cash conversion recovering above eighty percent, from seventy-six percent in the first half of 2026.

Third, the Target Setup.

No memo exists for Kerry, so this read is my own. The Target Setup is Major CfC Clearing in MQ non-cyclical or better business on Q1 multiple, leading to reversion to normal. Kerry is a medium-quality business, with margin and return on capital below Givaudan and Symrise. Food is a non-cyclical end market. The multiple sits in the cheapest quarter of its own history. The cause for concern is that Kerry wins volume and loses margin. The first half of 2026 began to clear that concern, as the margin rose sixty basis points and management raised its profitability targets. The American recipe wave adds a demand-driven element to the re-rating.

Fourth, Case Riskiness and simplicity.

The case is simple to state but has two legs, and the second is unproven. Volume is already delivering. What needs to go right is that margin steps up to eighteen to nineteen percent in 2026 and cash conversion recovers above eighty percent, so that earnings compound at the high single digits management promises. There are no Bottom-up or Stock-driven ratings, and the weekly Lists judgement noted that Kerry's score rests on the environment alone.

The main risk is that the margin gain is a false friend. The research on file finds Kerry the weaker of its peer set on every quality measure. The sceptical reading is that margin rises by under-investing in research, at four point six percent of sales against seven to eight percent at Givaudan and Symrise, by destocking for cash, and by restructuring, rather than by a better product mix. Over half the product mix is judged at severe risk of becoming a commodity. The first half of 2026 showed the cash side of this doubt. Free cash flow of two hundred and sixty-two million euros missed the consensus of three hundred and seventy-one million. The trigger to watch is foodservice volume growth below two percent for two quarters in a row. Morgan Stanley's bear case of sixty-four euros is about a third below today's price. The reverse test is just as clear. Sustained volume outperformance in North American retail, with cash conversion recovering, would retire the doubt.

Fifth, business quality.

Kerry is medium quality with real moats. Co-created recipes create switching costs, because changing an ingredient supplier risks the customer's product consistency. Kerry's ingredients are only one to two percent of a customer's cost of goods. Kerry holds over twelve hundred patents and patent applications. About forty percent of the taste portfolio rests on proprietary fermentation. No customer is more than five percent of revenue. And Kerry won new business with nineteen of the top twenty foodservice chains in two years.

The gaps are margin, returns and pricing power. The margin of just under eighteen percent in 2025 compares with nearly twenty-four percent at Givaudan and nearly twenty-two percent at Symrise. Return on capital of about ten and a half percent compares with twenty to twenty-five percent at Givaudan. Contracts pass raw material costs through automatically. That protects gross margin in inflation, but forces price cuts in deflation, as happened in the first quarter of 2026. Staff turnover of twenty-nine percent, against ten to thirteen percent at the peers, is the under-priced weakness. For predictability, judge Kerry at constant currency. Currency translation is over ninety-five percent of earnings sensitivity, and reported earnings per share grew three percent in 2025 against seven and a half percent at constant currency.

Sixth, returns and valuation.

Kerry is cheap against peers and against its own history. The shares trade at sixteen times earnings, against Givaudan at twenty-nine times and Novonesis at twenty-five times. The weekly TSR Valuation rating of the thirtieth of October is B, a valuation reading only. The weekly routine's plausible return of forty-seven percent a year rests on the multiple moving to twenty-eight times, near the top of its history, and I do not lean on it.

My own read is a C to B return. Earnings growth of about ten percent a year alone is a D against your bar. A multiple moving to about twenty times over three years, still far below Givaudan, adds about seven points a year. That gives high teens to twenty percent with dividends, a C to B. An A needs the peer discount to close most of the way, which a large staple on a cheap reading can take years to do. The weekly Sell-side Momentum rating of the twenty-eighth of October is A, as estimates and target prices rose after the first half. The weekly Technical Momentum rating of the thirtieth of October is B, with the shares within two percent of their fifty-two week high.

Seventh, the value chain and thematics.

The pros are two big sector stories with Kerry at their centre. The sector note of the twenty-fifth of October judges Kerry the purest way to own American recipe changes. The same note judges Kerry the second purest way, after Symrise, to own the real return of volume in food ingredients. Brokers raised Kerry's 2027 organic growth forecast to four point three percent from three point eight on volume alone. Over forty countries now mandate front-of-pack nutrition labels. And synthetic Red Dye three must be out of American food by January 2027. The weekly Thematic Fit Momentum rating of the thirtieth of October is B, from the environment only.

The cons are timing and price pressure. The American notice rule is not yet binding. The comment period closes on the ninth of December, with no final date set. A Coca-Cola specialist says no major food company is going all in on replacing synthetic colours, which can cost up to ten times as much. Food companies are pressing suppliers on price. The October rise in oil prices reaches contract prices with a six to twelve month lag. The proposed Unilever and McCormick merger could bring flavour work in house. And the food ingredient suppliers rose forty-one percent over six months and then slowed to nine percent over three, so the easy part of the move is done.

The one-line view. Kerry is the volume winner of food ingredients priced as a margin loser, and the case depends on margin and cash now following volume.
